Localization has become standard for mid-to-large releases.
 Almost every game ships in English, new titles support twice as many languages as they did a decade ago, and cheaper translation platforms and machine-assisted workflows have put a broad language set within reach of even small teams.
That makes localization a product decision made in layers: 
which languages to ship, at what depth, and with or without audio.
 But the returns on each layer are far from even. A language that is hard to justify on PC can pay off on console, and the same language can deliver different results depending on the type of game it ships in.
Our Special Focus Topic, 
Game localization in 2026: where localization pays off
, is part of Newzoo’s 
Global Games Market Report 2026
. It draws on our 
Game Performance Monitor
, covering 5,581 main games tracked across 38 markets and 22 languages, plus more than 65,000 Steam titles.
The report measures how much each language lifts a game’s audience on PC and console. It shows which languages have become standard, where coverage still trails the size of the audience, and how platform, genre, and audio change the return.
Who is this report for?
This report is for anyone making or influencing localization decisions across PC and console, including developers and publishers, platform holders, language service providers, and investors assessing where the next growth will come from.
Key takeaways
1. Ten languages is the new benchmark for a mid-to-large release
English is standard, but the real question is what ships alongside it. The top 1,000 Steam titles, which account for close to 70% of player records, now ship a median of 10 languages. The rest of the field is catching up, with releases above 15,000 players more than doubling their average language count since 2015. The report shows how that benchmark has shifted over the past decade and which languages have joined the default set.
2. Localization pays off more on console than on PC, for the same translation
Text is translated once and serves both platforms, but across 22 languages the average lift on console is well above that on PC. The report explains why, and shows how lift relates to English proficiency in each market. It also highlights one country where the picture flips, from a low priority on PC to the top priority on console.
3. The biggest untapped returns are in underserved languages
French, German, and Spanish are now standard, shipping in roughly 70% of main games on PC. Five languages return a higher lift than any major European language, yet each ships in fewer than one game in five. The report names them and explains why the publishers who ship them face little competition.
4. The type of game moves lift more than the language does
Lift varies more within a single language than between languages. On console, the return on Japanese text can nearly double depending on genre. The report breaks down which genres gain most, which gain least, and why.
What you’ll find in the report
How many languages new games ship with, how that has changed since 2015, and which languages have overtaken the traditional European set.
How text lift varies by language on PC and console, and how it relates to English proficiency in each market.
Which languages remain underserved relative to the size of their audience, and where the opportunity is greatest.
How genre and game type change the return on localization.
When audio localization pays off, and what post-launch language additions can and cannot achieve.
Methodology:
 This report is based on Newzoo’s 
Game Performance Monitor
, covering 5,581 main games tracked across 38 markets and 22 languages, and 65,965 Steam titles for language counts, as of June 2026. Language lists come from each game’s Steam listing. Lift measures how much a market’s share of players rises when a game ships its language. A lift of 2x means the share doubles. It is a ratio of shares, not a player count. Player counts are title-level and not de-duplicated.

Why it matters
Newzoo's data puts a number on what the top of Steam already treats as routine: ten languages is the median for the titles where most players actually are, which leaves the small releases launching with one or two languages further outside the standard than they look.
Key Facts
Newzoo published the report "Game Localization in 2026: where localization pays off."
The report draws on language-support data for 5,581 titles in Newzoo's Game Performance Monitor database and 65,965 Steam titles.
Among Steam's top 1000 titles, where 69% of players are concentrated, the median number of supported languages is 10.
Average lift from supporting a language is 2.11x on consoles and 1.41x on PC across 22 languages.
Among new Steam releases in 2025, 82.7% include Chinese text, and Chinese players account for 28.2% of the world's PC players.
Among Steam's top 1000 titles, where 69% of players are concentrated, the median number of supported languages has reached 10. Newzoo measures a "lift" of 2.11x on consoles and 1.41x on PC when a language is supported.
The report tracks a decade of growth in language support. For new Steam releases with 15,000 or more cumulative PC players, the average number of supported languages more than doubled between 2015 and 2025; across roughly 5,500 major titles the increase is about 74%. Most new Steam releases are small and still launch with one or two languages, so the top tier carries the figure.
The language mix shifted too. Chinese passed German, French and Spanish in 2017, and Japanese followed in 2021. Among new Steam releases in 2025, 82.7% include Chinese text, which Newzoo still calls not enough given that Chinese players make up 28.2% of the world's PC players.
