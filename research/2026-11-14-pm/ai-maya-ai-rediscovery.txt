I'm going to tell this story as a diary, because the timeline is probably the best part.
18 October
I was traveling in Peru, Gemini-ing about the history of the place, when I learned about 
Hiram Bingham
, the American explorer who "discovered" 
Machu Picchu
 in 1911. "What luck!" I thought. He was actually looking for a different city, 
Vilcabamba
, the last refuge of the Incas, when a local farmer in the valley below guided him to the ruins. "Imagine if Bingham had our tools," I thought. "What would he do?"
Left:

Right:
 Machu Picchu, photographed by Bingham the same year. Public domain, Wikimedia Commons (

,

).
I brainstormed a bit with a few agents (Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and Grok), and they all said 
lidar
. From above, a ruin in the jungle is just a bunch of trees (like Machu Picchu before it was cleared), so satellite photos don't help much. Lidar, instead, fires laser pulses through the gaps in the canopy and maps the ground underneath.
[1]
So the agents went looking for lidar data. Peru isn't well covered, and hiring a company to do a survey meant permits, costs and bureaucracy, so I gave up on that. Then I thought: why not the 
Maya
? Their region is much better covered by public lidar.
That night, I asked the agents to analyze it and went to bed.
19 October
I woke up and checked the agents' reports. 'DAMN!' They had found lots of villages and other regular shapes that looked like structures or houses.
A few of the sites the agents flagged overnight. Keep an eye on the bottom-left one.
So how did they do it?
 They turned the lidar strips (about 1,200 km² of jungle) into images, and 48 agents went through them one by one like very patient archaeologists, looking for pyramids, platforms, and plazas while filtering out natural hills, sinkholes, and roads. There was no special model or classifier involved: the agents literally looked at the pictures, following a precise guide.
Me:
 That's awesome! Please check which of these villages haven't been discovered yet. Search both the literature and the archives.

Agent:
 One major village doesn't seem to have been registered.
This is the hilltop from the top of this post. It sits on the edge of a cliff, under the jungle, and it has straight edges and sharp corners. Nature doesn't really do straight lines.
Zooming in, there's a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid at least 8–12 m tall, a raised courtyard attached to it and a flat-bottomed hollow that might have been a water reservoir.

So what was it?
This part is speculation, from a long chat with the agents. It looks like a small Maya civic centre from the Classic period (roughly AD 250–900), basically the "downtown" of a local elite. The plaza is where people gathered, for ceremonies and maybe even a market. The pyramid was probably a temple, and the raised courtyard next to it might have been where the local ruler lived. The hollow could have been an 
aguada
, a rainwater reservoir: Maya towns needed them to get through the dry season. Or it's just a hole. The three platforms around were probably houses.

Cook et al. 2013
) and how lidar collected for environmental research can be reused for archaeology (
Golden et al. 2016
). In 2024, archaeologists found a whole Maya city in Mexico this way (
Auld-Thomas et al. 2024

What's next
The site is now in the hands of the people who should have it: Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History (INAH) and the archaeologists who mapped it first. If anything comes out of it, I'll post an update.
What I've learned
You can just do things. I'm not an archaeologist, so I didn't know this was supposed to be hard. I just picked an old problem, tried the most obvious thing and threw some compute at it. Being a naive outsider might be a superpower now.
And it turned out professional archaeologists had already mapped the same site. I guess that's a good sign: an outsider with agents ended up in a similar place to the experts.
And I don't think this is really about archaeology. So far, most agentic AI stories have been about coding, math and physics, but it's much more general than that, and I hope this small example makes it a bit more obvious. We are on the brink of a revolution in all the sciences, and the only bottlenecks left will be curiosity and imagination (and compute).
At this point, we should probably lidar the whole planet and let the agents discover the rest.

The Maya site that 48 AI agents "discovered" in NASA lidar data had already been mapped. Pietro Miotti, the Google researcher whose post "My AI Agents Found an Unmapped Maya Site" passed a million views on X and topped Hacker News, corrected it on November 11: the site appears in a 2025 paper by Francisco Estrada-Belli, Marcello A. 10.1016/j.jasrep.2025.105288
, with the description "others used lidar to estimate how many people lived in the Maya lowlands". The prior art wasn't buried in a Mexican archive. It was cited at the bottom of the announcement.

What the agents did
Miotti, whose X bio reads "Research @Google Paradigm of Intelligence" and who stresses he is not an archaeologist, describes a three-day diary from October 18 to 21. The agents found public lidar for the Maya region, turned about 1,200 km² of lidar strips into images, and 48 of them went through those images one by one "like very patient archaeologists", following a written guide to tell pyramids, platforms and plazas from hills, sinkholes and roads. "There was no special model or classifier involved: the agents literally looked at the pictures."
Overnight they flagged many candidate structures. Miotti then asked which of them "haven't been discovered yet", searching "both the literature and the archives", and the agents narrowed it to one hilltop site: a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid of at least 8 to 12 m, a raised courtyard and a possible reservoir. They checked the raw point cloud to make sure the shapes were on the ground and not in the canopy, drafted a report for INAH, Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History, and translated it into Spanish. INAH replied on October 21 that "no registered archaeological site was identified at the location given in your report."
That reply is what the "unmapped" claim rested on, and it says less than it seemed to. It is a statement about INAH's register, not about the scientific literature. Miotti hedged at the time ("it's not official yet"), but the headline and the thread said "unmapped", and that is how the story travelled: "finds unmapped Maya pyramid in 72h" on Hacker News, "Google researcher discovers unmapped Maya site" in tech press reposts.
The paper that already had it
"New regional-scale Classic Maya population density estimates and settlement distribution models through airborne lidar scanning" was published in the Journal of Archaeological Science: Reports, volume 66 (October 2025), article 105288, and is open access under CC BY-NC-ND. Its abstract says the authors aggregated public and commercial lidar, "including reprocessed environmental lidar data from Southern Campeche and Southern Quintana Roo, Mexico with enhanced structure visibility", and modelled settlement across a 95,000 km² area of the Central Maya Lowlands. Environmental lidar reused for archaeology is exactly the kind of data Miotti's post describes; it also links NASA's G-LiHT instrument and a 2016 paper on that reuse.
We could not check page 14 ourselves: ScienceDirect didn't serve the full text to our client, and Miotti kept the exact coordinates in a confidential annex. The identification rests on his own correction; the DOI had already been circulating in the Hacker News thread a couple of hours before he posted it on X.
What held up, and what didn't
The detection part arguably came out better than the original story implied. A structure that 48 general-purpose agents picked out of rendered lidar images turned out to be one that professional archaeologists had also mapped from lidar, which makes it a confirmed true positive rather than an unverified anomaly. Miotti says "the rest of the story still holds".
What failed was the step that turned a detection into a discovery: the novelty check. The agents were asked to confirm the site appeared nowhere in the literature, said so, and were wrong about a paper that was open access, on the exact region, and linked in the same blog post. Miotti hasn't published the agent transcripts, so we can't say whether they searched for the paper and misread it, or never looked past its abstract. Our guess, and it is only that, is the second: a site's location in a paper like this lives in a map figure, not in searchable text, and "does this paper's settlement map include a plaza at these coordinates?" is a much harder question than "does any paper mention a new site here?"
That is the useful lesson for anyone pointing agents at science. Spotting candidates at scale is now cheap. Proving that nobody spotted them first still means reading the figures of the papers you already cite.

Test detection and novelty independently
A terrain reviewer can ask whether a shape in an image is worth checking in the underlying point cloud. A records reviewer can check whether a public authority has a matching entry. A literature reviewer can compare the candidate with earlier surveys, including papers whose coverage is described in maps or supplementary material. Success at one stage does not automatically translate to success at the next.
A research team could measure the screening method with a blind set of tiles containing known structures, similar natural shapes, and empty terrain. Report the share of known structures flagged and the number of false leads per 100 tiles. Keep a separate count of how often the literature check correctly recognizes a previously mapped location. That would show whether the agents are useful for finding candidates and how much independent checking their novelty claims still need.
