Voyager 1 is celebrating a big milestone as it approaches its 50th birthday: reaching one light-day away from Earth.
NASA's long-running interstellar 
Voyager 1
 probe will mark one light-day of distance from Earth on Nov. 18, when the spacecraft is 49 years, two months and 11 days old. The agency 
recently stated
 the record distance, which will be reached at 5:16:07 a.m. EST (101607 GMT) is "a huge milestone by any measure, and a day worth celebrating."
During the light-day crossover, Voyager will be in contact with the Madrid station of the three-antenna 
Deep Space Network
 that monitors all deep-space missions. The spacecraft is currently making its way through the constellation Ophiuchus from the perspective of Earth, said Jeffrey Hayes, a NASA official whose work includes Voyager 1.
(Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech)
"This [milestone] essentially kicks off the year's worth of celebrations leading up to the 50th anniversary in August and September of next year," added Hayes, who is discipline scientist in the agency's astrophysics and heliophysics divisions, in a 
livestreamed discussion
 with NASA's Night Sky Network on Nov. 4.
Going the distance
Voyager 1 is the farthest spacecraft from Earth, after launching from Kennedy Space Center in Florida on Sept. 5, 1977 a few weeks after its twin, 
Voyager 2
.
NASA's Voyager 1 spacecraft launches atop a Titan/Centaur-6 rocket from Kennedy Space Center in Florida on Sept. 5, 1977. 
(Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/KSC)
A light-day, as the name implies, represents the distance that light travels in a single day. In other words, at one light-day, a radio-wave message to Voyager 1 takes 24 hours to get to the spacecraft — which is roughly 16 billion miles (26 billion kilometers) from Earth. Voyager's response then takes another day to get back to our planet.
Voyager 2 — which is at a 
distance
 of 13.5 billion miles (21.7 billion km) from our planet — won't hit the light-day milestone until 2035, NASA stated. While the agency has not offered recent estimates for how long the two spacecraft will last, in 2022 Suzanne Dodd, project manager for Voyager at JPL, said they may keep operating 
into the 2030s
.
The two spacecraft had initial funding to survey 
Jupiter
 and Saturn, although NASA did launch them at a time when the planets aligned in such a way to bring Voyager 2 past Uranus and Neptune if funding continued — which it did.
Voyager 1, meanwhile, took a turn at 
Saturn
 to look at the planet's rings, and the pre-biotic (pre-life chemistry) moon of its largest moon 
Titan
, executing maneuvers which brought it high above the solar system's plane.
An image of Saturn captured by Voyager on Nov. 16, 1980 four days after the probe's closest approach to the planet, taken from a distance of 3,300,000 miles (5,300,000 kilometers). 
(Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech)
Hayes said the original design team likely did not foresee a 50-year lifespan for the mission, even though the Voyagers had many redundancies in them. But NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), which manages the mission, is looking to continue to max out the scientific return.
NASA says the 
mission costs
 from launch to Neptune, spanning about 12 years from 1977 to 1989, was $865 million — with $30 million more budgeted for its extended mission. "It's a lot of money, right? And it's everyone's money … so we want to make sure that we do it right," Hayes said.
Flying into the black
Both nuclear-powered spacecraft are still healthy enough to be chatting with Earth and taking measurements from interstellar space, although dwindling power supplies have forced instrument shutoffs and other creative techniques to be getting the most out of the missions. One example was a huge power swap that took place on both spacecraft this year, 
called the "Big Bang"
 because of its impact on the mission.
"It's a balance, right? Because some instruments are more power hungry than others," Hayes said of the ongoing work on keeping the Voyagers going. "Those instruments that use the least amount of power, but give you good science still, they would obviously rise to the top in any sort of priority discussion. Whereas an instrument that is more power-hungry, but still has interesting science, that's on the cusp."
Voyager 1 officially entered interstellar space on Aug. 25, 2012. 
(Image credit: Mark Garlick/Science Photo Library/Getty Images)
While the two spacecraft are still healthy, Hayes said their age — and the extreme environment they are operating in — makes them "fragile," in his words. "We're way out there. We're a light-day out there, which is just just over 16 billion miles. So it's really really far. It's also really really cold, right? We're only a few degrees above absolute zero here."
The Voyagers continue to lose four watts of power per year (their initial power was at 470 watts apiece) and the challenge is not only the power, but also the propellant. Hydrazine, Hayes said, is prone to freezing. "And if it freezes, then it's game over," he said, paying tribute to the JPL engineers that continue to keep the spacecraft going.
Voyager 1's next destination is vaguely near a star 17 light-years from our planet, in the constellation of Ursa Minor (the Little Bear, or Little Dipper). But the spacecraft will certainly be out of nuclear power by then, as its flyby — at a distance of 1.7 light-years of the star, called Gliese 445 — will take place in 40,000 years.

At 5:16 a.m. EST on Wednesday, Nov. 18, Voyager 1 will become the first spacecraft to reach a distance of 1 light-day from Earth. A command radioed from home will need 24 hours to arrive, and a reply another 24 to come back. NASA is 
asking the public
 to celebrate by sending the spacecraft a signal on the evening of Tuesday, Nov. 17.
What are the Voyager missions?
NASA launched the twin Voyager spacecraft in 1977 to tour the giant planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter and Saturn, while Voyager 2 went on to Uranus and Neptune and is still the only spacecraft to visit either one. Both then kept going. They’re now the only probes ever to study interstellar space directly, the region beyond the Sun’s magnetic field.
Each spacecraft runs on a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, the same kind of power source that drives NASA’s Curiosity and Perseverance rovers on Mars. Plutonium-238, the fuel source, gives off heat as it naturally decays, and the generators turn that heat into electricity. But because the output fades by about 4 watts every year, engineers have spent years shutting off components and instruments to stretch the spacecraft’s lives. This year, a risky maneuver nicknamed the “
Big Bang
” turned off several devices in one coordinated move to buy the spacecraft and their remaining instruments several more years of operation. Today, Voyager 1 only runs two of its original 10 instruments, and Voyager 2 runs three.
Where are Voyager 1 and 2?
Voyager 1 is closing in on 1 light-day from Earth (about 16.1 billion miles [25.9 billion kilometers]) — farther than any spacecraft has ever gone. Its twin is about 2 billion miles (3.2 billion km) closer to Earth, near enough that a signal takes about 20 hours to reach Voyager 2. 
Voyager 1 lifted off Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after Voyager 2 but on a shorter trajectory as it was traveling to Jupiter. After visiting Saturn in 1980, it drifted away from the solar system’s planets for good. A decade later, it captured the famous photograph of Earth known as the Pale Blue Dot. In August 2012, it became the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space, and Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Voyager 2 won’t reach the light-day mark until November 2035. You can see both missions’ exact locations on 
NASA’s mission status page
.
What is a light-day?
A light-day is a distance measuring how far light travels in 24 hours. At about 186,000 miles per second (299,792 km per second), that adds up to roughly 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km). For scale, the Moon is just 1.3 light-seconds away, the Sun about 8.3 light-minutes, and Neptune a bit over 4 light-hours. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun, lies more than 1,500 light-days away.
At a speed of nearly 38,000 mph (61,000 km/h), Voyager 1 is anything but slow. Still, it needed more than 49 years to cover the distance light crosses in a single day. 
Send a signal
Astronomy
 usually asks readers to dim their lights. But tomorrow night is an exception: To celebrate Voyager 1’s accomplishment, NASA invites everyone to flash Voyager’s call sign, V-G-R, skyward in Morse code on Tuesday evening. Use NASA’s 
automated flasher
 on your smartphone or tablet. Or grab a plain flashlight and do it manually: V: dot-dot-dot-dash; G: dash-dash-dot; R: dot-dash-dot. Dots are quick blinks of the light, and dashes are long flashes of about a second. Pause between letters.

Nov. 18, 2026: The Voyager 1 spacecraft, after traveling for nearly 50 years, crosses a milestone reached by no other human-made object — one light-day away from Earth. That is the distance light can travel in 24 hours.
Launched in 1977, NASA's Voyager 1 sets a new record every day for the farthest human-made object in the universe. On Nov. 18, 2026, Voyager 1 passed a particularly significant milestone when it became the first spacecraft to reach one light-day away from Earth.
A light-day is a measurement of distance, not time, and refers to how far light can travel in 24 hours. Light moves at an incredible 186,000 miles (299,000 kilometers) per second, or 16.09 billion miles (25.89 billion kilometers) per day. Voyager 1, traveling at about 38,000 mph (61,000 kph), has taken nearly 50 years to reach this distance.
Becoming the only human-made object to reach one light-day from Earth is the latest on a long list of remarkable achievements by the Voyager mission, managed by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, a division of Caltech. The twin Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 probes made historic discoveries at Jupiter and Saturn; and Voyager 2 was the first and, so far, only spacecraft to visit Uranus and Neptune. The first and only spacecraft to enter interstellar space, both probes are now sending back data from this region, which is outside the bubble of particles and magnetic fields created by the Sun. They each carry a golden record containing sounds and images from our planet, acting as ambassadors for humanity as they journey into the unknown.
