This 
paper
 announces the results of a large study to evaluate the ability of AI agents to solve unpublished research-level problems in theoretical physics. If you are at all interested in physics, please take a look.
The abstract should be self explanatory. I’ve been thinking about these models quite closely for a while. But, frankly, I wasn’t expecting this day to come as soon as it has. After we found these results, I couldn’t sleep properly for a couple of nights.
Let me add some back story since the timeline is revealing.
About 6 months ago, just for fun, I prompted a frontier agent with an unpublished problem whose answer I had worked out. The agent got the answer wrong but it was impressive that it managed to make any progress at all, and could solve the problem after a few hints.
Praneeth suggested it might be fun to collect a suite of such problems along the lines of the mathematicians’ First Proof assessment. Soon we had a small core group and started requesting experts across the world to contribute problems. Problem collection was a slow process, since we were making a significant ask of contributors. We wanted unpublished problems, with clean verifiable answers. So, our contributors had to either sacrifice potential publication elsewhere, or else undertake a small research project just for us.
Even as problems started coming in, the agents started improving as newer models were released. We kept urging people to push up the difficulty of their problems. But, there is a ceiling to this difficulty since even world experts have only so-many problems lying about whose answers they know and haven’t published.
I don’t know of any individual who can solve any significant fraction of our final collection of problems without AI assistance. For instance, I can solve the problems I contributed myself and understand a few others. Most problems don’t even make sense to me.
Our initial evaluation suggested that GPT-6-Astra’s score was in the late eighties followed closely by Opus 5.5
But, as we started to investigate the mistakes that the models might have made, the models, especially Astra, insisted that they were right and that it was the expert-supplied answers that were erroneous. When we went back to the experts it turned out, in multiple instances, that that the experts found some subtle errors buried deep inside their calculations. As they revised their answers, Astra’s score started to inch up and it finally ended up with 98% (49/50).
The one problem it doesn’t solve is not because it makes a “mistake”. This is a trick-prompt, and requires the model to make an inspired guess, and then bend the rules a little. It can’t do that yet.
We comment a little on the broader implications in our conclusions. Clearly, these agent are enormously powerful tools that will accelerate discovery in the short run. They also raise serious structural concerns about access, equity and pedagogy.

Why 98% does not prove autonomous physics research
Solving a specified problem is different from selecting a problem
The benchmark supplies the question, the relevant physical context and a checkable target. Real research also requires deciding which question is worth pursuing, identifying a tractable path, recognizing when an assumption is physically wrong and judging whether a result is important. TheoPhysEval does not measure those choices.
The result is not evidence of a new theory or major discovery
The tasks exclude major open questions and emphasize known methods applied to altered inputs. A system can be excellent at deriving a requested quantity without inventing a useful framework, finding an unexplained phenomenon or deciding that an unexpected result deserves experimental follow-up.

How reliable is the 49-of-50 grading?
One task defeated every model
The report says one problem stumped all evaluated systems. That is a reminder that the 98% figure is an aggregate over a small, selected set; it does not establish a uniform success rate across subfields or difficulty levels.
Reference answers were revised after expert review
Expert follow-up found subtle errors in several reference answers. After those corrections, Astra’s responses were judged correct on the affected cases. The revisions improve the final assessment, but they also show why benchmark quality and independent checking are part of the result—not administrative details to ignore.
What a robust replication should publish
The exact 50 prompts, assumptions and permitted tools.
Per-problem results rather than only the aggregate percentage.

What readers should conclude
The strongest defensible conclusion is narrow but significant: in a realistic software-and-literature workflow, GPT-6-Astra can produce correct answers to a broad sample of difficult, concrete theoretical-physics calculations at a reported average cost below $3 each. That makes it a potentially powerful assistant for derivations, numerical checks and literature-grounded problem solving.
The evidence stops short of showing autonomous science. TheoPhysEval does not test research taste, question selection, experimental design, theory invention or responsibility for deciding whether an answer is physically meaningful. Anyone using Astra for real work should still reproduce the mathematics, inspect units and assumptions, and have a domain expert review consequential conclusions.

Abstract
We present a suite of 50 unpublished research-level theoretical physics problems with verifiable answers. Without AI assistance, a typical problem in our collection could be solved by a world expert in about a day or less but would take considerably longer for experts in adjacent specialties. We test the ability of off-the-shelf AI agents to tackle these problems autonomously, without human hints. When given access to tools available in a realistic setting, including the ability to search the literature and write scripts for numerical and symbolic work, the best-performing agent, GPT-6-Astra harnessed in Codex, obtains the final answers with a near-perfect accuracy of 98% at an average cost under USD 3 per problem. On average, agents show only small changes in accuracy when denied web search and access to background research papers, suggesting significant memorization of the literature. These results suggest that state-of-the-art agents can accurately perform concrete calculations required across theoretical physics research. We comment on the broader implications of these findings and discuss possible explanations for the current lack of agent-led breakthroughs in theoretical physics despite their outstanding problem-solving abilities.

The result of each problem is, strictly speaking, novel, and sometimes even publishable, but not pathbreaking.
All our problems have verifiable solutions, either in the form of a symbolic expression or a numerical answer that cannot be guessed. It is nontrivial to construct problems that are relevant to research, are not significant in their own right, and have a solution that is verifiable, cannot be guessed, and is not publicly available. We asked experts to come up with the hardest possible problems in their respective areas. However, we did not reject problems merely because they were soluble by AI agents. The average change in accuracy for most agents between the two tracks was quite small. Our results indicate that some frontier models may have already surpassed the best human researchers in their ability to perform concrete calculations, even in specialized topics. This is a significant milestone, with implications for education and research in theoretical physics.

II 
Problems 
The problems in our collection were obtained by soliciting world experts from different areas of theoretical physics.
We asked experts to contribute problems that met the following three criteria.
1.
Problems related to current physics research;
2.
A known solution that was simple to verify but difficult to guess;
3.
A solution that could not be looked up in the published literature or elsewhere.
We also requested that contributors not publish their solutions in the
near future, to avoid contamination of the evaluation.
These criteria are quite restrictive. In particular, they exclude problems from ongoing research that the experts intended to publish in the near future.
One pathway for contributors to satisfy these criteria was to give us a problem from their research notes that they had worked out but not taken the trouble to publish.
A more common alternative was for contributors to adapt a problem solved in one of their recent papers. By changing some parameters in a physics calculation, it is often possible to obtain a new problem with an answer that cannot be guessed from the old answer. Problem contributors could solve such a problem by modifying their existing code or calculations. But another expert human, or an AI agent, would need to locate and understand the original solution; reconstruct the calculation in detail; and then adapt it to the modified problem.
Such an exercise is often assigned to graduate students who are starting research, since it helps them survey the literature and master the relevant techniques. This method is also sometimes used by researchers to write “incremental papers.”
Therefore, none of the problems in our dataset constitute breakthrough open problems. Solving them does not require great originality or the ability to construct new physical theories. However, it does require familiarity with current research, and the ability to understand and accurately adapt cutting-edge calculational techniques.

Given the level of specialization in modern physics, it might often be difficult for a single expert to attempt all problems in a given thematic class.
Our problems do not provide an exhaustive survey of all areas of theoretical physics. The distribution of problems is partly influenced by network effects, since the coordinators and moderators invited experts they knew.
