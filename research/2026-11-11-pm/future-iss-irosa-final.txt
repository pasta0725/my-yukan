Spacewalkers Install Final Roll-Out Solar Array
A roll-out solar array unfurls after spacewalkers Joshua Kutryk and Anil Menon installed it on the International Space Station’s starboard truss structure completing the upgrade of the orbital outpost’s power generation system. 
NASA+
NASA astronaut Anil Menon and CSA (Canadian Space Agency) astronaut Joshua Kutryk concluded their spacewalk outside the International Space Station at 3:35 p.m. EST. 
During the six-hour, 59-minute spacewalk, Menon and Kutryk installed and deployed the eighth and final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array (IROSA) on the 3B channel of the space station, completing the power augmentation upgrades. Work to augment the station’s power generation began in February 2021. Since then, astronauts have completed 18 spacewalks to prepare power channel equipment and install the eight arrays. Together, the roll-out arrays increase the station’s power generation capacity from 160 kilowatts to approximately 215 kilowatts.
U.S. spacewalk 101 was Menon’s fifth and Kutryk’s second. It also was the 286th spacewalk in support of space station assembly, maintenance, and upgrades.

Just as NASA is preparing to demonstrate "
Max Power
" on the ground, two astronauts on a spacewalk have provided the International Space Station (ISS) with the same.
Expedition 75 flight engineers Anil Menon of NASA and Joshua Kutryk of the 
Canadian Space Agency
 installed and deployed the eighth and final ISS Roll-Out Solar Array (iROSA) on Thursday (Nov. 5), augmenting the space station's legacy solar array wings to produce as much as 30% more electricity.
"We got it done!" said Menon at the end of the 6-hour-and-59-minute 
spacewalk
. The EVA (extravehicular activity) began at 8:36 a.m. EST and ended at 3:35 p.m. EST (1336 to 2035 GMT).

Repeating many of the same tasks as they did on an 
Oct. 29 spacewalk
 that added the penultimate iROSA, Menon and Kutryk worked this time on the starboard side of the station's backbone truss on the 3B power channel. The two retrieved the 750-pound (340 kilograms) iROSA assembly from a temporary stowage platform, carried it to the worksite by way of the 
Canadarm2
 robotic arm and mounted it to its previously assembled bracket kit.
Menon then attached the cables to tie the iROSA into the station's systems but ran into a problem.
"We are seeing half of the expected shunt current that we want," radioed Nichole Stilwell, a NASA astronaut serving as the spacewalk officer in Mission Control. "In this current config, we are not necessarily going to deploy."
To address the issue, the connectors needed to be inspected. Out of safety concerns — given that the arrays generate electricity whenever lit by 
the sun
 — the astronauts needed to wait for the space station to enter the shadow of 
Earth
 to see what could be done. In the interim, they spent the time cleaning up their worksite and returning the Canadarm2 to its park site as they would have done after the deployment.
Fortunately, they were able to clear the issue.
"We are seeing good shunt currents on the ground, so we are go for deploy," said Stilwell.
Canadian Space Agency astronaut Joshua Kutryk (at center) works on mounting the eighth and final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array (iROSA) during a spacewalk on Nov. 5, 2026. 
(Image credit: NASA)
After releasing two bolts, Kutryk and Menon watched as the array started to unfurl to its full 63-foot (19 meters) length. No motor was needed; the iROSA, as designed, used only the potential energy stored by its rolled-up carbon composite booms to extend itself. The entire process took about 10 minutes.
"Today marks the completion of not just one solar array but, over 15 years after its development, eight arrays," said Menon at the close of the spacewalk. "These solar arrays are incredible. Half the size of the old ones and bringing 40% more power."
NASA graphic showing the location and power channel for all eight now-deployed International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Arrays (iROSAs). 
(Image credit: NASA)
Each of the eight iROSAs and their mounting assemblies were delivered to the space station by 
SpaceX
 Dragon cargo spacecraft. Beginning in June 2021, four teams of two astronauts did as was done on Thursday to deploy each array. In addition to Kutryk and Menon's work on the 2A and 3B power channels, the spacewalkers included Shane Kimbrough of NASA and Thomas Pesquet of the 
European Space Agency
 for the 2B and 4B channels; Josh Cassada and Frank Rubio, both with NASA, on 3A and 4A in December 2022; and NASA's Steve Bowen and Woody Hoburg on 1A and 1B in June 2023.
The need for the iROSA augmentations was discovered by flight controllers when the space station's eight original arrays began to show degraded power output as they exceeded their 15-year design life. The iROSAs provide the additional electricity needed to support ISS operations through its retirement and 
controlled deorbit
, which are currently scheduled to occur in late 2030.
"Congratulations to you all on your stellar work to finish the last of the roll-out solar arrays and the upgrades to the ISS power system that will take it to the end of its journey," said Stilwell, addressing Menon and Kutryk. "It is fitting that we just surpassed 26 years of constant human presence on the 
International Space Station
, and you all showed us what the best of humanity looks like executing one of the most difficult and dangerous [things] humans do and making it look easy. Congratulations!"
Menon has now logged 33 hours and 30 minutes on four spacewalks, while Kutryk has tailed 14 hours and 10 minutes on two EVAs. Thursday's spacewalk was the 101st U.S. EVA at the International Space Station without a 
space shuttle
 being present and the 286th overall in support of ISS assembly, maintenance and upgrades. It was the ninth spacewalk of 2026 and the sixth conducted by the station's 75th resident crew.

Spacewalkers complete five-year effort to upgrade space station power supply
Houston Harwood
November 5, 2026
10:28 p.m.
Anil Menon, riding the space station robot arm, moves the folded, rolled-up IROSA assembly to its installation point at the Starboard end of the space station truss. Image: NASA.
Spacewalking astronauts Joshua Kutryk and Anil Menon unfurled the last of eight new solar arrays outside the International Space Station Thursday, working through an unexpected power snag to complete a five-year upgrade designed to keep the orbiting laboratory running through its retirement in 2030.
The final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array, or IROSA, unrolled at the far starboard end of the station’s main truss at about 3:05 p.m. EST (2005 UTC), roughly six and a half hours into the spacewalk. That came only after flight controllers in Houston spotted lower-than-expected electrical current from the freshly connected array and held the deployment through another trip around the planet so Kutryk could inspect a power connector in the darkness of orbital night.
“Josh, deployment is complete,” Mission Control radioed Kutryk after the previously rolled-up array reached its full length of 63 feet.
According to NASA, the eight IROSAs will raise the station’s available power by up to 30 percent, supporting the outpost through its final years, including what the agency says will be its “safe and controlled deorbit.”
Kutryk, 44, and Menon, 50, switched their spacesuits to battery power at 8:36 a.m. EST (1336 UTC) to begin the 286th spacewalk devoted to station assembly, maintenance and upgrades, and began repressurizing the Quest airlock at 3:35 p.m. EST (2035 UTC), formally ending a 6-hour, 59-minute spacewalk.
It was the ninth spacewalk of 2026 and the sixth by members of the station’s Expedition 75 crew, NASA commentator Joseph Zakrzewski said. It was Menon’s fifth spacewalk, bringing his total time outside to 33 hours and 30 minutes, and the second for Kutryk, a Canadian Space Agency astronaut who logged 14 hours and 10 minutes across his 
pair of IROSA installation spacewalks.
Kutryk led Thursday’s outing with the call sign ‘EV1’, wearing the red-striped suit, a week after he and Menon 
installed the seventh IROSA during a seven-hour, 11-minute spacewalk Oct. 29.
The final array was arguably the hardest to reach. Its mounting point on the station’s outermost starboard truss segment sits beyond the reach of Canadarm2, the station’s Canadian-built robotic arm, so the spacewalkers had to close the last stretch by hand.
After Kutryk released the last of the bolts holding the folded array, about 10 feet long and roughly 750 pounds, to the carrier it launched on, Menon locked his boots into a foot restraint at the end of the arm and took it in hand. NASA astronauts Luke Delaney and Jessica Watkins, working with flight controllers, then flew Menon and the array down the truss in a careful series of yaw and roll maneuvers.
At about 10:23 a.m. EST (1523 UTC), Menon handed the array to Kutryk, who was waiting in a foot restraint of his own near the installation site. Menon then climbed off the arm, set up another foot restraint and, about 40 minutes later, took the array back from Kutryk to guide it into place.
“Another handoff complete,” Zakrzewski said as Menon took control of the array during an orbital night pass. Flight controllers said the pair was about 15 minutes ahead of the timeline.
Menon and Kutryk work to install the seventh and final International Space Station Roll-Out Solar Array during a spacewalk on Nov. 5, 2026. Image: NASA.
By this point, Menon was on familiar ground. He and NASA astronaut Jessica Meir bolted the triangular mounting bracket for this array, known as a modification kit, onto the old solar wing’s base during an Aug. 6 spacewalk.
After some wiggling and a slight yaw adjustment to line up an alignment guide, the spacewalkers seated the IROSA on the bracket and, about 15 minutes later, swung it open to its full 20-foot width.
The power cables had to wait for darkness. Because the station’s existing solar wing and its cabling are live whenever they’re in sunlight, the crew can only connect the new array’s power cables during the roughly 45 minutes of each 90-minute orbit when the station passes through Earth’s shadow.
The pair finished mating the cables at about 12:36 p.m. EST (1736 UTC), well within the darkness window, but then came the snag. About four and a half hours into the spacewalk, the ground relayed that the new array was delivering only about half the current engineers expected.
Because the cables could only be safely handled again in darkness, flight controllers reshuffled the plan. Kutryk and Menon moved up their cleanup tasks, stowing the foot restraints and the robotic arm hardware, while waiting for the next orbital night. Mission Control told them the extra time would stretch the spacewalk to about seven hours, which their spacesuits could support.
The spacewalkers had to troubleshoot a problem with the electrical connectors that feed electricity from the IROSA arrays to the station’s power system. Image: NASA.
When the station slipped back into Earth’s shadow, Kutryk returned to the worksite, disconnected one of the cable connections, inspected both ends and plugged it back in, confirming it was firmly seated.
“Certainly looks like a good solid mate now,” he said.
After the station returned to daylight, flight controllers reported “good currents” and cleared the crew to deploy the array. NASA did not immediately say what caused the initially low readings.
Kutryk then released the final restraint bolts, and the solar panels began unfurling on their own, driven by strain energy stored in carbon-fiber composite booms. The process took about 10 minutes, Zakrzewski said.
Each IROSA generates about 8 kilowatts of power, Zakrzewski said, compared with 19 kilowatts for each of the station’s original wings when they were new.
Space shuttles delivered those original solar arrays between 2000 and 2009. They were designed to last 15 years, and their output has gradually declined. Rather than replace them, NASA opted to mount the smaller, more efficient roll-out arrays in front of the old wings, partly shading them while the wings’ exposed portions keep producing power.
Work to install the eight IROSA solar arrays spanned five years. Image: NASA.
The first IROSA unrolled in June 2021. Six arrays were in place by June 2023, and NASA ordered the final pair later that summer. Those two arrays launched to the station Oct. 13 atop a Falcon 9 rocket.
Hehn led Thursday’s spacewalk from Mission Control in Houston, alongside Greg Whitney, who led the Oct. 29 outing. NASA astronaut Nichole Stilwell served as spacewalk communicator.
With the power upgrade finished, NASA’s next planned U.S. spacewalks are targeted for early January to upgrade the Alpha Magnetic Spectrometer, a cosmic-ray detector mounted on the station’s truss, Gaurang Patel, operations integration manager for NASA’s Low Earth Orbit Program, said at an Oct. 20 briefing.
After reentering the ISS’ Quest airlock, Kutryk reflected on his second outing in low Earth orbit — one that required some on-the-fly coordination with Mission Control to pull off.
“So impressive to see the team respond and get us to where we needed to be, despite the challenges that we encountered along the way,” Kutryk said, addressing Mission Control. “Like I said last time, you are masters of your craft.”
Menon touted the importance of this final, and successful, IROSA spacewalk.
“Today marks the completion of not just one solar array, but over 15 years of development efforts to deploy eight arrays and provide power that will allow the station to continue to be a staircase to the moon and eventually Mars,” Menon said. “Privilege of a lifetime. I’ll never forget it.”

Completing a 
complex upgrade
 five years in the making, two spacewalking astronauts on Thursday mounted an eighth and final roll-out solar wing on the 
International Space Station
, boosting the lab's power output to compensate for the age-related degradation of its original arrays. 
NASA began the power-system upgrade in 2021 when two spacewalkers installed the first ISS roll-out solar array, or IROSA, at the base of one of the original eight wings. Six additional panels were attached during subsequent spacewalks, with the most recent installation 
carried out last week
 during a spacewalk by NASA astronaut Anil Menon and Canadian astronaut Joshua Kutryk.
Astronaut Anil Menon, anchored to the end of the International Space Station's robot arm, carries the eighth and final set of add-on solar arrays to the far right side of the lab's power truss for installation.
                NASA TV
The duo ventured back outside Thursday to install the eighth and final IROSA on the far right end of the station's power truss, beginning the extravehicular activity, or EVA — NASA speak for "spacewalk" — at 8:36 a.m. ET.
The final two IROSAs were delivered to the station aboard a 
SpaceX Cargo Dragon capsule earlier this month
, with each one folded in half and stowed in the ship's unpressurized trunk section. The lab's robot arm mounted both rolled-up blankets on the left side of the station's power truss to await installation.
Attachment and extension of the seventh panel last week was relatively straightforward because the 58-foot-long robot arm could easily carry Menon, holding onto a new IROSA, to its nearby installation point on a fixture at the base of an inboard left-side solar wing.
Menon hands the IROSA to crewmate Joshua Kutryk.
                NASA TV
But the Canadarm II does not have the reach to carry Menon and the final blanket directly to the far right end of the power truss as required, so he and Kutryk handed it off to each other leapfrog fashion to get it all the way out to a waiting support frame.
Once mounted on the base of the outboard array, Menon and Kutryk unfolded the new IROSA, bolted it in place, and connected power and data cables during orbital darkness when no sunlight was available to generate electricity.
As the station moved back into sunlight, flight controllers noticed one of the two still-rolled-up blankets did not appear to be generating a small amount of electricity as expected. The spacewalkers then waited for another night pass to double-check the connections. The problem was resolved, and the final IROSA blankets were extended to their full length of 63 feet.
Once installed on a support fixture and wired into the station's power circuits, the final ISS roll-out solar array, or IROSA, unwound to its full length of about 63 feet.
                NASA TV
"These solar arrays are incredible, half the size of the old ones. It's delivering 40% more power," Menon said after returning to the airlock. "It's really empowering all this research that we do and everything that we do to transform space and open the doors to the future." 
The six-hour, 59-minute spacewalk ended at 3:35 p.m.
The IROSAs were designed to offset age-related degradation that has reduced the power output of the station's original arrays over more than two decades of use in the harsh space environment.
The new panels are smaller but more efficient, featuring state-of-the-art solar cells. Even though they shade portions of the arrays they are mounted on, they still provide a net boost in power.
Before the first IROSA was installed, the original arrays were generating about 160 kilowatts of power. The eighth and final IROSA was expected to boost the total power output to some 215 kilowatts, enough to sustain the lab through its planned retirement and de-orbiting in 2030, powering life support systems, computers, research and visiting vehicles.
