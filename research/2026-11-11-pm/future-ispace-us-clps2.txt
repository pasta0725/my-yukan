Colorado-based company eligible to compete as prime contractor for future lunar missions 
DENVER, Colo. — November 7, 2026 — ispace technologies U.S., Inc. (ispace-U.S.), the Colorado-based American entity of ispace, inc. (“ispace”), today confirmed it has been selected for NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services 2 (CLPS 2) Indefinite Delivery, Indefinite Quantity (IDIQ) master contract. The selection makes ispace-U.S. eligible to compete for future NASA task orders as a prime contractor, providing end-to-end payload transportation and lunar surface operations using the company’s ULTRA lander and future lander designs. 
Elizabeth Kryst, CEO of ispace-U.S., with Carlos García-Galán, NASA’s Moon Base Program Manager
“We’re grateful to NASA for this opportunity,” said Elizabeth Kryst, CEO of ispace-U.S. “Over the past six years, our Colorado team has built a strong foundation with real engineering, integration, and flight operations experience behind it. CLPS 2 gives us the opportunity to compete in leading those missions for NASA’s lunar science and Moon Base objectives in the years ahead.” 
ispace-U.S.’s ULTRA lander program is supported by the company’s Denver, Colorado, headquarters, which houses spacecraft development, assembly, integration and test (AIT), and mission operations capabilities, including personnel across engineering, production, test, and flight operations. The company has invested more than $200 million in lunar mission development and supporting technical capabilities. 
The ULTRA lander program also leverages lunar lander development, testing, and flight operations experience from ispace’s Japan location, which has flown two lunar missions to date. 
“ispace-U.S. welcomes the opportunity to support NASA’s Moon Base Program with our ULTRA lander,” said Bretton Alexander, EVP of Business Development at ispace-U.S. “ULTRA offers NASA reliable and affordable access to any location on the lunar surface for science and technology demonstration missions.” 
CLPS 2 is a follow-on to the CLPS1 IDIQ contract, under which NASA has issued task orders to commercial providers for robotic lunar delivery missions since 2018. The new contract vehicle includes a 10-year base ordering period with a 5-year option period, with a not-to-exceed contract ceiling of $10 billion. Selection onto the IDIQ does not itself constitute a funded mission; ispace-U.S. will compete for individual task orders as NASA issues them. Under CLPS 1.0, ispace-U.S. has supported NASA lunar delivery missions as a subcontractor. CLPS 2 provides the opportunity to compete directly as a prime contractor on future task orders.

About ispace-U.S.
ispace-U.S., an American lunar exploration company headquartered in Denver, Colorado, is building the foundational infrastructure for a sustainable lunar economy. The company develops landers and relay satellites—including its Alpine and Lupine satellites, designed and manufactured in the United States—in support of NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services program, national security space organizations, and commercial customers. ispace-U.S. has invested more than $200 million in U.S. lunar technology development at its Colorado facilities. Backed by the global ispace organization’s record of two completed lunar missions, ispace-U.S. combines proven engineering heritage with U.S.-based design and mission operations.

CAPE CANAVERAL, Fla. — NASA is pushing ahead with its plan to build a moon base for astronauts by 2032, with the agency unveiling up to $10 billion in contracts to launch and deliver cargo to the moon and other efforts on Saturday (Nov. 7).
The NASA announcement, revealed here during the space agency's "Max Power" air show event at 
Kennedy Space Center
, included plans for a new $10 billion Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) framework, dubbed CLPS 2, about$1.3 billion in funding for the agency's Venture-Class Acquisition of Dedicated and Rideshare (VADR) program, and the nuclear-powered 
"PROMISE" rover
 on the moon.
The announcements mark another step in NASA's effort to establish infrastructure on 
the moon
 before 
Artemis
 astronauts return to its surface, including a rover and other cargo and research elements.
"Under the current CLPS contract, it's all all or nothing, which might be fine if you're only doing one or two of those [missions] per year," NASA Administrator 
Jared Isaacman
 told Space.com during a press conference on Saturday. "But when you're trying to scale up to what we're talking about today, you want to have the flexibility to move pieces around."
Landing cargo on the moon
NASA selected eight companies to compete for the new CLPS 2 missions under the framework: 
Blue Origin
, Firefly Aerospace, Intuitive Machines, ispace-U.S., Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, 
SpaceX
 and Voyager Technologies.
The contracts for the 10-year CLPS 2 architecture carry a combined maximum value of $10 billion, rather than $10 billion in immediately awarded missions. NASA also increased the ceiling of its existing CLPS contract from $2.6 billion to $4.2 billion to support the agency's "Phase One" 
Moon Base
 objectives.
NASA is also following through on a promise Isaacman made in June, when he proposed repurposing an engineering model of the 
Perseverance
 and 
Curiosity
 Mars rovers (essentially an Earth-based twin) as a mission-ready rover to send to the moon. That rover, called PROMISE (short for "Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration"), is a nuclear-powered robot intended to explore the lunar south pole beginning in late 2028.
The autonomous rover will carry a drill and scientific instruments to search for volatiles and water ice on the lunar surface. NASA awarded Blue Origin a $94 million base contract, with an additional $140 million option period based on performance, to deliver PROMISE to the lunar surface aboard its Blue Moon 
Mark 1 lander
.
Communication satellites, navigation tests and more
In addition to that contract for Blue Origin, NASA awarded six launch and orbital transportation task orders worth a total of approximately $1.3 billion to Arrow Science and Technology, Blue Origin and Impulse Space under its Venture-Class Acquisition of Dedicated and Rideshare (VADR) program.
The missions will demonstrate commercial launch and orbital transportation capabilities, including cargo transfers between Earth and lunar orbits, navigation tests with the CAPSTONE 2 spacecraft and lunar scouting with 
"MoonFall" drones
. Blue Origin, SpaceX and Relativity Space will provide launch support with their 
New Glenn
, Falcon 9 and 
Terran R
 rockets, respectively.
Under a separate lunar logistics contract, NASA selected SpaceX to launch communications satellites aboard its 
Starship
 megarocket in 2028, helping to establish a future lunar communications network with spacecraft similar to the company's 
Starlink
 spacecraft.
NASA also awarded contracts to Honeybee Robotics, Redwire and Yank Technologies to develop components for a lunar power and communications tower, known as miniCAST.
The announcements support NASA's Phase One Moon Base strategy, which is scheduled to extend through 2029 and focuses on robotic exploration, infrastructure demonstrations and commercial deliveries. Phase Two, which runs from 2029 to 2032, will continue to build out infrastructure for the base, which will get up and running during Phase 3.

NASA Awards Moon Base Transportation, Technology, Science Projects
 Jet Propulsion Laboratory 
 https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/ 
Nov. 7, 2026
Artist’s concept of living and working at Moon Base.
 Credit: NASA
Announcements include the agency’s decision to move forward with the nuclear-powered rover PROMISE and transportation for the MoonFall lunar drones — two missions managed by JPL.
NASA announced a suite of awards and mission updates on Saturday to expand the agency’s Moon Base exploration, science, and infrastructure capabilities. The investments will advance commercial robotic deliveries, a nuclear-powered rover named PROMISE, launch and orbital transfer services, and surface infrastructure as the agency works with American industry to establish a sustained presence on the Moon’s South Pole.
The agency shared the news at the MAX POWER aerospace expo and air show at NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.
“We are building the Moon Base mission by mission,” said NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman. “Today’s announcements expand nearly every part of that campaign, using the playbook that carried NASA through its greatest eras: fly often, learn, apply those lessons, and get more better and more capable with every mission. When our astronauts return to the Moon, they will arrive with the first elements of the Moon Base already waiting for them on the surface.”
“In the eight months since Ignition, we’ve made incredible strides in accomplishing the near impossible: establishing a permanent outpost on the lunar surface,” said Carlos García-Galán, manager, Moon Base Program. “We’ve consolidated numerous efforts into a streamlined program, evolving our human exploration architecture into an iterative and sustained campaign for Moon Base technology development and implementation. Most importantly, we’ve taken action, awarding new contracts and forging new partnerships to accelerate development.”
Moon Base cargo landers
To strengthen the cargo lander network needed to support the Moon Base, NASA selected eight companies for the next phase of its CLPS (
Commercial Lunar Payload Services
) initiative. The companies are eligible to compete for future lunar surface delivery missions under the new CLPS 2 contract:
Blue Origin
Firefly Aerospace
Intuitive Machines
iSpace US
Lockheed Martin Space
Northrop Grumman Space Systems
SpaceX
Voyager Technologies
Under the CLPS 2 framework, the companies will provide lunar delivery services, including managing, building, and operating landers that transport NASA science, technology, and infrastructure payloads to the Moon.
The CLPS 2 framework is a 10-year, multiple-award, indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity master contract with a combined maximum value of $10 billion. NASA increased the value of the current CLPS 1.0 contract from $2.6 billion to $4.2 billion to support initial 
Phase One
 Moon Base objectives. The original CLPS contract will remain in place through November 2028, with an additional five-year period to complete awarded lunar deliveries through November 2033.
Nuclear-powered Moon rover
NASA also announced it is moving forward with development of PROMISE (
Polar Rover for Observation, Mapping, and In-Situ Exploration
). Carrying science instruments and technology demonstrations, the six-wheeled rover will land at the Moon’s South Pole to characterize the surface and subsurface, assess resource potential, and provide data to enable landing site selection, as well as future infrastructure and exploration activities. The nuclear-powered rover can operate during long lunar nights and within permanently shadowed regions thought to harbor ice, which is crucial for a sustained human presence on the Moon.
The agency’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, a division of Caltech, will manage the mission and build the rover from a combination of spare flight components and engineering test models for NASA’s Curiosity and Perseverance Mars rovers. Using proven hardware, software, and Mars rover operation approaches significantly reduces cost and enables integration and testing of the complex PROMISE rover on a shorter timeline. NASA has assessed the compatibility of the hardware with the lunar environment and determined it can tolerate the harsh conditions found near the Moon’s South Pole.
The PROMISE rover will carry a drill to extract regolith, or lunar dust, to characterize ice and other resources from approximately 3 feet (1 meter) below the surface, spectrometers for analyzing the chemical composition of surface material, temperature and radiation sensors, and visible-light cameras. It will demonstrate a next-generation, radiation-tolerant computing system, 
NASA’s High Performance Spaceflight Computing
, and advanced autonomy sensors and algorithms. NASA is targeting late 2028 to launch PROMISE and planning an 18-month mission for the rover.
NASA awarded Blue Origin a $94 million base contract, with an option period worth an additional $140 million based on performance to deliver PROMISE to the lunar South Pole. The award expands the task order 
awarded
 in May 2026 to deliver two lunar terrain vehicles to the Moon. Blue Origin will use its Mark 1 lander to deliver and deploy the rover as part of the Moon Base’s first phase.
Launch, transportation awards
Delivering Moon Base infrastructure will require a robust transportation network between Earth and the Moon. Orbital Transfer Vehicles (OTVs) act like long-distance freight trucks, carrying large loads across space. Lunar landers handle the final delivery, bringing supplies, equipment, and infrastructure safely down to the Moon’s surface.
To demonstrate those capabilities, NASA awarded six task orders as part of the agency’s Venture-Class Acquisition of Dedicated and Rideshare (VADR) launch services contract. The awards were given to Arrow Science and Technology, Blue Origin, and Impulse Space to integrate and transport Moon Base cargo.
Three mission types will allow NASA flexibility to fly science and technology demonstrations when they are ready.
The first will include missions to reduce risks of future long-term human spaceflight for lunar exploration. The missions will verify that communication and navigation methods work properly during the journey from Earth orbit to the Moon, and OTV capability to support delivery of a CLPS lunar lander to a desired orbit. This mission type will deliver the 
CAPSTONE 02
 spacecraft to deep space to test proximity maneuvers, navigation, and communications around the Moon.
The second category of missions will prepare the lunar South Pole for human exploration by sending a group of highly mobile drones to survey the surface before astronauts arrive, enabling safer, more informed human missions while demonstrating advanced robotic capabilities. Such missions will include the agency's 
MoonFall
 drones, designed to scout areas beyond the reach of traditional rovers, as well as delivery of additional Moon Base assets necessary to support Phase One objectives.
To reduce risk for future missions, the third mission type will test navigation technologies and demonstrate operations in multiple cislunar orbits, between Earth and the Moon. This mission type will demonstrate commercial orbital transfer capabilities intended to increase the amount of cargo that future landers can deliver to the lunar surface.
The task orders have a value of approximately $1.3 billion combined and fall under the 10-year VADR indefinite-delivery/indefinite-quantity contract, which has a maximum total value of $5 billion across all contracts. The VADR contracts are managed by NASA’s Launch Services Program Office at the agency’s Kennedy Space Center as part of the agency’s Commercial Access to Space Program. By procuring multiple launch and in-space transportation services together, NASA provides industry with a more predictable set of mission opportunities while gaining access to a broader range of commercial capabilities for future lunar exploration.
Some missions will launch aboard Blue Origin’s New Glenn, Relativity Space’s Terran R, and SpaceX’s Falcon 9 rockets. In-space transportation spacecraft for these missions will be provided by Blue Origin, Firefly Aerospace, Impulse Space, and Quantum Space.
Complementing those in-space transportation demonstrations, NASA selected SpaceX for the Cislunar Communications Deployment Demonstration under an existing lunar logistics contract. Through a Specialized Delivery Mission task order, SpaceX will launch communications satellites aboard a Starship to cislunar space in 2028. The demonstration will help inform the development of future lunar communications networks that keep astronauts, mission controllers, and the Moon Base connected.
Lunar architecture, infrastructure
To extend infrastructure capabilities on the lunar surface, NASA plans to purchase commercial components for a Mini-Communications and Solar Tower (miniCAST), including solar arrays, batteries, and wireless power-transfer hardware. The agency awarded Honeybee Robotics, a Blue Origin company, for energy storage capability, Redwire for power generation, and Yank Technologies, Inc for wireless power transfer in support of miniCAST.

The miniCAST will combine power and communications capabilities to support future Moon Base activities. Engineers at NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, will assemble and test the systems before they are delivered to the lunar surface.
NASA expects work to begin within months, and the initial effort will involve components rather than a major integrated system. The agency also plans a separate solicitation for fully integrated power systems, which is anticipated in early 2027.
NASA intends to release a directed call under its Next Space Technologies for Exploration Partnerships, or NextSTEP-3 B, solicitation to help close 
architecture-driven
, high-priority data gaps for Moon Base development. The effort will help NASA gather information it needs to plan future missions, reduce risk, and support technologies and infrastructure for sustained operations on the Moon’s South Pole. The work could include small spacecraft missions, payloads on future Moon Base flights, or the purchase of data collected by commercial partners.
As part of a Golden Age of innovation and exploration, NASA will send Artemis astronauts on increasingly difficult missions to the lunar South Pole to explore more of the Moon than ever before. In addition to providing economic benefits and advancing scientific discovery through its Moon missions, NASA will establish an enduring human presence on the lunar surface and build on this foundation for the first crewed mission to Mars.
