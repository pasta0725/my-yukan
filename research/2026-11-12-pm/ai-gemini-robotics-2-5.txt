Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics 2.5 scores 96 percent sorting metal parts in a Mercedes-Benz assembly pilot
Maximilian Schreiner
View the LinkedIn Profile of Maximilian Schreiner
					Nov 10, 2026				
Google Deepmind
Key Points
Google Deepmind introduced Gemini Robotics 2.5, a new VLA model it says performs 30 to 100 percent better than its own previous baselines depending on the task type.
On active Mercedes-Benz production lines in Hungary and Germany, Apptronik's humanoid robot Apollo 2 sorted metal parts with a reported success rate of 96 percent.
Google Deepmind uses its language model Gemini 4 Argon as a simulation engine for training and released the open-source benchmark ASIMOV-Jailbreak to test the safety of robotics models.
Google Deepmind has unveiled a new AI model for robot control. In a pilot test on active Mercedes-Benz production lines, a humanoid robot reportedly achieved a 96 percent success rate.
Google Deepmind has introduced 
Gemini Robotics 2.5
, a vision-language-action model (VLA) that processes visual information, understands language instructions, and translates them into robot movements. The model builds on its predecessor, introduced earlier this year, which was designed to give robots 
full-body control
 for the first time.
Google Deepmind says it tested the model on more than 200 tasks, including serving food with tongs and pipetting liquids. Compared to the company's own previous baselines, it reportedly achieves a 30 to 100 percent higher success rate depending on the task type and completes tasks 20 to 30 percent faster.
Ad
The model is designed to work with different robot types, including Apptronik's humanoid robot Apollo 2 with a five-fingered hand that has 22 degrees of freedom, and the Franka Duo platform with two-finger parallel grippers.
Ad
Google Deepmind tests the model on active Mercedes-Benz production lines
Together with Apptronik and Mercedes-Benz, Google Deepmind tested the model on active production lines in Kecskemét, Hungary, and Sindelfingen, Germany. The Apollo 2 robot handled kitting: it sorted metal parts from bulk bins and placed them into trays in the order needed for each vehicle on the assembly line. Two AI models worked in tandem. 
Gemini Robotics ER
 monitored the workflow, while Gemini Robotics 2.5 controlled hands and arms.
Video: Google
Ad
Google Deepmind reports a 96 percent success rate for this pilot test. Apptronik CEO Jeff Cardenas said the models improved significantly within a few weeks of post-training and successfully transitioned to Mercedes production with minimal performance loss.
New Safety Benchmark Against Jailbreaks
According to Google Deepmind, Gemini Robotics 2.5 uses real-time control that enables the robot to avoid physical hazards while performing a task. In its own evaluations, safe task completion improved by 33 percent near obstacles and by 43 percent near people.
Ad
In addition, Google Deepmind has published the open-source 
ASIMOV-Jailbreak
 benchmark 
on Hugging Face
. It contains hundreds of prompts that disguise unsafe requests in dozens of scenes and is designed to test whether robotics models can be tricked into performing dangerous actions. According to the company, Google’s own model, Gemini 4 Argon, achieves a jailbreak resistance of 99.5 percent, compared to 82 percent for its predecessor, Gemini Robotics ER 2.
Ad
Argon Language Model Generates Training Data via Simulation
During the development of Gemini Robotics 2.5, Google Deepmind used its own language model, Gemini 4 Argon, as a simulation engine. Argon can generate thousands of simulated training runs from a real camera scene and create a digital twin. In combination with real-world training demonstrations, this is intended to help the robot generalize more quickly to new scenes and objects. Argon can also derive hypotheses about the causes of errors from video recordings and test improvements in simulation and on the actual robot.
According to Google Deepmind, Gemini Robotics 2.5 is being rolled out to partners Agile, Apptronik, and Mercedes-Benz.

On November 10, 2026, Google DeepMind announced Gemini Robotics 2.5, a new AI model that controls robot movements. In a proof of concept on live Mercedes-Benz production lines, the company says it achieved a 
96%
 success rate on parts sorting and kitting tasks. However, that figure applies to a specific parts-preparation task, not to the success rate across the entire automotive manufacturing process.
The demonstration combined "Gemini Robotics ER," which tracks the progress of the overall task, with "Gemini Robotics 2.5," which controls the movements of the robot's hands and arms. In addition to improved task performance, DeepMind published evaluation results on hazard avoidance during operation and resistance to dangerous instructions. The robot used in the demonstration also has a safety mechanism that detects surrounding objects and stops. These address different kinds of risk, so the AI model's judgment and the hardware's safety measures need to be evaluated separately.
AD
Automating parts preparation on live Mercedes-Benz lines
According to 
Google DeepMind's official announcement
, the proof of concept was carried out on Mercedes-Benz's live production lines in Kecskemét, Hungary, and Sindelfingen, Germany. The robot used was "Apollo 2," a humanoid developed by Apptronik. It was tasked with picking up metal brackets tossed loosely into supply bins and arranging them in a different bin in the order required for each vehicle.
In this task, Gemini Robotics ER tracks how the work is progressing, while Gemini Robotics 2.5 controls hand and arm movements. DeepMind also describes a function that detects missing parts and corrects for them. The aim is to let robots handle this kind of parts preparation so that human workers can focus on more complex assembly steps.
The 
96%
 success rate DeepMind reported is strictly the result of a proof of concept for this parts sorting and kitting task. The relevant section of the official announcement does not provide details such as the number of trials, the evaluation period, or how often humans intervened during the work. Performing a specific task on an operating production line is meaningful, but this number alone cannot be used to judge long-term stable operation or applicability across an entire factory.
Improved dexterity and task speed across roughly 200 tasks
DeepMind evaluated Gemini Robotics 2.5 on roughly 200 tasks. Examples include handling food with tongs and tying a bow with ribbon on a gift box. These tasks require not only accurate tool use but also fine movements for handling materials that easily change shape. The evaluation emphasizes tasks that demand dexterous hand manipulation, going beyond simply moving objects.
According to DeepMind, success rates improved by 30 to 
100%
 over previous baselines, depending on the type of task. Task completion speed also rose by 20 to 
30%
. However, these are relative improvements over earlier models; they do not mean that every task reached a 
100%
 success rate or that success rates rose by up to 100 percentage points. Actual success rates and the size of the improvement vary by task.
The supported robots are not limited to a single machine, either. Apollo 2, used in the factory demonstration, has a SharpaWave hand with five fingers and 22 degrees of freedom, while the Franka Duo uses two-finger parallel grippers. On its 
Gemini Robotics model page
, DeepMind says the model also supports whole-body control that coordinates the entire body, not just the hands. Movements such as walking while adjusting the center of gravity, squatting, and bending over are envisioned, but these are capabilities the company describes, not a guarantee that the model works on every machine or in every environment.
"Gemini 4 Argon" was also used in training the model. DeepMind describes a method that generates thousands of simulated practice scenarios from footage of real environments captured by cameras, building digital twins that replicate real-world settings. By combining these simulations with demonstration data from real robots, the company aims to improve the model's ability to handle diverse situations. The system also helps form hypotheses about the causes of failures from footage of failed attempts and test improvements. However, no specific figures have been given for how much this approach shortens training time.
AD
Three different mechanisms underpin robot safety
The safety measures DeepMind described fall broadly into three categories: the robot's ability to avoid obstacles and people while operating, resistance to instructions that encourage dangerous behavior, and a physical stopping mechanism built into the hardware. Because the hazards addressed and the evaluation methods differ, the individual performance metrics cannot be combined into a single safety indicator.

On hazard avoidance during operation, DeepMind says the ability to complete tasks safely improved by 
33%
 around obstacles and 
43%
 around people. This evaluates the ability to finish tasks while avoiding dangerous movements through inference-time control, and does not mean physical accident rates fell by the same proportion. The announcement does not give absolute success rates before and after, so there is not enough information to judge what level of safety was reached.
For resistance to dangerous instructions, the evaluation used the public benchmark "ASIMOV-Jailbreak." The test measures resistance to hundreds of prompts that disguise dangerous requests across dozens of scenarios. Gemini 4 Argon scored 
99.5%
, ahead of the 
82%
 recorded by Gemini Robotics ER 2, the comparison model. However, this figure is an evaluation result for Gemini 4 Argon and is not a direct measurement of the operational safety of Gemini Robotics 2.5 itself. Moreover, being able to refuse dangerous instructions is a separate matter from a real robot avoiding physical accidents.
On the hardware side, Apollo 2 has an object detection and stopping mechanism. According to Apptronik, Apollo 2 has a zone that triggers an immediate stop when an object is detected, and a surrounding zone in which behavior can be configured when people, equipment, or obstacles approach. This mechanism provides hardware-side safety that is independent of whether the AI model recognizes and refuses a dangerous instruction. However, how each zone was configured in the Mercedes-Benz factory demonstration cannot be confirmed from the published materials.
None of these results guarantees that the robot will not cause an accident. The AI model that chooses safe movements, the mechanism that refuses dangerous instructions, and the hardware stopping mechanism each play a different role. In real workplaces, these must be combined and then verified to confirm that contact with equipment and people can be prevented.
Full factory deployment will require validation of recovery from failures and sustained operation
DeepMind is rolling out Gemini Robotics 2.5 to partners in the robotics field, and the model page also directs interested parties to an early-access waitlist. However, availability to partners is different from being freely available to general developers and companies. Anyone considering adoption needs to check who is actually eligible and what the terms of use are.
On the operational side, the robot's own management systems come into play alongside the AI model. According to Apptronik's product description, "Artemis" coordinates functions such as perception, planning, and control, while "Fleet Connect" handles robot status monitoring, task coordination, and deployment management. However, detailed specifications on exactly how these systems were used in this factory demonstration, or how they connect to Google's model, have not been confirmed.
A 
96%
 success rate on live Mercedes-Benz lines represents progress toward practical use of humanoid robots for parts preparation. But completing a specific task with high probability is not the same as operating stably in a factory over a long period. Going forward, the key questions will be whether the robot can recover on its own when a task fails, how much human intervention is needed, and how performance and work efficiency change with continuous operation. If such operational data becomes available, it will be easier to connect the results of this proof of concept to deployment decisions on actual manufacturing floors.

Gemini Robotics 2.5 on Apollo 2 at Mercedes: How to Read the 96%
        Jaime Delgado
          Verify on LinkedIn
Robot enthusiast, former lawyer turned software developer and product expert. More interested in what leaves the factory than what gets promised on a keynote stage — because that's where the real story usually hides.
November 11, 2026 · 10 min read
Graphic: RobotTesters. Data: Google DeepMind (10 Nov 2026); misses per 1,000 is our arithmetic on the 96% figure.
      On 
10 November 2026
, Google DeepMind introduced 
Gemini Robotics 2.5
, the newest version of its vision-language-action model.
      A VLA model takes camera images and an instruction in ordinary language and turns them into motor commands for a robot’s arms and hands, so the same model can, in principle, learn many jobs instead of one.
      The headline that travelled was a single number from a factory: Apptronik’s 
Apollo 2
 humanoid, running the new model, kitted car parts on live Mercedes-Benz lines with a 
96% success rate
.
      That is a real step for humanoids, and it’s also the kind of figure a tester should slow down on.
      This piece explains what Apollo actually did in Kecskemét and Sindelfingen, then reads the 96% the way we’d read any robot claim before believing it: what was counted, what wasn’t, and what it would need to mean for a production line.
Key takeaways
Gemini Robotics 2.5 was announced on 
10 November 2026
. DeepMind reports a 
30–100%
 higher success rate than its previous model depending on the task, and 
20–30%
 faster completion, across more than 200 tasks.
In a proof-of-concept with Apptronik and Mercedes-Benz, Apollo 2 humanoids picked metal brackets from bulk bins into sequencing containers in Kecskemét (Hungary) and Sindelfingen (Germany), with a reported 
96%
 success rate.
DeepMind did not publish the number of trials, the definition of a success, how often a person stepped in, or a cycle time. Mercedes has not published its own figures.
The model runs on cloud servers, the factory video was sped up 2× according to Ars Technica, and Gemini Robotics 2.5 is only rolling out to partners. There’s no price and no date for anyone else.
What Apollo 2 did on the Mercedes lines
      The task is called kitting. Parts arrive in big bulk bins, and someone has to pick them out one by one and drop them into trays in the exact order a specific car on the line will need them.
      Carmakers call that just-in-sequence supply, and it matters because a wrong part in the wrong slot can stop the line further down.
      It’s repetitive and physically tiring, which is why it keeps coming up as a first job for humanoids, as it did with Figure at BMW’s Spartanburg plant (see our 
Figure 03 BotQ piece
).
      DeepMind describes two models working together. 
Gemini Robotics ER
, the embodied-reasoning model, keeps track of the workflow: which tray, which part, what’s missing.
Gemini Robotics 2.5
 drives the arms and hands, reaching into the bin, picking a bracket and placing it, “all while correcting for any gaps”, in DeepMind’s words.
      Apollo 2 does this with the five-finger 
SharpaWave
 hand, which has 22 degrees of freedom. A degree of freedom is one independent joint motion, so 22 in a single hand is a lot of control to coordinate for a part that a simple gripper could also grab.
      Jeff Cardenas, Apptronik’s co-founder and CEO, said the models “improve[d] dramatically in just a few weeks of post-training” and carried over to Mercedes production “with very little drop-off”.
      Post-training means taking the general model and tuning it on data from the specific job.
      If that holds up, it’s the most interesting claim in the release, because weeks of tuning per task is what would make a general robot cheaper to deploy than a custom cell.
      It’s also a claim from the company that builds the robot, and I haven’t seen an independent check of it.
The 96% has no denominator yet
      A success rate is a fraction, and DeepMind published only the result.
      The blog post doesn’t say how many kitting attempts the 96% covers, what counts as one success (a single pick, a full tray, a full shift), or whether a human reset the robot when it got stuck.
      TechTimes made the same point on 11 November and showed why it matters: 24 out of 25 and 960 out of 1,000 both round to 96%, but the first could plausibly sit anywhere from about 80% to 99%, while the second is pinned close to 96%.
      Here is the checklist I’d want answered before calling this production-ready, in the order a plant manager would probably ask:
Trials.
 How many kitting attempts, over how many shifts, at each plant.
Success definition.
 Is a success one correct pick, or a complete, correctly ordered tray?
Interventions.
 How often did a person step in, reset a part or restart the robot, and are those attempts counted as failures?
Throughput.
 Picks per hour against a human doing the same station. DeepMind gave no cycle time for the pilot.
Variants.
 How many part types and sequences were tested. Variety is exactly what makes car plants hard to automate.
Latency.
 What happens on the line when the connection to the cloud model is slow or drops.
      None of that makes the result fake. It means the 96% is a demonstration number for now, and the follow-up data is what turns it into a deployment number.
What 96% means at factory scale
      To see why the last few points matter, run the arithmetic. The table below is our own calculation on the one public figure, not something DeepMind or Mercedes published.
      It assumes each attempt succeeds or fails independently, which real failures usually don’t, so treat it as a sense of scale.

      Forty misses in a thousand is a lot of manual fixing on a line that runs all day, and if the 96% is per pick rather than per tray, two in three complete trays come out clean.
      If it’s per tray, the picture is much better. That’s why the definition matters more than the number.
      DeepMind is open about the gap. Kanishka Rao, a DeepMind director, told Ars Technica that closing the last 4% will be a “steep road”, and robotics head Carolina Parada framed the trade-off directly: you can be very good at one application at the cost of generalization, or push on generalization “and maybe not get to 99.9 in every application.”
      I think that’s the honest summary of the whole release. Gemini Robotics is a bet on one model that does many jobs reasonably well, and factories traditionally pay for machines that do one job almost perfectly.
What the release does show
      The jump from lab to line is real. Most humanoid manipulation results still come from research benches, and this one comes from two working plants in two countries.
      The lab numbers behind it are big too: DeepMind tested more than 200 tasks, from tying a bow on a gift box to pipetting liquid and serving food with tongs, and TechTimes listed task-level gains such as a toolbox lid going from 0% to 93% and ribbon bows from 0% to 70%.
      Deformable objects like ribbon and clothing are some of the hardest things to manipulate, so those gains count, even where the final numbers are still well below anything you’d sell.
      Safety got more attention than usual. DeepMind says inference-time reasoning, where the model checks the scene for hazards before and during each action, raised safe task completion by 
33%
 around obstacles and 
43%
 around nearby people.
      It also released 
ASIMOV-Jailbreak
, an open benchmark of prompts that try to trick a robot into unsafe actions, and reports 99.5% resistance for Gemini 4 Argon against 82% for Gemini Robotics ER 2.
      Those are developer-run tests on a developer-built benchmark, but publishing the benchmark lets other labs check them, which is more than most humanoid safety claims offer.
      It fits the conversation at this week’s 
CoRL and Humanoid Safety Summit
.
      The training story is worth a line as well. DeepMind says Gemini 4 Argon generated thousands of simulated practice runs from real camera scenes, building digital twins of the workcell, and mixed them with real demonstrations.
      That is the 
sim-to-real
 problem in a new form: whatever the model learns in a virtual copy still has to survive real lighting, friction and part variation.
What it doesn’t show yet
      This is a proof-of-concept, not a commercial deployment. Neither DeepMind nor Mercedes has said how many Apollo units will work at either plant, or when.
      Ars Technica reports that the factory video is sped up 2× to look more human-like, and that the model runs on cloud servers rather than on the robot, so every gain in accuracy still carries network latency.
      Mercedes isn’t a neutral venue either: it has a commercial agreement with Apptronik and has invested in the company, alongside Google, which owns DeepMind.
      And there’s nothing to buy. Gemini Robotics 2.5 is “rolling out to our robotics partners”, which Ars lists as Agile Robots, Apptronik and Boston Dynamics.
      Apptronik hasn’t published a price for Apollo, which is why it stays unscored on our 
Apollo page
 under our 
methodology
.
How we’d test a kitting humanoid
      If we had an Apollo 2 at a kitting station for a week, the test would be dull on purpose, which is how factory tests should be.
      We’d run full shifts rather than clips, log every attempt with a written pass rule, count every human touch as an intervention, and time picks per hour next to a person at the same station.
      We’d rotate part variants in the middle of a shift, and pull the network cable once to see what the robot does when the cloud model goes quiet.
      That’s the same approach as our 
guide to testing a humanoid robot
, applied to one station.
      For the bigger picture of which carmakers are putting humanoids on their lines, see 
the carmakers’ humanoid race
, and for how VLA models fit with the rest of a robot’s software, 
our humanoid AI systems explainer
.
      Boston Dynamics is also on DeepMind’s partner list, so the next interesting result could come from 
Atlas
 (more on its CEO in our 
Robert Playter profile
).
      My read: Gemini Robotics 2.5 is the most convincing factory result a general-purpose robot model has published so far, and the 96% is a starting point, not a finish line.
      I’ll believe it’s ready for production when someone publishes the trial count and the interventions per shift next to it.
