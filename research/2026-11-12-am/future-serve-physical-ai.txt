Introducing Serve Physical AI Services
Introducing Serve Physical AI Services
Article
Today, we’re introducing Serve Physical AI Services, opening the technology behind our own robots to other companies building physical AI.
We’ve deployed more than 2,000 robots in more than 40 cities and 25 hospitals. Operating at that scale has meant building tools to keep our fleets connected and find the real-world data that can help our models improve.
Physical AI Services brings that technology together through high-performance connectivity, data curation and real-world training data. Bedrock Robotics, which builds autonomous systems for heavy construction equipment, is among our launch customers.
What Serve Physical AI Services includes
Serve Link
Serve Link provides a high-performance, reliable connection between machines in the field and off-board intelligence. As more of the computation behind physical AI moves to data centers, it connects robots to the models, monitoring and training systems that help make them smarter in real time.
Serve Data Curator
Serve Data Curator helps engineering teams find the rare moments in fleet data that matter for model improvement. By surfacing valuable examples from the volume of data a fleet produces, the tool is designed to shorten the loop between what robots see in the field and what their models learn.
Serve Data Catalog
Our foundation-model data catalog draws on Serve’s own sidewalk deployments, including terrain and infrastructure data. It gives teams real-world examples to help train models to understand complex human environments, such as city sidewalks.
Supporting fleets beyond the sidewalk
Serve Physical AI Services already powers thousands of vehicles across a dozen industries, including construction, mining and autonomous trucking, as well as our own sidewalk food delivery fleet.
Bedrock Robotics is one example. It uses Serve Link to connect its machines over Wi-Fi, LTE, 5G and satellite networks.
With Serve Physical AI Services, we’re making the technology behind our own fleets available to more teams building machines for the physical world.
To learn more about Serve’s Physical AI Services, visit 
serverobotics.com/physical-ai-services
.

“Building a robot is only the beginning. Keeping thousands of them connected, learning and improving requires another layer of technology. We’ve put that technology to work in our own fleet. Now we’re opening it to other companies so they can focus on what their robots do, rather than rebuilding the systems around them.”
Ali Kashani, Co-founder and CEO of Serve Robotics.

Serve Robotics, the sidewalk delivery robot company, is opening up the connectivity and data tools it built for its own fleet and selling them to other robot makers as a new business line called Physical AI Services. 
Why it matters:
 Serve is betting the durable money in robotics is not only in running robots, but in selling the infrastructure every robot company needs — the same playbook Amazon followed with AWS.
November 5, 2026
What happened
Serve Robotics announced Physical AI Services on November 5, 2026, alongside its third-quarter earnings. The company, which spun out of Uber’s acquisition of Postmates in 2021 and went public in 2024, will offer other companies building robots its connectivity and data tools. Bedrock Robotics, a startup developing self-driving systems for construction equipment, is already a customer, according to Serve. Serve is also working with T-Mobile to bring AI computing power closer to robots in the field.
Chief executive and co-founder Ali Kashani told TechCrunch the company is solving the problem of robots operating in human spaces, and described the move as the company’s AWS moment: technology built first for its own fleet, then opened up for others to build on. The delivery business is not going away — Serve also runs a hospital robot business from its acquisition of Diligent Robotics, and its sidewalk delivery operation is expanding while shifting away from Uber toward partners including DoorDash and Wonder.
What Physical AI Services sells
The new business line has three products. Serve Link connects robots in the field to AI systems running in data centers, so a robot can tap more powerful models than it can carry onboard; Kashani said the hard part is keeping that connection reliable over unreliable networks, whether the robot is collecting data or needs exception handling when something goes wrong. A data curator tool helps companies find edge cases in the large volumes of real-world data a robot collects, so those cases can be fed back into training to improve performance. The third product is a real-world, foundation-model data catalog built from Serve’s own sidewalk robots, meant to teach AI models how to understand complex human environments.
The target customers are companies building robots for farms, warehouses and other uncontrolled environments — places where, like sidewalks, the robot has to share space with people.
Why it matters
Every robotics company hits the same wall: the robot itself is only part of the problem, and the unglamorous layers underneath — connectivity that survives bad networks, data pipelines that surface the moments a model got confused, training data from real human environments — take years to build. Serve has spent those years at fleet scale, and it is now testing whether rivals would rather buy those layers than build them. If Physical AI Services signs on customers beyond Bedrock, Serve becomes a platform supplier to the industry it also competes in; if it doesn’t, the launch is a reminder that infrastructure built for one fleet does not automatically fit everyone else’s robots. Either way, it is a notable strategic turn for one of the few publicly traded pure-play robotics companies.
