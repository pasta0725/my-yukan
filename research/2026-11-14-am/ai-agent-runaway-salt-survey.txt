State of Agentic AI and API Security 2H 2026
Nearly Half of Enterprises Report an AI Agent Took an Unauthorized Action Last Year, Salt Security Research Finds
State of Agentic AI and API Security 2H 2026: survey of over 380 security and technology professionals plus Salt Labs production telemetry finds AI agents deployed, autonomous, and outpacing the governance controls organizations need to manage what they do.
PALO ALTO, Calif., October 13, 2026 - 
Salt Security
, the leader in agentic and API security, today published the 
State of Agentic AI and API Security 2H 2026
, a survey of over 380 security and technology professionals combined with Salt Labs production telemetry from hundreds of customer environments. The report finds that agentic AI has moved into enterprise daily operations faster than the visibility, governance, and containment controls organizations need to manage what agents do.
The central finding: 49.8% of respondents confirmed or suspected an AI agent in their organization took an action in the past 12 months that the organization did not intend, expect, or authorize. Among that group, more than half reported an unauthorized external API call or transaction, and more than 40% reported that an agent modified or deleted data.
"The data shows a gap that is already producing real operational consequences," said Roey Eliyahu, CEO of Salt Security.
 "Organizations are deploying agents and granting them access to enterprise data, APIs, and services before they can trace what those agents are doing or stop them automatically when something goes wrong. Closing that gap is the discipline of agentic security, and it is what lets enterprises give AI agents the autonomy they need without inheriting unmanaged risk."
Key findings
Agents are widespread and increasingly autonomous.
 86.4% of respondents report at least one AI agent operating in their organization; 59.5% run more than ten. 76.8% report agents that chain actions across other agents, tools, and APIs -- sequences no single team designed in advance.
Most organizations cannot trace what agents do. 
Only 12.5% can consistently trace a complete agent path across all agents, from initial instruction through tools and APIs to outcome. More than 60% would need hours or days of manual work to reconstruct what a specific agent did the previous day.
Containment runs at human speed, not machine speed.
 Only 17.5% can automatically stop an agent that begins behaving unexpectedly in production. Most can intervene only manually, or by taking the application offline.
Shadow MCP infrastructure is already a live operational problem.
 32.9% have found an unauthorized, shadow, or misconfigured MCP server in the past 12 months; 54.3% confirmed or suspected one. Salt Labs independently found MCP-related infrastructure in 21.6% of customer environments in August 2026, up from 16.7% in January -- a roughly 30% relative increase in detected prevalence.
AI-generated code is a live security problem in production.
 83.5% of respondents estimate AI-generated or AI-assisted code contributes to at least some of their application security findings. Only 6.8% resolve issues inside the coding assistant at generation time; the rest land in code review, testing, or after deployment.
The survey data reflects a risk that Salt Labs has observed in production. In October 2026, 
Salt Labs demonstrated that a single malicious email could hijack the Manus agentic AI platform
 and reach a user's connected email, cloud storage, and code repository accounts without a stolen password or a clicked link. The platform's own guardrail detected the attack, but still could not stop it before the malicious code had run. That finding illustrates the gap the survey quantifies: most organizations have detection controls that fire after an agent has already acted. 
"The Manus research and this survey data are two views of the same problem," said Yaniv Balmas, Head of Research at Salt Security.
 "In the field, we showed how an agent can be weaponized through a single malicious email, how the guardrail detected the attack at exactly the right moment, and still could not stop the code from running. The survey quantifies how widespread that architecture is. Only 17.5% of organizations can automatically contain a misbehaving agent in production. Manus is one example of what the other 82.5% looks like." 
The full State of Agentic AI and API Security 2H 2026 report, including sector breakdowns, CISO recommendations, and Salt Labs telemetry analysis, is available at: 
https://salt.security/api-security-trends
.
About the research
The 2H 2026 survey contains over 380 analyzed responses from security, technology, architecture, application security, API platform, and DevOps professionals. Respondents span technology (35%), financial services and insurance (16.7%), healthcare (11%), manufacturing (11%), and additional sectors. Salt Labs telemetry is drawn from hundreds of production customer environments. Full methodology is in the report.

Only 17.5 percent of respondents to a new Salt Security survey said their organizations could automatically stop an AI agent behaving unexpectedly in production. Most could intervene only manually or by taking the application offline.
The security vendor’s October 13 report draws on more than 380 responses from professionals working in security, technology, architecture, application security, API platforms and DevOps. It also includes a separate analysis of telemetry from hundreds of production customer environments. The survey figures describe respondents’ accounts of their organizations, rather than an independently verified incident tally.
Nearly half, 49.8 percent, said they had confirmed or suspected that an agent had taken an action their organization did not intend, expect or authorize during the previous 12 months. The combined figure includes suspicions and should not be read as a count of confirmed breaches.
Among that group, 53.9 percent reported an external API call or transaction, 41.9 percent reported modified or deleted data, and 32.5 percent reported access to data the agent should not have reached. These percentages apply to the incident-reporting group, not the entire sample.
Tracing activity was also difficult. Only 12.5 percent said they could consistently follow the complete path from an agent’s instruction through its tools and APIs to the outcome across all agents. Reconstructing everything a particular agent had done the previous day would require hours or days of manual work for 64.5 percent.
The survey found extensive deployment: 86.4 percent reported at least one AI agent, while 59.5 percent reported more than 10. Technology companies accounted for 35 percent of respondents; financial services and insurance accounted for 16.7 percent. The public release does not establish that the sample represents enterprises generally. The full report, including its methodology, is available through a registration form; the company has published the findings and sector breakdown online.
Salt’s separate customer telemetry found Model Context Protocol infrastructure in 21.6 percent of environments in August, up from 16.7 percent in January. MCP connects agents to tools and data. Its presence alone does not indicate an attack or an unauthorized deployment.
The company also points to a controlled Manus test it disclosed on October 1. Researchers placed malicious instructions in an email, then asked the connected agent to read the inbox. The agent executed code that exposed access tokens for connected accounts before its guardrail raised a warning. Salt says the flaw has been resolved and is no longer exploitable.
