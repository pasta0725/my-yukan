Netflix and the Korean Film Council Partner Up… 120 Filmmakers Gather
Sharing Know-How on Global Content Planning and Production
From On-the-Job Training to Achievements in Professional Workforce Development
Choi Hee-jae
2026-11-11 17:58:38
LANGUAGE
Share
                        [Edaily Reporter Choi Hee-jae ] Netflix is joining forces with the Korean Film Council and the Korean Film Academy to support the development of talent in the domestic film and video industry. The initiative aims to strengthen the capabilities of domestic creative professionals and expand the production ecosystem by sharing everything from global content planning strategies to practical know-how from the production floor.
Kim Ki-jae, CE, explaining the importance of “Local for Local” (Photo courtesy of Netflix)
On the 10th, Ha Jeong-soo, Director of Netflix Korea’s Content Production Division, and Kim Ki-jae, Creative Executive (CE) of Netflix Korea’s Content Division, served as speakers at the Korean Film Council’s “KAFA+ Filmmaker Education: Understanding Netflix’s Global Strategy and Production,” held at the Korean Film Archive’s Cinematheque.
The session was attended by 120 participants, including film and video industry professionals and aspiring filmmakers—such as students majoring in film and video, program graduates, creators with credits in feature films or OTT productions, and those with experience in related fields. Key personnel from Netflix who have been responsible for the planning and production of Korean content took the stage to share their insights on content planning strategies and the experience they’ve accumulated throughout the production process.
◇ Global Content Planning Starting with “K”
CE Kim Ki-jae delivered a lecture titled “Understanding Netflix’s Global Strategy: Planning in the ‘Local for Local’ Era.” He emphasized that to create content competitive in the global market, it is important to start with Korean stories rather than targeting overseas markets first.
Citing the Netflix series “D.P.” as an example, he explained that stories rooted in the realities and characters of Korean society can resonate across borders. He continued, “The ‘Local for Local’ approach—starting with the stories that Korean creators excel at and the stories that Korean viewers love—is crucial.” He explained that adding new elements to familiar themes can spark viewers’ curiosity and anticipation.
He also outlined how Netflix has broadened the range of themes and genres in Korean content. Beginning with “Yoo Byung-jae: Black Comedy” in 2018, the platform introduced works featuring new themes and bold genre experiments, such as “Kingdom” and “Lessons of Humanity.” Subsequently, through series like “Squid Game” and “D.P.”—which combine the unique characteristics of Korean society with genre-driven entertainment—Netflix introduced distinctly Korean stories to the global market. It has also expanded its content portfolio into variety shows, including “Physical: 100,” “The Black and White Chef: The War of Culinary Classes,” and “Solo Hell.”
Recently, through collaborations with various creators—including director Lee Chang-dong and writers Kim Eun-sook and Im Sang-chun—Netflix has been releasing works across multiple genres, such as the film *Possible Love* and the series *The Glory* and *I’ve Been Completely Duped*.
According to Netflix, from 2016—when it launched in Korea—through 2025, the company has collaborated with more than 80 production companies to produce or co-produce over 200 Korean titles. These productions involved approximately 25,000 cast and crew members, as well as about 70,000 extras and day-laborers. Filming took place in more than 100 cities and towns across the country.
Director Ha Jeong-soo delivering the presentation (Photo: Netflix)
◇From Safe Production Environments to the Use of Cutting-Edge Technology
Director Ha Jeong-soo presented on the topic “Understanding Netflix Productions for Professionals in the Korean Film and Television Industry,” introducing practical aspects of production and examples of technology utilization. He focused on the process of bringing creators’ visions to life through production expertise, technological innovation, and collaboration with partners.
Director Ha explained that Netflix works closely with partners from the early stages of production to ensure creators and production teams can perform at their best in a safe and respectful environment. He noted that the company has established procedures to facilitate smooth and safe collaboration among on-set members, ranging from an onboarding process that shares production procedures and technical standards to “Respect” sessions attended by actors and staff before filming begins.
He also presented case studies for specific productions. For “Physical: 100,” potential risks were identified and mitigated through expert consultation and pre-production simulations. Regarding “Solo Hell,” he noted that the production team is committed to protecting participants by conducting health checkups, psychological evaluations, and background checks, as well as providing psychological counseling even after filming wraps.
The importance of leveraging technology was also emphasized. Director Ha stated, “It is important to embrace and apply new technologies with an open mind,” and introduced technologies designed to enhance the viewing experience, such as 4K HDR video and Dolby Atmos sound. Citing *Okja*—the first Netflix film to feature both Dolby Vision and Dolby Atmos—as an example, he explained that color, texture, and immersive sound convey the creator’s intent and the spaces within the work more vividly.
Using the tug-of-war scene from Season 1 of *Squid Game* as an example, he shared the process of visualizing a scene before filming and designing the scope of set and visual effects (VFX) usage. He explained that deciding in advance on the appropriate production methods—such as backdrops (printed background screens) or LEDs—when rendering the scenery outside a window can reduce the burden of post-production and allow for efficient management of the budget and shooting schedule.
The role of the post-supervisor, who bridges the gap between the filming set and post-production, was also introduced. The post-supervisor is responsible for coordinating communication between the filming set and post-production, as well as discussing budget, schedule, and the quality of the final product with producers and directors. Netflix announced that it has trained 71 post-supervisors to date.
Following the lecture, a panel discussion moderated by pop columnist Kim Tae-hoon took place, focusing on opportunities for emerging creators and the professional growth of production staff. The two speakers discussed the importance of collaborating with new talent, educational programs and exchanges that share on-set experiences, and opportunities to showcase finished works to audiences.
Director Ha stated, “Curiosity is the most important quality for a creator,” adding, “An attitude of constantly asking questions and exploring new possibilities is the driving force behind creating great content.” CE Kim Ki-jae encouraged creators to take on new challenges, emphasizing the importance of continuous reflection and passion for the stories they want to tell.
From left: Pop columnist Kim Tae-hoon, CEO Kim Ki-jae, and Director Ha Jeong-soo (Photo courtesy of Netflix)
◇Moving Beyond Education to the Industry… Achievements in Cultivating Professional Talent
Since 2024, Netflix has been running “Grow Creative,” a training campaign to cultivate production professionals and share production expertise and experience with the domestic industry. Through the “KOCCA × NETFLIX Production Academy,” a collaboration with the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA), the company is also sharing production know-how—from content planning to filming and post-production—with industry professionals and aspiring creators.
There are also cases where training has led to actual employment and participation in productions. The VFX training program, run by Netflix and the Korea Radio Promotion Association for two years and six months from November 2022 to June 2025, saw approximately 330 participants across five cohorts. Approximately 70% of graduates from Cohorts 1 through 4 secured jobs in the industry, and 32 of them were hired by companies that participated in the training program and are Netflix partners. Graduates who found employment contributed to 39 Netflix productions, both domestic and international, including “Squid Game” Seasons 2 and 3 and “I’ve Been Completely Duped.”
Director Ha said, “We launched this training program based on the belief that widely sharing production experience and technical skills will enable more creators to utilize them, and that these capabilities will spread throughout the industry, leading to even better Korean content.”
An official from the Korean Film Council (KOFIC) remarked, “It is meaningful that, through our collaboration with Netflix, we can provide domestic filmmakers with experience in planning and producing global content, as well as practical know-how applicable on set.” The official added, “We hope this training program will serve as an opportunity for filmmakers to broaden their creative horizons, and we will continue to collaborate with the industry to expand educational opportunities that support the growth of the next generation of talent.”
A Netflix spokesperson remarked, “The power to share Korean stories with the world comes from creators and talent on the production floor.” They continued, “We hope that the planning and production insights shared with the Korean Film Council will help domestic filmmakers embark on new creative endeavors, and we will continue to expand opportunities for more talent to learn and grow within Korea’s creative ecosystem.”

The KAFA+ program “Understanding Netflix’s Global Strategy and Production” brought together 120 film and screen industry professionals and aspiring filmmakers.
Jungsu Ha, Director, Production - Korea at Netflix, shared how production teams build on Korea’s strengths to deliver high-quality picture and sound across a range of viewing environments.
Kijae Kim, Manager, Content (Korea) at Netflix, said, “Reaching a global audience is an outcome, not the goal,” urging creators to keep exploring the stories they feel most passionate about telling.
Netflix continues to work with the Korean Film Council (KOFIC) and the Korean Academy of Film Arts (KAFA), two leading institutions in Korean film education, to support the next generation of Korean filmmakers.
On November 10, Netflix Korea’s Jungsu Ha, Director of Production, and Kijae Kim, Content Manager, spoke at the Korean Film Archive Cinematheque as part of “Understanding Netflix’s Global Strategy and Production,” a session in KOFIC’s KAFA+ Film Professionals Education program.
The 120 attendees ranged from film and media students and graduates of training programs to professionals with at least one credit on a feature film or streaming title or at least a year of industry experience. Drawing on their own work developing and producing Korean titles at Netflix, Ha and Kim offered practical advice participants could apply to their own projects, from the first spark of an initial idea to the screen.
Where Korean Stories Begin: Rethinking the Creative Process
In his session, “Understanding Netflix’s Global Strategy: Developing Stories in the ‘Local for Local’ Era,” Kijae Kim explained that Korean titles resonate internationally when creators start from stories rooted in Korea, rather than writing with overseas markets already in mind. Citing 
D.P.
 as an example, Kim said the series showed him how a story grounded in the realities of Korean society and the lives of its characters could resonate across borders. “That’s why ‘local for local’ matters. It means starting with the stories Korean creators tell best and Korean viewers love to watch,” he said. He added that putting a new spin on familiar elements is what sparks viewers’ curiosity and builds anticipation.
Kijae Kim discusses the importance of “Local for Local.”

 brought stories rooted in Korean society to audiences around the world, while unscripted titles including

Bringing Creators’ Visions to the Screen: Safe Production, Technology and Talent Development
Jungsu Ha presents on Netflix production.
Jungsu Ha spoke next, in a talk titled “Understanding Netflix Production for Korea’s Screen Industry Professionals.” He walked through how production expertise, technological innovation and collaboration with partners help bring creators’ visions to the screen. Netflix works closely with partners from the earliest stages of production so that creators and crews can do their best work in an environment where they feel safe and respected. That starts with onboarding, when Netflix shares its production procedures and technical standards with partner companies, and continues through pre-shoot “Respect” sessions, where cast and crew get to know one another and align on how to work safely on set. On 
Physical: 100
, the production team consulted experts and ran simulations ahead of filming to identify and address potential risks. On 
Single’s Inferno
, participants go through health screenings, psychological assessments and background checks before filming, and are offered counseling once it wraps.
“It’s important to approach new technologies with an open mind and put them to use,” Ha said, describing how 4K HDR video and Dolby Atmos sound can elevate the viewing experience. He pointed to 
Okja
, the first Netflix film to use both Dolby Vision and Dolby Atmos, as an example of how rich color, detailed texture and immersive sound can carry a scene’s emotion and sense of place exactly as its creators envisioned it. He used the tug-of-war scene from Season 1 of 
Squid Game
 to show how the team visualizes a sequence before filming even starts, deciding early which elements to build practically on set and which to create with visual effects (VFX). Decisions as small as how to create the view through a window — a printed backdrop versus an LED screen, for instance — can make the finished shot more convincing while helping teams use their budgets and schedules more efficiently. Making those calls early also cuts down on unnecessary post-production work and frees up time to refine the scenes that matter most.
He also spoke about the post-production supervisor’s role in connecting these stages — coordinating between the crew on set and the post-production team, and working closely with producers and directors to track budget, schedule and the quality of the finished work. Netflix has trained 71 post-production supervisors to date.
From left: Pop culture columnist Taehoon Kim, Kijae Kim, and Jungsu Ha during the discussion.
Pop culture columnist Taehoon Kim then moderated a discussion, drawing on questions participants had submitted in advance, about how emerging creators can take on new challenges and how production professionals can grow in their own careers. Ha and Kim talked about working with new talent, passing on on-set experience through training and exchange programs, and the importance of giving creators real opportunities to bring finished work to audiences — closing with words of encouragement and confidence in the next generation of creators.
“Curiosity is the most important quality a creator can have,” Ha said. “The drive to keep asking questions and exploring new possibilities is what makes great work possible.” Kijae Kim emphasized the value of “continuing to think deeply about the stories you want to tell and staying passionate about them,” and encouraged creators to keep taking on new challenges.
“I believe the creators in this room are the future of Korean culture,” Taehoon Kim said in closing. “Ultimately, it’s people who make films and shows, and they help shape the future of Korean culture when they pass their experience and passion on to the next generation. I look forward to seeing the range of work you’ll create.”
Turning Production Expertise into Talent Growth: Partnering with Korea’s Creative Ecosystem
Since 2024, Netflix has run 
Grow Creative
, a training initiative that shares the experience and technical expertise it has built up over the years to help production professionals in Korea grow their own skills. Through its partnership with the Korea Creative Content Agency (KOCCA), Netflix also runs the KOCCA × Netflix Production Academy to share practical knowledge with industry professionals and aspiring creators at every stage of production, from development and filming to post-production.
Specialized training has helped participants find real work in the industry. Between November 2022 and June 2025, around 330 people took part in five cohorts of a VFX training program run by Netflix and the Korea Radio Promotion Association — about 70% of graduates from the first four cohorts went on to jobs in related fields, including 32 who joined Netflix partner companies involved in the program. Those graduates have since contributed to 39 Netflix productions in Korea and abroad, including Seasons 2 and 3 of 
Squid Game
 and 
When Life Gives You Tangerines
. “We started this training because we believed that if we shared our production experience and technical knowledge more widely, more creators could put it to use,” Ha said. “As those skills spread across the industry, they can help creators in Korea make even better films and shows.”
“Working with Netflix has given us the opportunity to share experience in developing and producing titles for global audiences, along with practical knowledge Korean filmmakers can use in their own work,” said a KOFIC representative. “We hope this program helps filmmakers broaden their creative horizons. We’ll continue working with industry partners to expand training opportunities for the next generation of filmmakers.”
“It’s the creators and the people working behind the scenes who bring Korean stories to audiences around the world,” said a Netflix representative. “We hope the development and production experience we shared through this program with KOFIC will help Korean filmmakers try new things. We’ll continue to expand opportunities for more people across Korea’s creative community to learn and grow.”
