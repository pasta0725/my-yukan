Starship Flight 15 Ends With First Ship Tower Catch
Share
Irene Klotz
November 14, 2026
SpaceX returned an orbital Starship upper stage to its launch tower for the first time Nov. 14 to conclude the program's 15
th
 flight test.
Credit: Irene Klotz/Aviation Week
STARBASE, Texas—SpaceX wrapped up a 22.5-hr. flight test of its Starship-Super Heavy launch system Nov. 14 with its first launch tower catch of the reusable orbital space vehicle.
“That was the missing link, the holy grail of spaceflight,” said SpaceX mission commentator Dan Huot.
The first return of Starship marks a significant step in the company’s foundational goal to develop a fully reusable super-heavy-lift orbital launch system with minimal processing time between flights.
As of Nov. 14, SpaceX’s Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rocket fleet have launched 716 times, but each of those required a disposable upper stage. NASA’s now-retired space shuttle orbiters returned for reuse, as did the shuttle’s solid rocket boosters, but their external fuel tanks were discarded during each flight.  
Returning both the Super Heavy booster and Starship upper stage would mark the first time an orbital launch system is fully recovered. SpaceX had hoped to catch both vehicles in its unique launch tower landing  system on Flight 15, but the booster was low on fuel and slightly off course during its return to Starbase after launch, triggering the rocket to autonomously abort the tower landing attempt. The Super Heavy diverted to a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico 6.5 min. after liftoff at 5:47 p.m. EST (4:47 p.m. local time) Nov. 13.
“Slight underperformance of engines on this flight is the reason,” SpaceX founder, CEO and chief engineer Elon Musk wrote on X Nov. 14. “The booster is meant to land with tiny residuals, so the solution is to improve engine performance and performance consistency—underway with Raptor Version 3.2.”
In addition, the booster’s flight software will be adjusted to complete its 33-engine ascent burn about 1 sec. earlier to ensure there is enough propellant for the boostback and landing burns, Musk added. 
“These early ships and boosters are already outdated by improved designs in production or on the test stand, so this booster loss does not change our timeline to reach high volume, fully reusable Starship flights,” Musk said. “We have the next booster almost ready for flight, but losing the tower or launch mount would potentially cost us 2-3 months.”
SpaceX landed Super Heavy Version 1 boosters three times previously, during the fifth, seventh and eighth flights in 2024-25, and had hoped to land its first Super Heavy Version 3 (V3) booster as part of Flight 15.
After separating from the Super Heavy and heading into orbit, the Starship deployed 26 next-generation Starlink satellites. It then circled Earth 14 times—completing SpaceX’s longest Starship mission to date. At 3:22 p.m. EST, Starship fired a single Raptor engine for 10 seconds to slow the ship and depart from its 171-mi.-high perch to begin a 56-min. glide back to the launch site, located on the southern tip of Texas. 
Approaching from the west, the Starship headed toward the Texas Rio Grande Valley, passing north of South Padre Island and out over the Gulf of Mexico before making a final turn south toward the launch site. 
Shortly before reaching the tower, Starship ignited its three sea-level Raptor engines, flipped itself into a vertical position and stowed its flaps. The ship than shut down one engine and maneuvered itself between the tower's outstretched mechanical arms. Finally, with just a single Raptor burning, Starship came to a near-hover over the launch mount as the arms quickly closed to catch the ship on two fittings just below the forward flaps, leaving it suspended in midair. 
Once Starship registered contact with the tower arms, the final Raptor shut down, marking the end of the program’s 15
th
 flight test. “It was almost magic,” NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman wrote on X. “Congratulations to Elon Musk and the SpaceX team.”
SpaceX is working on a version of Starship to ferry astronauts to and from the surface of the Moon as early as 2028.
