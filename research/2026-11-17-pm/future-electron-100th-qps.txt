Rocket Lab just hit the century mark.
An 
Electron
 rocket topped with a private Japanese Earth-observing satellite lifted off from 
Rocket Lab's
 New Zealand site on Saturday (Nov. 14) at 12:30 a.m. EST (0530 GMT; 6:30 p.m. local New Zealand time).
The mission, which Rocket Lab called "The Mountain God Rises," was a big one: It marked the 100th launch to date for the California-based company.
A Rocket Lab Electron rocket launches from New Zealand on Nov. 14, 2026 on "The Mountain God Rises," the 100th mission for the company to date. 
(Image credit: Rocket Lab)
Rocket Lab launched for the first time in May 2017, sending the brand-new Electron up on a test flight from New Zealand. That effort failed, but Electron bounced back on its second-ever liftoff, delivering several cubesats to 
low Earth orbit
 (LEO) in January 2018.
In the years since, the 59-foot-tall (18 meters) Electron has become one of the most prolific and reliable 
rockets
 in the world, carrying small payloads to the final frontier for a variety of customers.
And it hit the century mark on Saturday in record-breaking fashion: No commercially developed rocket has gotten there faster, not even SpaceX's workhorse 
Falcon 9
, according to Rocket Lab founder and CEO Peter Beck.
"It's an unprecedented milestone for a commercial small-lift orbital rocket, and a threshold rarely crossed in modern spaceflight," Beck said on Monday (Nov. 9), during Rocket Lab's third-quarter earnings call with investors. "But what matters most to us and our customers is that we have achieved a 96% reliability rate across 99 launches."
The first stage of Rocket Lab's Electron rocket falls back to Earth during "The Mountain God Rises" mission on Nov. 14, 2026. 
(Image credit: Rocket Lab)
Electron met the moment in style on Saturday morning, thanks to some fancy livery.
"Black rocket 🤝 Golden milestone. Electron is all dressed up for launch 100," Rocket Lab said via X on Tuesday (Nov. 10), in 
a post
 that shared a photo of the specially painted first stage.
Not all of Rocket Lab's missions have gone orbital, it should be noted: A suborbital variant of Electron known as 
HASTE
, which customers use to test technologies in a hypersonic flight environment, has nine missions under its belt to date.
The Electron rocket that flew Rocket Lab's 100th mission sported some special livery. 
(Image credit: Rocket Lab)
"The Mountain God Rises" was Rocket Lab's 11th launch to date for the Tokyo-based company iQPS, which is building a constellation of three dozen synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellites in LEO. And all went to plan on the mission: Electron's upper "kick stage" deployed a single iQPS spacecraft 357 miles (575 kilometers) above Earth.
SAR satellites can see through cloud cover and image our planet at night as well as during the day, offering key advantages over spacecraft that are optimized to view in optical wavelengths.

Rocket Lab Hits 100 Electron Launches, Fastest Commercial Rocket to Reach the Milestone
November 14, 2026
PDF Version
LONG BEACH, Calif., Nov.  14, 2026  (GLOBE NEWSWIRE) -- 
Rocket Lab Corporation
 (Nasdaq: RKLB), a global leader in launch services and space systems, today announced mission success for its 100
th
 Electron launch, setting a new record for reaching 100 launches faster than any other commercially developed rocket.
“The Mountain God Rises” mission launched from Rocket Lab Launch Complex 1 in New Zealand at 6:30 p.m. on November 14, 2026, deploying the YAMATSUMI-II synthetic aperture radar (SAR) Earth-imaging satellite to a 575km low Earth orbit for the Institute for Q-shu Pioneers of Space, Inc. (
iQPS
). This mission marks Rocket Lab’s 11
th
 Electron launch for iQPS with 100% mission success since 2023. iQPS has booked seven more dedicated Electron launches through 2030 to complete its satellite constellation.
Reaching 100 launches in under a decade is a major milestone for commercial spaceflight. What started as a vision to open up access to space with the world’s first carbon-composite rocket has grown into an unmatched launch cadence across the small-launch industry.
Rocket Lab founder and CEO, Sir Peter Beck, says: “Few rockets reach 100 launches in their total operational lifetime. Not only has Electron now reached 100 launches, but it reached the milestone faster than any other commercially developed rocket. That record pace comes down to the relentless dedication of the Rocket Lab team, and I want to congratulate everyone behind Electron. This is the team that pioneered 3D-printed rocket engines, built the world’s first carbon-composite orbital rocket, launched back-to-back from two hemispheres, and shattered responsive space records along the way, putting Electron in a league of its own. To our customers who have trusted us with their spacecraft, many of them across multiple launches, thank you. The next 100 Electrons are already booked so we’re only accelerating from here.”
Since its first launch in 2017, Electron has deployed hundreds of satellites for commercial constellation operators, national security agencies, and scientific institutions from launch pads in New Zealand and Virginia. Backed by vertically integrated manufacturing and a packed manifest extending well into the next decade, Electron continues to deliver access to orbit while laying the operational foundation for Rocket Lab’s next medium-lift rocket, Neutron.

ロケット・ラボは、日本時間11月14日午後2時30分（世界時5時30分、現地時間午後6時30分）、ニュージーランド北島のマヒア半島にある自社の発射場から、エレクトロンを打ち上げました。ミッション名は「The Mountain God Rises（山の神が昇る）」。打ち上げの約50分後に衛星を切りはなし、その約8分後にQPS研究所が衛星との最初の通信に成功して、機器が正常に動いていることを確かめました。
エレクトロンとはどんなロケット？
高さ約18mの小さなロケットで、地球のまわりの低い軌道に運べる重さは約300kgまで。大きな衛星ではなく、小型衛星を1機ずつ、好きなタイミングで届けるのが得意です。
機体の大部分が炭素繊維でできていて、エンジン「ラザフォード」は3Dプリンターで作られ、電池で動くポンプで燃料を送るという、めずらしいしくみです。
最初の打ち上げは2017年5月で、このときは軌道に届きませんでした。2018年1月の2回目で初めて衛星を軌道に入れ、その後、何度かの失敗をのりこえて打ち上げを重ねてきました。100回という数には、エレクトロンをもとにした弾道飛行（軌道に入らない試験飛行）用の「HASTE」の打ち上げもふくまれます。
ロケット・ラボは、民間が開発したロケットとしては最も速く100回に届いたとしています。
QPS研究所の衛星はなにをする？
QPS研究所は、九州大学の研究をもとに生まれた福岡の会社です。打ち上げたのは「SAR（合成開口レーダー）」という種類の衛星で、地上に電波を当て、はね返ってきた電波から地面の画像を作ります。光ではなく電波を使うので、夜でも、雲があっても地上を観測できます。QPS研究所はアンテナを小さくたたんで打ち上げ、宇宙で大きく広げる方式で、衛星を軽く小さくしています。
QPS研究所の衛星をエレクトロンで打ち上げるのは今回で11回目で、11月3日の99回目の打ち上げもQPS研究所の衛星（QPS-SAR20号機）でした。報道によると、運用中の衛星はこれで13機になり、将来は36機をそろえて、同じ場所を10〜40分に1回観測できるようにすることを目指しています。19号機は今後、アンテナを広げる作業などを進め、最初の画像の撮影を目指します。

Rocket Lab Electron completed its 100th launch and placed a Japanese radar-imaging satellite into orbit from New Zealand on Nov. 14, 2026, giving the company a new marker of launch cadence. Rocket Lab identified the mission as “The Mountain God Rises” and said it deployed YAMATSUMI-II for the Institute for Q-shu Pioneers of Space, or iQPS.
The company reported liftoff from Launch Complex 1 at 6:30 p.m. local time and deployment to an approximately 575-kilometer orbit. The announcement calls Electron the fastest commercially developed rocket to reach 100 launches. That is Rocket Lab's stated comparative claim; the count covers its Electron launch family and must not be confused with 100 identical orbital satellite-delivery missions.

Rocket Lab Electron delivered a radar satellite on flight 100
YAMATSUMI-II carries synthetic aperture radar, a sensing technology that transmits radio waves and processes the return signal to produce images. Unlike conventional optical imagery, radar can provide useful observations at night and through many cloud conditions. That makes it relevant to disaster monitoring, maritime activity and other commercial Earth-observation services, although the customer—not the launch provider—determines the satellite's operations and product availability.
Rocket Lab said this was its 11th Electron launch for iQPS since 2023. The company also described a record of mission success on that customer campaign. These are mission-provider figures in its November release, not an independently audited claim that every future satellite will meet its orbital or imaging objectives.
What 100 launches says about small-launch demand
An operator that flies repeatedly must coordinate production, range access, payload integration, testing and launch operations. Reaching a triple-digit flight count therefore says something about the continuity of Electron's launch program. It does not, by itself, establish a perfect reliability record or disclose the financial profitability of the rocket. Customers still assess cost, orbit, schedule and performance for each specific mission.
Rocket Lab traces Electron's operational history to 2017. It operates launch sites in New Zealand and the United States, allowing customers to consider different launch locations and regulatory arrangements. The November milestone comes while the company also develops Neutron, a larger rocket aimed at a different market. Electron's accumulated launches are an operating track record; Neutron's future capabilities remain separate development objectives.
iQPS has booked seven more dedicated Electron missions
Rocket Lab says iQPS has booked seven additional dedicated Electron launches extending through 2030. The Japanese company is building a radar-imaging constellation, so placing spacecraft into particular orbits at particular times is part of a broader deployment schedule. A booking for future launches is not evidence those satellites are already operating or that any specific launch date is fixed.
The latest mission placed one identified satellite into orbit, rather than launching a general rideshare manifest of unrelated customer payloads. Dedicated access can help a constellation operator specify its mission timing and orbital requirements. Whether the next seven flights proceed as planned depends on spacecraft readiness, licensing, weather and launch preparation—conditions not resolved by announcing the contracts.
The milestone sits beside other orbital-launch developments
The wider launch market includes much larger rockets designed for different payloads and recovery concepts. For example, TAD's coverage of the 
Starship Flight 15 orbital test
 concerns a high-capacity spacecraft and booster system rather than Electron's small-satellite service. Comparing the two solely by flight count would obscure their markedly different missions and design targets.
Rocket Lab's statement also notes that Electron has supported government and scientific payloads as well as commercial constellations. That customer diversity matters, but the November announcement does not provide a breakdown of contracted revenue, manufacturing margins or production lead times. Its immediate verified result is successful deployment of the iQPS payload and a cumulative flight milestone.
FAQ: Rocket Lab Electron
What did Rocket Lab Electron launch on its 100th flight?
It carried YAMATSUMI-II, an iQPS synthetic aperture radar satellite, to a roughly 575-kilometer orbit from New Zealand on Nov. 14.
Does the number mean 100 satellites were launched?
No. It refers to the stated number of launches in the Electron program, not a count of satellites deployed on this particular mission.
Is the Neutron rocket included in this milestone?
No. The November announcement concerns Electron. Neutron is a separate medium-lift program being developed by Rocket Lab.
Looking Ahead
Rocket Lab says seven further iQPS launches are booked through 2030, providing a visible customer pipeline. Dates for individual future missions require fresh confirmation, and the company's plans for Neutron should be judged against separate testing and flight milestones.

Rocket Lab is the primary launcher for iQPS’ commercial synthetic aperture radar (SAR) constellation, which is being developed and deployed at a rapid pace to provide high resolution and near-real time SAR imagery to its global users.
MILESTONE MISSION
Electron reaching 100 launches is an unprecedented milestone for a commercial small-lift orbital rocket and a threshold rarely crossed in modern spaceflight. Over its 99 missions to date, Electron has rewritten the playbook when it comes to launch for small satellites, and delivering an Earth-observation satellite for iQPS to space on our 100th launch reflects the very mission that started it all: opening up access to space to improve life on Earth.
Since its first launch in 2017, Electron has become one of the world’s most frequently launched rockets and has redefined access to space for small satellites. Electron has achieved a string of world-firsts and industry-leading achievements along the way including:
The first carbon composite orbital launch vehicle.
Powered by the first 3D-printed orbital rocket engine, Rutherford, which is also the first rocket engine to use electric fuel pumps instead of traditional gas turbines.
Launched from two hemispheres. Rocket Lab operates launch pads in Virginia, USA, as well as New Zealand, where the company built the world’s first private orbital launch complex.
Achieved a new responsive space record earlier this year when Electron was launched with just 16 hours and 42 minutes notice for the U.S. Space Force.
On track to be the world’s first commercial small-lift orbital rocket to reach 100 launches.
