United Launch Alliance's Vulcan Centaur rocket suffered an anomaly during its launch today (Nov. 15), resulting in the loss of 40 Amazon internet satellites.
The 
Vulcan Centaur
 lifted off from Florida's 
Cape Canaveral Space Force Station
 today at 12:40 p.m. ET (1740 GMT), hauling 40 
satellites
 for the Amazon Leo broadband constellation toward 
low Earth orbit
 (LEO). But they never got there.
"Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit," ULA said in an 
update on X today
, 50 minutes after launch. "Since the anomaly just occurred, we don’t have any specific data yet to understand what occurred today."
A United Launch Alliance Vulcan Centaur launches 40 Amazon internet satellites from Florida on Nov. 15, 2026. 
(Image credit: ULA)
Today's launch was supposed to be a bounce-back mission for the Vulcan Centaur, the successor to ULA's workhorse 
Atlas V
 rocket.
Vulcan debuted 
in January 2024
 and had four total flights under its belt before today. On two of those missions — its second, in 
October 2024
, and its fourth, in 
February of this year
 — the rocket experienced an issue with one of its solid rocket boosters (SRBs).
In both cases, an SRB nozzle burned through and fell off about 30 seconds after launch. Vulcan Centaur overcame the problem on both launches, successfully delivering its payloads to their designated orbits. But the 
U.S. Space Force
 was concerned enough that it decided to 
pause national security launches
 on Vulcan until ULA addressed the problem. (Vulcan's third and fourth launches were Space Force missions.)
ULA worked hard on that front and got Vulcan ready to return to flight, which finally occurred today after nine months..
"The integrated team has made great strides in improving our understanding of the SRB anomaly that occurred on the previous Vulcan flight and have identified several potential contributors, which have been mitigated through design and process improvements to the nozzle," the company said in an 
Oct. 28 update
.
That nozzle work apparently paid off: The SRBs appeared to perform as expected during today's launch. But a different, more serious problem cropped up today, and Vulcan Centaur could not overcome it.
The Vulcan Centaur rises into the sky on Nov. 15, 2026. 
(Image credit: Vulcan Centaur)
Today's launch was supposed to help build out Amazon's broadband constellation in LEO, which was previously called Project Kuiper but is now known as Amazon Leo. It will eventually compete with SpaceX's 
Starlink
 network, which has been up and running for several years.
Amazon Leo will consist of more than 3,200 satellites. Before today, about 400 of them had reached space on 15 launches, which were conducted by three different rockets — the Atlas V, SpaceX's 
Falcon 9
 and Arianespace's 
Ariane 6
. Today's launch was the first such mission for the Vulcan Centaur, which explains ULA's name for the launch — Vulcan Leo 1.
The Amazon Leo launch was part of a very busy stretch in spaceflight. SpaceX launched its 
Starship
 megarocket 
on Friday
 (Nov. 13), on a 
test mission
 that featured the 
first-ever catch
 of the vehicle's Ship upper stage. And Rocket Lab 
launched its 100th-ever mission
 early Saturday morning (Nov. 14), sending a private Japanese Earth-observing satellite to orbit.

ULA suffers first outright failure with loss of Vulcan rocket carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites
William Harwood
November 15, 2026
7:16 p.m.
A United Launch Alliance Vulcan, equipped with six solid rocket boosters, lifts off from Cape Canaveral's pad 41. A failure 10 minutes into flight resulted in the loss of 40 Amazon Leo satellites. Image: Michael Cain/Spaceflight Now
In a major setback for United Launch Alliance, the company’s new heavy-lift Vulcan rocket carrying a batch of Amazon Leo internet satellites, malfunctioned during the climb to space Sunday, dooming the payload and throwing a costly wrench into the launch schedule of both companies.
“Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 (at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station) suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit,” ULA said in a statement some 50 minutes after liftoff.
“We will work closely with our customer and other appropriate organizations to find the cause, determine the appropriate corrective action and return to flight as quickly as possible.”
It was the first outright failure of a United Launch Alliance rocket since the company, a partnership between Boeing and Lockheed Martin, was formed in 2006.
It wasn’t immediately known what went wrong, but the Vulcan, the rocket replacing ULA’s fleet of Atlas 5 rockets, will face a potentially significant grounding while engineers review telemetry and implement fixes for whatever caused the mishap.
The six solid rocket booster peel away from the Vulcan first stage booster one minute 35 seconds into flight. Photo: Michael Cain/Spaceflight Now.
It was only the fifth flight of a Vulcan rocket and the first in nine months as ULA and booster-supplier Northrop Grumman worked to resolve problems with nozzles used by strap-on solid-fuel boosters that marred two earlier flights.
The solids appeared to work normally Sunday, helping boost the rocket out of the dense lower atmosphere before burning out and falling away. The two methane-burning BE-4 engines powering the Vulcan’s first stage then continued the climb toward orbit, but they appeared to fire longer than expected based on ULA’s pre-launch timeline.
After stage separation, the Vulcan’s hydrogen-fueled Centaur upper stage engines took over for a planned 12-minute 15-second burn. According to ULA’s update, something went wrong during that Centaur firing, but no additional details were immediately provided.
Sunday’s launch was the first of 38 Vulcan flights purchased by Amazon to launch its Leo satellites, designed to compete head-to-head with Elon Musk’s Starlinks and other space-based internet providers. ULA already has launched eight LEO flights using Atlas 5 rockets.
Amazon also has booked 24 Leo launches aboard Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket, 24 using the European Ariane 6, and 13 aboard SpaceX’s Falcon 9s. More than 3,300 Leo satellites will be needed to complete the constellation. Sunday’s flight would have pushed the total number launched to date to 436.
But it was not to be.
Instead, Amazon Leo will now have to wait on ULA’s failure investigation to run its course before trying again with a Vulcan. The company also is waiting for Jeff Bezos-owned Blue Origin to get its New Glenn rocket flying again after a catastrophic on-pad explosion last May.
That failure was caused by problems with an oxygen valve in one of the New Glenn’s seven BE-4 first stage engines. The BE-4, also built by Blue Origin, powers the Vulcan’s first stage, but the engines used Sunday had been retrofitted with a fix by Blue Origin engineers.
While the cause of the failure is not yet publicly known, “ULA is committed to full transparency with our customers, and they will have visibility into the investigation process, which utilizes rigorous methodology and corrective action,” the company said.
NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman promised assistance if needed.
“Appreciate you quickly keeping the public and your partners informed,” he wrote on the social media platform X. “The team at @NASA is available to assist in all matters related to the investigation and remediation effort. Artemis, Moon Base, and the nation need ULA. We are in your corner and here to help.”
The Vulcan rocket thunders away from Florida’s space coast on Nov. 15, 2026. Image: Michael Cain/Spaceflight Now.
The 214-foot-tall Vulcan blasted off from pad 41 at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 12:40 p.m. EST, thundering away on a northeasterly trajectory atop 3.8 million pounds of thrust from six Northrop Grummon strap-on solid-fuel boosters and two methane-burning BE-4s.
A launch attempt Friday was scrubbed after problems knocked out a gaseous nitrogen plant that serves multiple launch providers. Another delay was ordered Saturday because of network problems between the Vulcan and ground systems.
On Sunday, launch controllers called a hold at the T-minus 20-second mark because of an unexplained technical issue with the rocket. Re-targeting the end of the launch window, ULA resolved the problem and the rocket roared to life and climbed away at the end of the launch window.
It’s not yet known if the problem that interrupted the countdown played any role in the subsequent failure.
In any case, the strap-on boosters helped propel the rocket out of the dense lower atmosphere, burning out and falling away about a minute and 40 seconds after liftoff. But the first stage appeared to burn longer than called for in the company’s pre-launch timeline.
The first stage also appeared to separate from the rocket’s hydrogen-fueled Centaur upper stage later than expected and telemetry-driven animation showed the Centaur making unusual orientation adjustments early in the burn.
ULA’s launch commentators, however, did not mention any problems and the launch broadcast came to an end without confirming whether the Centaur’s first planned engine firing went for the full duration.
ULA spent months working to resolve problems with the strap-on booster nozzles that occurred during two earlier flights, one in 2024 and the other this past February. While ULA was free to press ahead with commercial flights, the U.S. Space Force put Vulcan launches of national security payloads on hold pending additional analysis.
“We’ve identified three credible causes (of the nozzle issue), and we have taken positive steps to mitigate all of those,” ULA CEO Mark Peller told Aviation Week & Space Technology magazine in a pre-flight interview.
The boosters appeared to work normally Sunday.
As for the BE-4 engines, Peller said Blue Origin developed a “design improvement” to resolve the valve issue and carried out a “retrofit” on the engines installed on the Vulcan Leo 1 rocket.
“They were down at our facilities probably a couple months ago and implemented that retrofit kit on the engines on this upcoming mission,” Peller said. “And they’re working with us to work through our inventory of engines.”

A 
United Launch Alliance 
Vulcan rocket suffered an anomaly during its first launch for the 
Amazon
 Leo constellation on Sunday, in a return to flight mission after months out of service. The Vulcan rocket was carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites, the most in one single launch so far for the constellation. 
The mission took off at 12:40 p.m. ET on Sunday from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. The launch broadcast showed the vehicle cleaning the pad and all solid rocket boosters jettisoned about a minute and a half into the mission. 
The booster engine successfully cut off, and the upper stage engine ignited successfully, about five minutes into the mission. The launch broadcast confirmed the payload fairing was jettisoned. 
The mission seemed to be on track during the last update around 9 minutes and 30 seconds into the mission, but the launch broadcast ended before announcing the anomaly.
ULA said in a statement Sunday afternoon that the Vulcan vehicle “suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit.” This is the first full launch failure for ULA.
ULA has not shared further details on the anomaly at this time. The next milestone was set to be Centaur first main engine cut off, at around 17 minutes into the mission. 
“ULA exists to provide assured access to space for our nation and for the customers who depend on us. That purpose is why we take on some of the hardest missions in spaceflight, and why we hold ourselves to the standard of mission success. Today, we did not meet that standard,” the company said in a statement. “We will work closely with our customer and other appropriate organizations to find the cause, determine the appropriate corrective action, and return to flight as quickly as possible.”
Sunday’s launch was delayed twice — it was delayed from Friday due to a power interruption in the air liquide gaseous nitrogen (GN2) plant, then delayed from Saturday due to a network communication issue between the ground equipment and the launch vehicle. 
The Vulcan rocket has been out of service since February, when it experienced an anomaly with the solid rocket motors built by Northrop Grumman. The February launch for the U.S. Space Force as a customer was successful, but the Space Force 
suspended Vulcan launches
 for the National Security Space Launch (NSSL) program to fix the anomaly. 
ULA recently stated that after an investigation with its team, Northrop Grumman, the Space Force, and government agencies, changes to the nozzle were made and fully tested. 
“The preliminary data shows the GEM 63XL boosters performed as expected and successfully separated from the Vulcan launch vehicle before today’s anomaly,” Northrop Grumman said in a statement. 
This was the fifth launch for the rocket, which had its 
debut mission in early 2024
. 
The mission was carrying 40 Amazon Leo satellites — the largest launch yet for Amazon Leo. The rocket was configured in the Vulcan VC6L variant with the Centaur V shorter, optimized version of the upper stage and six side-mounted solid rocket boosters. 
It was the first of 38 planned Vulcan rocket launches for Amazon, following eight missions for Amazon Leo with the Atlas V rocket. 
Amazon called ULA a “trusted partner” in a statement after the failure. “There is risk with any launch, and that’s especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01. ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they’ll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan,” Amazon said. 
In a pre-taped interview during the launch broadcast, Amazon Leo Launch Systems Director Melissa Wuerl said Vulcan would be a “gamechanger” and a “workhorse” for the constellation.
“This is meant to fly routinely, it’s meant to fly at a very high cadence,” Wuerl said. “We’re launching close to 1,500 satellites on Vulcan, which is nearly half of our constellation. This is a vehicle that’s going to make a difference for us.”

Vulcan lifted off Sunday for the first time in nine months, carrying 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites. The rocket failed about 10 minutes into flight. (United Launch Alliance Photo)
A United Launch Alliance Vulcan rocket malfunctioned about 10 minutes after liftoff Sunday and failed to reach orbit, losing 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the Seattle company’s planned challenger to SpaceX’s Starlink internet service.
It was ULA’s first total mission failure since the Boeing–Lockheed Martin joint venture was formed in 2006, 
Spaceflight Now reported
. The flight was Vulcan’s first in nine months, and the first of 38 Vulcan launches that Amazon has purchased.
ULA said it doesn’t yet have specific data on what went wrong, and that it will work with Amazon and others to find the cause and return to flight as quickly as possible.
Amazon Leo 
said in a post on X
 that “there is risk with any launch, and that’s especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01.” It added, “ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they’ll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan.”
The rocket lifted off from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station at 12:40 p.m. ET (9:40 a.m. PT), after two days of delays and a countdown hold with 20 seconds left on the clock to resolve a technical issue. It isn’t known whether that issue played any role.
The rocket’s six solid rocket boosters, made by Northrop Grumman and the source of problems on two earlier Vulcan flights, appeared to perform normally and burned out less than two minutes after liftoff, 
according to Space.com
 and Spaceflight Now.
The first stage, powered by two BE-4 engines from Jeff Bezos’ Kent, Wash.-based Blue Origin, fired from liftoff before separating from the Centaur upper stage, which carries the payload the rest of the way to orbit.
The anomaly came about 10 minutes into the flight, during the Centaur’s burn, according to Spaceflight Now. The Centaur is powered by two RL10 engines made by L3Harris.
Spaceflight Now also reported that the first stage appeared to burn longer than planned, and that a telemetry-driven animation showed the Centaur making unusual orientation adjustments early in its burn. ULA hasn’t identified the cause.
Impact on Amazon Leo:
 Before Sunday, Amazon had launched 396 satellites for its Redmond-based satellite internet network, formerly known as Project Kuiper, on 14 missions since April 2025. Its last successful launch was in July, when ULA flew 
the last of eight Leo missions
 on its Atlas 5, the older rocket that Vulcan is replacing.
The larger Vulcan carried 40 satellites on Sunday, compared with 29 on the last Atlas 5 launch, and is slated to carry more than 1,500 of Amazon’s first-generation satellites, more than any other rocket in the program.
Melissa Wuerl, Amazon Leo’s director of launch systems, called it the program’s “primary workhorse over the next several years” in 
a preview of the mission
. Amazon had said the second Vulcan mission could fly within weeks of the first. That flight now has to wait for ULA’s investigation.
Amazon also 
has 24 launches reserved
 on Blue Origin’s New Glenn, which has been grounded since a 
May ground-test explosion
, and has booked others on Arianespace’s Ariane 6 and SpaceX’s Falcon 9.
In June, the Federal Communications Commission gave Amazon permission to 
keep deploying satellites
 even if it missed its July 30 deadline to put 1,616 into service. Satellites that weren’t operational by then temporarily lose priority in key spectrum bands.
Amazon still has until July 30, 2029, to deploy and operate all 3,232 first-generation satellites. Amazon has said it plans an initial rollout of commercial internet service this year.
Vulcan’s track record:
 Sunday’s mission was the fifth for Vulcan, which debuted in January 2024, and the first with six solid boosters. Boosters suffered nozzle failures on the second flight in October 2024 and on a national security mission in February. Both reached orbit, but the Space Force paused Vulcan national security launches after February.
ULA flew Sunday’s mission with an improved nozzle design. The rocket’s BE-4 engines had also been retrofitted to fix the oxygen valve problem behind the New Glenn explosion in May, ULA CEO Mark Peller said before the flight, 
CBS News reported
.
ULA is also under new leadership. Longtime CEO Tory Bruno 
left last December for Blue Origin
, and Peller, who led Vulcan’s development, was named CEO in August.
Amazon’s next scheduled Leo launch is an Ariane 6 mission carrying 36 satellites, targeted for Nov. 23 at the earliest.
