Microsoft Traces Global 365 Outage to a July Fix That Its Own Failover Spread    
            A latent gateway defect, a routine Teams failover and reduced capacity combined to trigger cascading failures across Microsoft 365’s shared networking infrastructure.        
                Published on Nov 16, 2026            
                            Written by
                                    Alessandro Mascellino                                
Microsoft published a 
preliminary post-incident report
 on Nov. 16 into 
the outage
 that began on Nov. 12, which left users unable to reach Exchange Online mailboxes and hit many other Microsoft 365 services. It came a day after Microsoft confirmed to Expert Insights that it had 
rolled back a spam rule change
 that had blocked emails to Outlook.com.
The report is available in the Microsoft 365 admin center under MO1550821 and EX1550543. Charles Lamanna, Microsoft’s executive vice president of Copilot, Agents and Platform, posted an 
abridged version on X
. Microsoft said its final review was due later this week.
Three conditions converged during peak US traffic. A latent defect came from a fix for Microsoft 365 networking gateway servers, whose deployment began in July. It ran worldwide for more than three months without observed issues. Microsoft said neither the defect nor the fix applied to broader Windows deployments.
Lock contention in an internal process left gateway servers overwhelmed under certain loads. Testing missed it because labs used different traffic and hardware mixes and focused on weaker machines with fewer cores. The problem was worse on newer machines with more cores.
Failover and Rerouting Turned a Latent Defect Into a Cascade
About 15 hours before the outage, Teams failed over from an alternative gateway to the primary gateway shared with Exchange Online. Microsoft said this type of failover was part of normal operations, but it increased load and changed traffic patterns.
An unrelated issue the previous day had already taken nearly 14% of gateway capacity out of service. On its own, that loss had no impact.
Together, the conditions triggered the defect. Automatic rerouting sent traffic to healthy endpoints, spreading the same load across fewer servers and causing a cascading failure. In under two hours, the defect had hit the fleet. At the lowest point, more than 80% of servers were unresponsive.
For context, the gateway is built from thousands of “capacity units,” shards of capacity, hardware and networking grouped into regions, with bulkheads between them meant to contain failures. Microsoft said a separate issue, which it did not describe, kept those bulkheads from stopping the cascade, letting it spread across the gateway infrastructure globally.
Teams, OneDrive, SharePoint and Office apps failed out of the gateway and recovered earlier, with Teams chats and meetings working for most of the outage. Exchange Online could not fail out of the gateway, and Microsoft did not say why.
Recovery Systems Could Not Reverse the Failure Quickly
Waiting traffic overwhelmed restarted servers, and automated recovery was not designed for such a broad unresponsive state. Because the defect had been deployed months earlier, normal rollback, flighting and feature flags could not quickly reverse it.
Engineers built an update restoring the previous code path, then took groups of servers out of service, patched and restarted them. At times, they applied the process to 50% or more of a region at once.
During the outage, Microsoft paused all deployments, and on Nov. 12 its status account 
said
 it had “not identified any recent change correlated with the start of impact.” It extended the email timeout to 48 hours on Nov. 13 to preserve delayed mail, and said on Nov. 14 that service had been restored to the remaining impacted customers and regions.

What happened
Microsoft 365 email was down for over a day
 last week, and Microsoft’s preliminary report says three separate problems lined up to cause it. Customers couldn’t open their mailbox in Outlook, Outlook on the web or the mail app on their phone. The little email that did get through was often late. Teams, SharePoint, OneDrive, Copilot and other services had problems too, mostly in the parts that rely on 
Exchange Online
 (Microsoft’s email service). Teams chats and meetings kept working for most people.
As with many modern system failures, there wasn’t a single cause. Instead, it was a “sleeper” bug that had been sitting on Microsoft’s systems for over three months before a sequence of events triggered the bug which then cascaded through the servers.
The hidden bug in front door servers
The root cause was a 
hidden bug
 on Microsoft’s “front door” servers (Microsoft calls them the 
networking gateway
). Connections to Exchange Online and many other Microsoft 365 services pass through these servers. In July, Microsoft rolled out a Windows fix just for these servers, and it ran without trouble for more than three months. But under heavy load on newer servers with lots of processor cores, the fix made the machines seize up. Microsoft’s lab testing used less powerful hardware with fewer cores, and different traffic, so it never found the problem.
The domino effect
On the day, two more things piled on. About 15 hours before the outage, Teams traffic was automatically moved onto the same gateway Exchange uses, which is a routine move. And nearly 14% of server capacity was already out of action because of an unrelated fault the day before.
When US business hours hit their peak, servers started locking up. Microsoft’s automatic safety net did what it always does and moved the traffic to the servers that were still healthy. Those servers then locked up too.
It was a 
domino effect
. In under two hours, more than 80% of the servers had stopped responding. The barriers that should stop a failure in one region from spreading to others didn’t work, for a reason Microsoft hasn’t explained yet.
Recovery was slow because the waiting traffic swamped every server the moment it was restarted. Microsoft’s usual “undo” tools didn’t help either, because the faulty change was months old. At 7:20 AM UTC Friday, engineers started rolling out a fix that put the code back to its earlier working state. They brought servers back in batches, starting with Asia Pacific and then Europe. Webmail was back to normal worldwide by 2:17 PM UTC. Microsoft didn’t declare the incident over until 4:10 PM UTC Friday.
What does this mean for me?
Email wasn’t lost.
 Microsoft gave incoming messages extra time to be delivered, so they weren’t bounced back as undeliverable.
Outgoing messages from desktop Outlook should have stayed in the Outbox until a connection was reestablished. Outlook mobile apps may have failed to send.  If you sent something important on Thursday or Friday and haven’t heard back, it’s worth checking with the recipient.
There’s nothing to fix on your own PC, Mac or device.
 The bug was in a Windows fix made only for Microsoft’s gateway servers.
The uncomfortable lesson:
 Exchange Online can’t switch away from this gateway “for scale reasons.” So when that one front door jams, email stops for nearly everyone.

Root Cause
The incident on Thursday, November 12th, 2026, was caused by three main factors that together caused a global outage for the networking gateway that fronts many Microsoft 365 services.
First, a latent defect caused Windows servers to become overwhelmed and unresponsive under certain load conditions. This was due to lock contention inside an internal Windows process which caused the machine’s resources (CPU & memory) to be exhausted. This defect was the result of a fix that was prepared specifically for Microsoft 365 networking gateway servers; neither the underlying defect nor the fix applies to broader Windows deployments.
The initial change in behavior first started deployment in July of this year. The update went through safe deployment and saturated globally over several weeks, during which time no issues were observed. For more than three months, the change was deployed worldwide without impact.
Our normal load testing did not detect the latent issue because the traffic mix and SKUs used in our labs. The contention issue is worsened on machines with more cores and newer hardware; whereas our load testing tended to focus on weaker hardware with fewer cores.
Second, a surge in load increased the likelihood for the networking gateways to encounter the contention. Within Microsoft 365, we maintain two independent and heterogenous network gateway systems designed for isolation and high availability. This allows many Microsoft 365 services to fail to/from them during outages or disruptions. Approximately fifteen hours before the outage, Teams failed over from the alternative networking gateway to the ‘primary’ gateway that also supports Exchange Online. This increased the load on the networking gateway and changed its traffic patterns. This type of failover is part of normal operations and is integral to standard Microsoft resiliency efforts.
Third, at the same time, nearly 14% of server capacity for the networking gateway was out of service due to an unrelated issue from the previous day. This server capacity was on track to be returned to active service through routine maintenance and health processes. The service had sufficient capacity headroom, so the reduction in server capacity had no impact on its own.
Taken together, the increased load and the reduced server capacity, caused the network gateway to encounter the latent bug from July. This caused requests to build up until servers ultimately stopped responding. This occurred during the peak window of traffic in the United States.
When servers lose connectivity, our traffic management systems automatically route users to other healthy endpoints. This happens often and is critical to avoiding any interruption due to hardware or software defects and is normally what makes the service resilient. However, the redirected traffic increased the likelihood for the same problem to occur on the remaining endpoints. This caused a cascading failure from server to server as the same amount of load was spread across fewer servers. In less than two hours, the previously unknown defect was encountered across our fleet and rendered them unresponsive. At its lowest, more than 80% of servers were unresponsive.
Normally, the network gateway is composed of thousands of ‘capacity units’ which represent shards of capacity, hardware, and networking infrastructure. These ‘capacity units’ are then organized into regions. In this case, due to a separate issue, the normal bulkheads between these different partitions did not stop the cascading failure. This is what allowed the impact to spread across our network gateway infrastructure globally.
Those services that were able – like Teams, OneDrive, SharePoint, and Office apps – failed out of the faulty network gateway infrastructure. That is why those services were partially restored earlier, with Teams chats and meetings functioning without issue for most of the outage. But not all services, like Exchange Online, can fail out of this network gateway for scale reasons.
Recovery took longer than usual because as servers returned to service, they were immediately overwhelmed by waiting traffic and became unresponsive again. Our normally automated recovery processes were not designed to restore service from this broad unresponsive state. Additionally, since the main code defect was a latent bug from several months previous, our normal rollback, flighting, and feature flags were unable to quickly revert the change.
After engineers confirmed the defect as the cause, we developed an update that returned the code path to the previously known good state in Windows. To effectively apply this fix, and to slowly bring the service online again, we took groups of servers out of service, applied the change, and restarted them before returning them to service. This process was carefully applied to large quantities of infrastructure at a time (e.g. 50%+ of a region).
The final Post-Incident Review (PIR) will provide more details.

 Automated recovery actions continued to redirect traffic to healthy endpoints; however, further service degradation was detected across multiple regions.

6:33 PM:
 We triggered failovers to ensure traffic was distributed evenly across our infrastructure and components that manage user connections.
7:00 PM:
 We expanded our response to accelerate mitigation efforts. As a precaution, we paused all ongoing and planned deployments and configuration changes.
7:20 PM:
 We determined that the elevated load we were observing was not driven by an overall increase in traffic, but by many servers being unexpectedly unavailable, which was shifting load onto and overwhelming the remaining infrastructure.
8:00 PM:
 We deployed a targeted change to bypass components we found to be triggering failed health checks on affected servers while we also investigated why those servers were showing elevated processor usage while handling requests.
8:30 PM:
 While attempting to validate the effectiveness of our targeted change, we found that many of the affected servers had become unresponsive. We began work to restart those servers.
9:00 PM:
 We prepared an alternative health check method on a subset of affected infrastructure, which allowed the servers in that subset to successfully return to service, and we began applying that change more broadly. In parallel, we prepared an automated recovery process to continually detect and restart unresponsive servers, to help keep them in service while the change progressed.
10:00 PM:
 Our telemetry and signals confirmed that the actions we’d taken to restore service had not been effective, and we broadened the scope of our investigation and response accordingly. We began analyzing traffic patterns across our entire service infrastructure and evaluating whether isolating traffic within each region would allow affected regions to recover independently.
11:00 PM:
 Work was underway to return a portion of the affected servers to service and shift traffic and connections to alternative pathways wherever possible. We observed limited recovery in some regions. To further support recovery, we implemented protective traffic management measures in a specific location to reduce load on the affected infrastructure, with plans to expand these measures to additional locations if we observed positive results.
11:55 PM:
 We determined that the servers we’d returned to service initially remained healthy but then become unresponsive again upon receiving traffic.
