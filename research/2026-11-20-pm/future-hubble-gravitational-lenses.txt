Astronomers plan to use the lenses, first found by DESI, to weigh galaxies and dark matter. Roman, launched August 30, is in its final commissioning phase.

In brief
NASA released a gallery of Hubble images of strong gravitational lenses on 
November 12, 2026
. The lenses were first detected with DESI at Kitt Peak, and Roman will observe them further.
Astronomers plan to use the images to model how much mass, dark matter included, sits in each foreground galaxy.
Roman, which launched on August 30, entered science commissioning in October; no start date for science operations has yet been given.
NASA has released a set of Hubble Space Telescope images of galaxies acting as natural magnifying glasses, and some of them are to be observed again by its new Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. The gallery, published on 
November 12, 2026
, shows strong gravitational lenses that were first detected with the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, DESI, at Kitt Peak and then imaged by Hubble. Astronomers want to use them to weigh the foreground galaxies, including the dark matter that cannot be seen directly.
How a lens weighs a galaxy
A gravitational lens forms when a massive foreground galaxy lines up almost exactly with a more distant one. The nearer galaxy's gravity bends, stretches and magnifies the background galaxy's light, so it appears as arcs around the lens. This happens only when the foreground galaxy's mass density reaches a critical point. The degree of distortion tells astronomers how much mass the lens contains and how it is spread out, invisible dark matter included, and the team plans to build lens models from a large and varied sample.
What the gallery shows
No two of the lenses look alike. Most of the foreground galaxies are ellipticals, which tend to be older, denser and more compact, but not all of them are, and researchers can use those differences to trace how the galaxies evolved. Bluish arcs point to young stars in the background galaxies and reddish arcs to older ones.

Two telescopes, two jobs
Hubble sees mainly visible and ultraviolet light, with some infrared, and gives sharp views of single objects. Roman sees mainly infrared, with some visible light, and its field of view is at least 100 times larger than Hubble's. As a result, Hubble gets the better view of an individual lens while Roman will catch more of them; if Roman finds an especially interesting one, researchers may point Hubble at it. On November 12, 2026, NASA released a collage of nine strong gravitational lenses observed by 
Hubble
. These massive galaxies bend light from objects farther away, turning their images into arcs, rings or several bright points. DESI, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument at Kitt Peak, found them first. Hubble then gave them a sharp look. The set is meant to prepare 
Roman
, launched on August 30, 2026, for its hunt for dark matter.
Nine lenses, nine ways to bend light
The collage has nine panels, labelled A to I. Each one shows a foreground galaxy acting as a lens. Behind it, a more distant galaxy has its light bent, stretched and sometimes copied. The lens is not made of glass. Its mass, stars and 
dark matter
 together curve the space around it.
Panel A shows an arc and a counter-arc. The background galaxy appears twice, on either side of two foreground galaxies whose masses add up. Panel F holds an Einstein ring, a rare setup in the set. The lens and the source line up so well that the background image almost completely surrounds the foreground galaxy. Panel I shows a quadruply lensed galaxy, copied into four blurred points around the centre. Panel C shows another quadruple system, with two bright knots that are probably star-forming regions.
The nine strong lenses in the collage, flagged by DESI and then observed by Hubble. Panels A to I show arcs, rings and multiple images. Credit: NASA, ESA and X. Huang (University of San Francisco), processing by Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America).
The variety goes beyond the shape of the arcs. Some arcs look blue, others red. Blue points to young stars, red to older ones. Colour therefore tells us about the stellar population of the background galaxy, not just its position. Most lens galaxies are ellipticals, older, denser and more compact than average. Panel G shows an elongated lens, possibly a trick of perspective, and panels B and F show spiral arms.
Panel A, an arc-counterarc setup. The background galaxy appears twice, warped by the combined mass of two foreground galaxies. Credit: NASA, ESA and X. Huang (University of San Francisco), processing by Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America).
Panel D adds a detail that Hubble is well suited to catch. Its lens galaxy is crossed by a dark dust lane, a rare feature for a lens. The lane cuts through the bright arc and may hide part of an Einstein ring. Infrared light passes through dust more easily, so Roman may show whether the ring closes.
Panel D, a dust lane crosses the lens galaxy and cuts the arc. Roman's infrared view could reveal a more complete Einstein ring. Credit: NASA, ESA and X. Huang (University of San Francisco), processing by Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America).
How a lens weighs dark matter
A gravitational lens is not just a pretty picture. It is a measurement. The shape of the arcs depends on the total mass of the foreground galaxy. Astronomers build a model of the lens, compare the position and curvature of the images, then adjust the mass until the model matches the data. Stars, gas and dark matter all count.
The method is indirect, but it works. It is one of the few ways to weigh the dark matter of a single galaxy. Hubble supplies what ground surveys struggle to provide: a sharp view of the arcs in visible and ultraviolet light. Models can then look for tiny flaws in the curvature. Such flaws would betray invisible mass concentrations along the light path.
Cold dark matter theory predicts exactly that kind of lumpiness. In this picture, dark matter does not sit in one smooth blob. It clumps into small structures, with masses from one million to one billion solar masses. For scale, the dark matter halo of our Milky Way weighs about 1.3 trillion solar masses, or 1,300 billion. The clumps are dwarfs next to our halo, and their effect on an arc is tiny.
Panel I, a quadruply lensed galaxy. The position and sharpness of the four images constrain the mass of the lens galaxy. Credit: NASA, ESA and X. Huang (University of San Francisco), processing by Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America).
That is where the nine images earn their keep. They offer a range of shapes, masses and colours. Researchers can test their models on very different cases before applying them to the larger set of lenses Roman should find. NASA describes a software tool, a "super-intelligence tool", to search for similar arcs in the coming images. The name sounds grand. The job is practical: spot and measure fast, at a scale no human eye can follow.
Roman, the lens harvester
Roman
 launched on August 30, 2026, at 7:26 a.m. EDT on a SpaceX Falcon Heavy from Launch Complex 39A in Florida. It began a three-month trip to its final orbit. Its main advantage is simple: a wide field. Roman's view is at least 100 times larger than Hubble's, with comparable sharpness.
The division of labour is easy to state. Hubble isolates one lens and shows its details. Roman images large patches of sky and collects many lenses at once. One works like a magnifying glass, the other like a net. NASA pairs them for that reason. If Roman finds an especially interesting lens, Hubble can return to it in visible and ultraviolet light.
Roman observes mainly in the 
infrared
, with some visible light. Infrared passes through dust that blocks some arcs. It also reaches farther, to more distant and older galaxies. The telescope should thus weigh many lens galaxies at a range of distances and trace how dark matter has organised itself.
From DESI to Roman. The ground survey flags alignments, Hubble shows the arcs, models give the mass and Roman should scale up the search. Graphic: Tracker-1, after NASA.
A test bed, not an answer yet
One temptation is worth resisting. The nine images are not a dark matter detection. NASA writes that the observations could confirm or challenge a theory, not that they already have. The clumps between one million and one billion solar masses remain a prediction. Seeing them will take finer models, more arcs and deeper images.
The programme is preparation in the strong sense. Hubble provides the textbook cases and the measurement tools. Roman should provide the volume. Researchers describe a large and varied sample, able to test the theory on a much broader set. The question is no longer whether a lens can bend light. It is whether tiny flaws in the arcs carry the signature of the predicted clumps.
The nine panels also teach diversity. A lens galaxy is not always a neat elliptical. It can have spiral arms, a dust lane or an elongated shape. Every detail changes the model, and therefore the mass. By stacking cases, astronomers learn to separate physics from geometry. That sorting is what will make Roman's measurements usable.

Hubble’s nine lenses show Roman what to hunt for in dark matter
NASA has released a nine-panel gallery of gravitational lenses seen by Hubble, a preview of what the new Roman telescope will find in far greater numbers.
By 
the SpaceAndSky news desk
Published: 
November 12, 2026
6 Min Read
SHARE
NASA has published a gallery of nine gravitational lenses imaged by the Hubble Space Telescope, chosen to show the variety that the newly launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will survey. Astronomers will use them to weigh galaxies and test ideas about dark matter.
According to 
NASA
, the gallery lays the groundwork for follow-up observations by Roman, which launched in August 2026.
Image: NASA, ESA, and X. Huang (University of San Francisco); Processing: Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America)
What a gravitational lens looks like in practice
A gravitational lens forms when the gravity of a massive foreground object, such as a galaxy or a cluster of galaxies, bends and magnifies the light of a more distant galaxy behind it. The effect works like a giant lens in space and lets astronomers see objects farther away than any telescope could reach alone. Albert Einstein first predicted it.
In these nine systems, a distant background galaxy lines up with a foreground galaxy. The result is distorted, magnified images of the background galaxy, seen as arcs around the foreground one. The amount of distortion tells astronomers how much mass the foreground galaxy holds and how it is spread out, including the invisible dark matter.
File photo: Saturn from Far and Near Hubble Space Telescope. Image: NASA, ESA and Erich Karkoschka University of Arizona
NASA points to several panels that show how different these systems can be:
Panel A
 is an arc-counterarc configuration: the background galaxy appears as a duplicated pair of arcs, magnified by a pair of foreground galaxies.
Panel F
 is an Einstein ring, where the alignment is so precise that the lensed galaxy almost fully encircles the foreground galaxy.
Panels I and C
 each show a quadruply lensed galaxy, meaning the background galaxy is replicated four times.
Panel D
 has a visible dust lane in the foreground galaxy, which NASA calls rare in a lensing galaxy. The lensed galaxy could be an Einstein ring that the dust is “breaking” from our view.
Colors carry information too. Bluish arcs indicate young stars and reddish arcs older ones, so comparing them helps show how galaxies evolved earlier in the universe. Most foreground galaxies are ellipticals, which are older, denser and more compact, but NASA notes that panels B and F appear to have spiral arms, and the foreground galaxy in panel G looks elongated. Such differences let researchers explore the paths these galaxies took.
The lenses were first detected with the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument at Kitt Peak, then observed by Hubble, and Roman will observe them next.
Hunting small clumps of dark matter
The scientific prize is dark matter, the unseen material that makes up most of a galaxy’s mass. The cold dark matter theory predicts many small clumps of between a million and a billion solar masses. By comparison, NASA gives the dark matter halo of the Milky Way as about 1.3 trillion solar masses.
Such clumps would leave small imperfections in the lensed arcs. With Hubble’s sharp images and precise lens models, researchers expect to detect them, and finding them would support the cold dark matter theory. NASA says the team also intends to build an AI tool from the Hubble images to search for similar arcs in Roman’s data.
Why the two telescopes split the work
Hubble sees mostly visible and ultraviolet light with some infrared, giving a high-resolution view of single objects. Roman sees mainly infrared and some visible light over a much larger area, building panoramas. In NASA’s words, Hubble gets the better view of an individual lens, while Roman can capture many more of them.
NASA’s Roman mission page says the telescope will have a field of view at least 100 times larger than Hubble’s. That is what turns lens hunting from a handful of cases into a statistical sample, which is what the dark matter test needs: a few clumps in one lens prove little, while a pattern across many lenses can.
Infrared light is also less blocked by dust. For panel D, Roman could show whether the arcs close into a fuller Einstein ring behind the dust lane. If Roman spots an especially interesting lens, researchers may return to Hubble for visible and ultraviolet follow-up of that single object.
What to watch as Roman’s lens catalog grows
NASA did not give dates for the lens observations, so the markers to follow are Roman’s first science images and any lens candidates in them. Roman’s mission page describes the telescope as in commissioning, so full survey data will come later. Two results would matter most: whether the small clumps predicted by cold dark matter turn up in the arcs, and how many new lenses Roman finds beyond the Hubble set.
Hubble has also been busy closer to home; see our report on 
Hubble’s measurements of interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS
. NASA frames the lens work as an example of its Great Observatories (Hubble, Chandra, Webb and now Roman) working together to build a fuller picture of the cosmos.

NASA’s Hubble Offers Cosmic Sampler of Targets for Roman
NASA Hubble Mission Team
Goddard Space Flight Center
Nov 12, 2026
Article
A collage of strong gravitational lenses observed by Hubble, some of which will be followed up by observations with the Roman Space Telescope, which launched in August 2026. Lenses like these will be used to investigate the evolution of galaxies and dark matter.
Credit: NASA, ESA, and X. Huang (University of San Francisco); Processing: Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America)
A gallery of gravitational lenses captured by NASA’s Hubble Space Telescope showcases the diversity of this cosmic phenomenon and lays the groundwork for follow-up observations by the new Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope.
Gravitational lensing, first predicted by Albert Einstein, occurs when the gravity of a massive foreground object, such as a galaxy or cluster of galaxies, bends, warps, and magnifies the light from a more distant background object. The effect is similar to that of a giant lens in space that allows astronomers to view objects much farther away than any telescope could see on its own.
The lenses in this image formed from the precise alignment of a distant background galaxy with the mass of a foreground galaxy, creating distorted and magnified images of the background galaxy that appear as arcs around the foreground galaxy.
Astronomers will use these observations of a large and varied population of gravitational lenses to construct lens models that will help determine how much mass is in the lensing system’s foreground galaxy – and potentially prove or dispel a theory about dark matter. Astronomers study the amount of distortion of the lensed background galaxies to determine the quantity and distribution of mass in the foreground galaxy, including its invisible dark matter.
Hubble views the cosmos primarily in visible and ultraviolet light with some infrared coverage, providing a comprehensive, high-resolution view of individual objects. Roman sees primarily infrared light and some visible, over a much larger field of view to create panoramas of the universe. While Hubble gets a better view of individual lenses, Roman will be able to capture more lenses with its wider field-of-view. If Roman finds a particularly interesting lens, researchers may use Hubble to take follow-up observations using its visible and UV prowess for individual objects.
With Hubble's high-resolution imaging and precise models, researchers should also be able to detect small imperfections in these gravitationally lensed galaxy arcs that would indicate the presence of small clumps of dark matter. Researchers could use these previously undetected clumps to confirm the cold dark matter theory, which predicts the existence of many small dark matter clumps of between a million to a billion solar masses. In contrast, the dark matter halo of our Milky Way galaxy is around 1.3 trillion solar masses.
By studying Hubble lens images in detail, researchers intend to build a super-intelligence tool to efficiently search similar arcs in the upcoming Roman images. The Hubble images are expected to both yield their own results on dark matter and pave the way for analysis of Roman’s larger sample of lenses.
The unique lenses in this image display some of the different characteristics researchers will see as they collect a diverse and numerous assortment of lenses to test in their models. Panel A is an example of an arc-counterarc configuration, in which the lensed background galaxy appears as a duplicated pair of arcs. The lensed galaxy is being magnified by a pair of foreground galaxies whose combined mass bends the light of the background galaxy around them.
The diversity of lenses in this image demonstrate some of the characteristics researchers will uncover with Hubble and Roman. Each lens differs significantly from others in the panel.
Credit: NASA, ESA, and X. Huang (University of San Francisco); Processing: Gladys Kober (NASA/Catholic University of America)
Panel F is an Einstein Ring, in which the alignment of the background galaxy and approximately spherical mass of the foreground lens are so perfectly situated that the lensed galaxy almost fully encircles the foreground galaxy. Panel I shows a quadruply lensed galaxy, in which the background galaxy is replicated four times, appearing as smeared dots around the foreground galaxy. Panel C also shows a quadruply lensed galaxy, with double knots that are most likely strong star-forming regions in the background galaxy.
Some of the magnified and warped background galaxies appear blue and some appear red. These bluish arcs indicate young stars, while reddish arcs indicate older stars. Roman and Hubble will explore these differences to better understand how galaxies evolved earlier in the universe.
Most of the foreground lensing galaxies are elliptical galaxies, which are older, denser, and more compact. Lensed images of background galaxies only occur when the mass density of the foreground galaxy reaches a critical point.
But not all of these foreground galaxies are ellipticals, and researchers can use these differences to explore the evolutionary paths taken by these galaxies. In panel G, the foreground galaxy appears elongated, which may be true or a trick of perspective, and in panels B and F, the foreground galaxies appear to have spiral arms.
Panel D’s foreground galaxy has a visible dust lane, which is rare in a lensing galaxy and the kind of detail that Hubble’s sharp, visible-light vision excels at revealing. The lensed background galaxy could be an Einstein Ring, with the dust lane “breaking” our view of the ring. Because infrared light is less obscured by dust, future Roman infrared observations could help determine whether the arcs form a more complete Einstein ring.
These strong lenses were first detected with The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument at Kitt Peak. Hubble subsequently observed them, and Roman will take further observations. Images like these are an example of the kinds of collaborative efforts that routinely take place between NASA’s Great Observatories to build a comprehensive picture of the cosmos. As Roman joins the family of large space observatories that includes Hubble, the Chandra X-ray Observatory, and the James Webb Space Telescope, a new window opens onto the universe, revealing sights previously hidden while expanding our cosmic knowledge for all of humanity.
