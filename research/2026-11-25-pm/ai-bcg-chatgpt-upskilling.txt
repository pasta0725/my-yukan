AI Lifted Consultants’ Data-Science Scores, but Not Their Know-How
A randomized trial of 487 management consultants found that ChatGPT improved performance on unfamiliar data-science tasks. After the experiment, researchers found no evidence that AI users had gained technical knowledge they could apply without the tool.

487 management consultants used ChatGPT on unfamiliar data-science tasks in a randomized trial, but researchers found no evidence that the AI group gained technical knowledge they could demonstrate without the tool. The study, published October 26 in 
Nature Human Behaviour
, puts a boundary around what “upskilling” can mean when workers perform new tasks with an AI assistant: better work with the tool did not translate into measurable independent knowledge after the experiment.
Higher scores with ChatGPT, no offline knowledge gain
The researchers gave consultants access to ChatGPT and training in using it, then compared their performance with a control group. Participants worked on tasks outside their usual skill sets, designed so ChatGPT could not solve them independently. The AI-assisted group scored higher than controls on three data-science tasks, though the study found they did not perform at the level of expert data scientists.
“We find no evidence that treated workers are better at answering technical questions without ChatGPT post-experiment, suggesting that their demonstrated technical capabilities do not imply knowledge acquisition.”
Emma Wiles et al., “Generative AI and the temporary upskilling of knowledge workers”
The researchers’ follow-up questions tested whether participants could answer data-science and coding questions without AI. Their results did not show a reliable advantage for the people who had used ChatGPT. The finding does not establish that AI makes workers lose existing skills; the study tested whether participants acquired knowledge in an area outside their skills.
Python, statistics and a prediction problem
The exercises covered three kinds of work: writing Python code to clean and combine data, interpreting statistical information and correcting modeling errors, and building and assessing a predictive model. Consultants were assigned two of the three tasks and had up to 90 minutes for each. The prediction exercise used soccer-match data, while the coding task asked participants to clean customer and order records and identify customers with the highest average spending.
The trial began with 986 workers allocated between treatment and control groups; 573 started the survey, and 487 completed both tasks used in the main analysis. That distinction matters when interpreting the headline sample: the published abstract’s 487 refers to the analyzed participants, not everyone initially allocated. The supplementary materials also describe the researchers’ separate post-experiment knowledge questions, which were answered by a larger group.
Performance is not the same as learning
The results separate two outcomes that can look similar in workplace evaluations. An employee may complete a technical assignment more successfully with ChatGPT, while still being unable to explain or repeat the underlying work independently. The paper describes AI as an “exoskeleton” that can extend workers’ capabilities, but says those gains depend on continued use of the technology.
That distinction matters for companies that treat AI-assisted output as evidence of employee development. A finished analysis may demonstrate that a person can collaborate with an AI system, not that the person has learned data science. The study measured task performance and post-experiment knowledge; it did not test whether longer-term practice, structured teaching or repeated use of AI with deliberate instruction would change retention.
A BCG study with an OpenAI co-author
The trial involved management consultants recruited through Boston Consulting Group, whose Henderson Institute is listed as the affiliation for several authors. BCG provided employee time and internal resources for the research, and the paper says BCG-affiliated authors took part in its design, data collection, analysis and writing. Co-author Pamela Mishkin is affiliated with 
OpenAI
’s Economic Impacts Research group, which the paper says collaborated on task design.
The study is therefore evidence about one group of consultants working on a narrow set of data-science exercises, not a demonstration that AI erodes skills across occupations. Whether coached AI use can turn better supported performance into retained knowledge—and whether that happens in other jobs—remains outside the trial’s results.

===== https://employed-ai.com/articles/chatgpt-capable-not-skilled-temporary-upskilling/
(status 200)
A study in 
Nature Human Behaviour
 published on 26 October puts a number on something employers have mostly been guessing about. When 487 BCG management consultants were given ChatGPT for unfamiliar data-science work, they scored far above a control group that used conventional resources. When the same consultants answered technical questions without the tool, the two groups were indistinguishable.
The authors, led by Emma Wiles of Boston University and Lisa Krayer of BCG’s Henderson Institute, put the phrase “temporary upskilling” in the title of the paper. Our shorthand for what they found is that access made the consultants capable without making them skilled.
That gap is why the study is worth reading closely. It is the cleanest peer-reviewed test of the question so far, though it was run and designed by the consultancy whose consultants it studied: when generative AI does the work, what does the worker keep?
What did the study actually test?
The design is a randomised controlled trial, which is rare for questions about AI at work. Consultants were assigned at random to one of two groups. One got access to and training in ChatGPT, specifically GPT-4. The other got the same amount of training in conventional resources: Google, Stack Overflow and Khan Academy. Random assignment matters because it produces two groups that should otherwise look alike, so a difference in results can be attributed to the tool rather than to who happened to sign up.
Everyone then attempted two of three 90-minute data-science tasks covering coding, statistical reasoning and prediction. The tasks were built by BCG data scientists working with OpenAI’s Economic Impacts Research team, and they were designed so that GPT-4 could not solve them on its own: correct answers required human input at several steps. They were also chosen to fall outside the consultants’ existing skill sets.
Two consequences follow from that. The result measures a person and a model working together on problems the model could not finish alone, and the study does not test whether AI can teach.
The comparison point was 44 BCG data scientists who did the same tasks without generative AI. All scores are normalised so that zero is that group’s average and minus one is no points at all. Of 986 consultants randomised, 487 submitted gradable output for both tasks, and that 487 is the main analysis sample.
What does ‘temporary upskilling’ mean, and where does ‘exoskeleton’ come from?
“Temporary upskilling” is the authors’ own phrase, and it appears in the published title of the paper. The abstract uses a different word: generative AI can act as an “exoskeleton”, enhancing workers’ capabilities when attempting new skills, “but these gains depend on continued technology use.”
The metaphor is the authors’ own framing, and it is not new. Four of the paper’s authors, with a BCG colleague, made the same argument in a Fortune column on 4 October 2024, two years before the peer-reviewed version appeared. The paper was received in August 2024 and accepted in July 2026.
The word “depend” deserves care. It is an inference from the design: capability was measured while both groups had the tools they had been given, and the unaided test found no group difference. The study took no measurement after the tool was withdrawn and no follow-up of any kind, so it records nothing about skills persisting or fading.
What happened on the three tasks?
Treated consultants scored 49 percentage points above controls on coding, 20 above on statistics and 17 above on prediction. All three effects are significant below p = 0.001, using Huber-White HC0 robust standard errors, and the per-task samples were 300, 330 and 298.
The gains stopped short of expert level. On all three tasks the treated group stayed below the 44-data-scientist benchmark, and the paper says it can reject the hypothesis that treated consultants performed as well as expert data scientists.
How the tasks were scored shapes what those numbers mean. Coding was graded as the share of ten defined rubric steps completed correctly, and almost nobody produced a fully correct answer within 90 minutes. Statistics free-response items were graded with a validated GPT-4 pipeline. Prediction was scored by distance from the data scientists’ own answers.
One subgroup result is easy to overstate. Among consultants who already coded competently, treatment brought coding and statistics close enough to the benchmark that the authors could not reject equality. Failing to reject equality is a statement about the limits of a sample, and it is not a finding that those consultants matched the data scientists.
Why do the unaided questions matter more than the task scores?
After the tasks, participants answered five technical questions without ChatGPT. This is the only part of the study that looks for knowledge the consultants could use alone, and between 253 and 573 people answered each question.
The effects ranged from -7.7 to +4.8 percentage points, and none was individually significant. Two came close: +4.8 points on distance-based algorithms and scaling, with p = 0.075, and -7.7 points on classifying a prediction problem, with p = 0.057. A joint test across all five gives F(5,198) = 0.83 and p = 0.531, and the Bayes factors all sit below one, which favours the null hypothesis of no difference.
This is where our “capable, not skilled” reading comes from, and it is ours rather than the authors’ wording. Their vocabulary is AI-enabled task capability against durable, independent knowledge. What the data show is the absence of a detectable difference on five questions asked once, immediately afterwards. That is not a measurement of what any individual learned or failed to learn.
What are this study’s limits, and what can it not tell you?
Four limits belong next to those headline numbers.
The first is that the study is peer-reviewed but industry-run and industry-designed. Seven of its eight authors had a BCG financial relationship while the work was done: six are or were BCG employees, and Emma Wiles received consulting fees from BCG during the experiment. The eighth, Pamela Mishkin, works at OpenAI, whose Economic Impacts Research team helped design the tasks. Funding was BCG employee time and internal resources, with no external funding reported.
The second is scale and time. The analysis rests on 487 consultants at one employer who produced gradable output for both tasks, out of 986 randomised. The authors’ repository still lists 573 as its abstract’s sample size and as the number who began the survey, so that figure circulates; the published abstract reports 487, which is the analysis sample. The tasks lasted 90 minutes each, the training ran to two 20-minute sessions, and no follow-up measurement followed. The authors’ audit of the manuscript flags Table 1, whose total row prints 983 while the two arms sum to 986; the body text uses 986.
The third is what can be checked from outside. The underlying data are available only on request to certified academics who sign a usage agreement, and the 
public repository
’s data and computed-objects folders hold placeholders. That leaves the authors’ own reproduction script unrunnable by anyone outside that route. The numbers in this article come from the published abstract and the authors’ public repository outputs, because the full text is paywalled; the authors’ own repository audit notes the paper’s preregistration and supplement links are not resolvable from the repository.
The fourth is that an absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The unaided result rests on five questions asked once, and the authors’ own audit file flags a sample-size inconsistency in Figure 3: the figure note lists per-question samples of 323, 270, 270, 151 and 270, while the plotted bars, the text and the joint test use the full samples of 573, 399, 418, 253 and 408. The audit states that under the smaller subsample two questions would become individually significant, one positive and one negative, which the text does not report. That audit is the authors’ own rather than an independent one, and the published version of record is authoritative, so treat the discrepancy as a flag on the headline rather than a correction to it.
One further detail deserves a line. The paper reports that treated consultants became more confident that GPT-4 could answer problems it cannot, significant on all four such questions, with effects between 5.4 and 9.6 percentage points. That result is exploratory and was not preregistered.
A separate working paper posted the same day, arXiv:2610.36008, examines how the return to schooling shifts in occupations most exposed to ChatGPT, a wage-structure question rather than a skill one.
What does this mean for training budgets?
The trial measures the value of access to a tool, not the value of durable skill, and those are different line items in a budget. If a training budget is buying capability while the tool is present, the trial supports the purchase, since the treated group’s output on unfamiliar tasks was far higher than the control group’s. If it is buying skills that survive the tool, the trial offers no support, and it does not claim to.
For anyone assessing a vendor’s or a consultancy’s upskilling pitch, the practical question is what the programme measures. Task output while the tool is available is cheap to demonstrate. Unaided performance after a delay is the harder test, and this study shows how far apart the two answers can sit.
