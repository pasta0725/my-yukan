OpenClaw, working with a host of other industry players including Hugging Face, Nvidia, Red Hat, Google and Amazon, Tuesday rolled out an AI performance benchmark called ShellBench.
OpenClaw’s position is that the benchmark will compare various AI models (it will soon look at a total of 24) and test them on complex queries, ones that should better replicate the kind of complicated tasks that an enterprise would perform. 
“Ask an AI agent to summarize a document, and it will probably do a decent job. Ask it to reconcile a handful of receipts against a spreadsheet, check which information is current, flag a suspicious payment request, and explain how it reached its answer? Maybe not so well,” said the 
OpenClaw post describing the new benchmark
. “This kind of realistic test, one that expects an agent not just to find the right information but also to keep track of it along the way, is exactly what we’re launching ShellBench to evaluate.”
Analysts and consultants said the new benchmark findings appear initially to be more useful than existing AI performance benchmarks, but it is less clear how useful those results will be.
Thomas Randall
, a research director at Infotech, said that he sees ShellBench as focusing on those who are building and configuring agents directly, but most enterprises instead buy their AI capabilities from various large AI vendors.
“They are choosing capabilities within an enterprise platform, often with limited control over the underlying model or agent configuration. For these organizations, ShellBench offers little direct use,” Randall said. “ShellBench giving a strong score for an underlying model does not tell us the quality of output from a solution built on it. The tasks ShellBench tests are skewed toward personal knowledge work rather than heavy enterprise workflows that buyers typically evaluate for.”
Are results reproducible?
Another analyst concern is how open ShellBench will be, especially in terms of how easily IT can reproduce its findings. 
Brian Levine
, a partner with consulting firm Control Risks, said that reproducibility is essential.
“Whoever writes the exam has a stake in who passes it. That doesn’t make the results wrong, but the right question isn’t whether you trust OpenClaw. It’s whether you can check their work,” Levine said. “If the tasks, scoring, and raw results are open and others can reproduce them, pay attention. If not, read it the way you’d read a carmaker’s own crash test.”
However, the problem with ShellBench is that parts of it are open and parts of it are not.
It’s complicated
Louka Ewington-Pitsos
, a co-founder of Parsewave and a collaborating researcher for the benchmark, said the division of public and private elements in the benchmark is complicated.
“Teams can rerun ShellBench on the public task set, which is a subset of roughly 20 tasks of the full benchmark. The ShellBench GitHub repo includes the evaluation configurations and we pin the agent harnesses to the exact versions used in the study so teams can reproduce the same setup,” Ewington-Pitsos said. “The remaining tasks are private.”
He added that the “private part is only the 80 remaining tasks. The evaluation configs and the pinned harness versions apply to the whole benchmark and are available for anyone to use.”
Ewington-Pitsos said that the reason those tasks have to remain private is “to avoid benchmark contamination, which is where data leaks from the public internet into model training data, making the benchmark less valid for future models.”
The initial version of the benchmark looks at 13 models, but the number of models tracked will double to 26 by next week, said 
Vincent Koc
, the OpenClaw chief architect, in an interview. 
Koc said that although almost all enterprises will do their own AI performance benchmarks, they typically won’t be able to replicate ShellBench’s comprehensiveness. They “won’t have it at this speed and scale,” he said. 
Just about right
Most analysts and consultants thought that ShellBench specs were just about right, in that they focused on the more complex tasks most large businesses need. 
“Most agent benchmarks are the equivalent of testing a new hire on spelling. Enterprises don’t need an agent that can spell. They need one that can run a messy, multi-step job across five systems without locking the CFO out of email. A benchmark built around that kind of work is measuring the right thing,” Levine said. “From a risk standpoint, the most important result isn’t which agent finishes the task. It’s how an agent fails. An agent that stalls is an annoyance. An agent that confidently does the wrong thing with admin credentials is an incident. Realistic, complex tests are where you find out which one you have, before your production environment does.”
Ishraq Khan
, CEO of coding productivity tool vendor Kodezi, said that the leapfrog nature of AI model performance, in which models regularly exceed each others’ performance, only to be superseded by the next version of their competitor’s model, is a concern, but ShellBench could still be useful by providing a look at the trends over time.
“The leaderboard itself will inevitably become outdated. However, the underlying failure patterns and evaluation methodology have a much longer shelf life. Enterprises should use benchmarks like ShellBench as a foundation for continuously evaluating agents against their own operational workflows, rather than making a one-time purchasing decision,” Khan said. “As for OpenClaw’s credibility, the involvement of researchers from established academic and industry institutions is encouraging. But OpenClaw is also evaluating its own harness against competing products, so independent replication is important.”
Jason Andersen
, principal analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, said that he found much to like in ShellBench. 
“This is a really interesting benchmark since it finally decouples the harness and the model and measures it. So far the benchmark activity is limited to the model and that is not accurate in terms of how people are actually using models which is via a harness,” he said. “I think this is a trend we will see continue to expand due to technologies like OpenClaw and Hermes. Some vendors like IBM and Microsoft have been publishing results of open weight or small models with a good harness performing quite well versus medium and large frontier models.”
Harder to game
Andersen also suggested that the more complex testing from ShellBench may have the added benefit of making the results harder for vendors to fool.
“One of the things about benchmarks is that they can be gamed, and the simpler the benchmark, the easier it is for entrants to game with solutions or configurations that may never apply in real life,” he said. “But a more complex set of methods is harder to game, so there’s a chance this could be a more realistic set of outputs than the simpler model performance metrics.”
Frank Dickson
, principal analyst at Dickson Research, agreed, adding that ShellBench included a lot more verification in their analysis. 
“The researchers deserve credit for the bar they set. Requiring every criterion to pass, running each task three times, and publishing exactly where and how agents fall short is more rigor than most AI benchmarks offer,” he said. 
Dickson pointed to some of the initial findings of the benchmark as examples of the model’s advantages. 
“Consider the spread: The top three models, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2, and GPT-6.1 Sol, finished within 1.1 percentage points of each other,” he said. “Yet GPT-6 Astra completed 75.1% of tasks at least once in three tries and only 48.8% on all three. OpenClaw notes that its grader may account for some of that swing. Either way, a 26-point swing inside one model dwarfs a 1.1-point gap between the leaders.”
So, he observed, “the enterprise question is not who is number one this week. The question is which tier a model sits in, and which failures show up no matter who leads. ShellBench found a median gap of 45.7 points between the best and worst model within the same harness. That is the gap worth caring about.”

Though agents have become a massive part of the enterprise AI conversation, new research suggests that these systems aren't always dependable.
On Tuesday, the OpenClaw Foundation debuted ShellBench, a new benchmark testing agents and harnesses on 100 real world tasks, designed in partnership with Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat, and UC Berkeley. The benchmark finds that, on average, even agents powered by the leading models fail to fully complete four out of every ten tasks.
"We wanted a good distribution of tasks that gave us good signals on how the models were performing," Vincent Koc, chief architect at the OpenClaw foundation, told The Deep View in an exclusive interview. "Really, the focus was on everyday use."
Along with testing on realistic tasks, one thing that sets ShellBench apart from other AI benchmarks is the setting in which these models are tested. The places where everyday work is done are hardly ever as clean and organized as those that other AI benchmarks test in. That's why OpenClaw tested models on common tasks using short requests in realistic workspaces, such as emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, receipts, PDFs and more.
"Some of these are going to become more realistic to individual needs," said Koc. "If I'm an enterprise and I'm deploying agents at scale for each one of my employees, I think having agents and models … that are able to work in a scenario that's more aligned with personal use, that is going to translate to agents that are going to be more effective."
ShellBench tested a suite of 13 models, both open source and proprietary, from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, DeepSeek, MoonShot, and Nvidia, across five different agent harnesses: Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, OpenClaw, and Pi.
When tested in messy environments on tasks that weren't clear cut, the results were staggering, even for the top of the line agents.The highest-performing models tested, GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Fable 5.2, and Gemini 4 Carbon, were only able to successfully complete tasks six times out of ten. Meta Muse Wonder 1.0, DeepSeek v4.1 Flash and Claude Sonnet, meanwhile, only saw a task pass rate of roughly four times out of ten.
OpenClaw's other insights include:
Oftentimes, even if the agents were able to locate accurate information, they left out important details, with 51% of task completion failures being related to information omission, despite 87% of those cases having collected the relevant information in the first place.
These models weren't reliably consistent across repeated testing. For instance, GPT-6 Astra completed tasks with a 75% accuracy rate in one of the tests, but when repeated two more times, that number dropped to 49%.
Paying more doesn't always mean a more capable model. Though the three highest performing models saw little difference in performance, they varied wildly in price, with GPT-6 Astra scoring a 62.5% accuracy rate on ShellBench at $1.01 per run, while GPT-6.1 Sol scored 61.4% at only 17 cents per run.
Additionally, the benchmark tested model harnesses, finding that the four open source harnesses that were tested, including Hermes, OpenClaw, Pi and Codex CLI, performed with slightly more accuracy than the proprietary Claude Code harness.

What is ShellBench?

Which AI agent scored best?
Averaged across five agent setups, GPT-6 Astra led with 62.5%. Claude Fable 5.2 scored 61.9% and GPT-6.1 Sol 61.4%, so the top three sit within 1.1 points of each other.

ShellBench also repeated each task. GPT-6 Astra got a task right at least once in three tries 75.1% of the time, but right on all three tries only 48.8% of the time.

Companies now sell agents that pay bills, book trips and sort your files. A score near six in ten means a person still has to check the result, and the safety numbers say the check should not be skipped.

===== https://openclaw.ai/blog/shellbench
(status 200)
Ask an AI agent to summarize a document, and it will probably do a decent job. Ask it to reconcile a handful of receipts against a spreadsheet, check which information is current, flag a suspicious payment request, and explain how it reached its answer? Maybe not so well.
This kind of realistic test, one that expects an agent not just to find the right information but also to keep track of it along the way, is exactly what we’re launching ShellBench to evaluate.
Shellbench is a new benchmark developed in collaboration with researchers at The OpenClaw Foundation, Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat, and UC Berkeley. It draws on more than 10 million anonymized, real-world AI agent interactions to measure how AI agents perform on the everyday work people actually delegate to them.
ShellBench evaluated twelve models: GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Carbon, Muse Wonder Full, Claude Opus 5.5, Muse Wonder 1.0, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Kimi K3.1 Pro, DeepSeek V4 Pro, and GPT-6 Luna. These were tested across five agent harnesses: Claude Code, Codex, Hermes, OpenClaw, and Pi.
We tested 100 tasks, with 53,100 graded runs in total.
Testing agents on everyday work
Most AI benchmarks are designed around clearly defined problems, like answering a question, solving a math problem, or writing test-passing code. But real requests from real people for their daily work are rarely so organized. More likely, you’re asking your agent to find the latest version of a document, reconcile expenses from dozens of sources, or prepare a reply based on a long email thread.
ShellBench tests this kind of work.
Each tested scenario starts with a short request (median of just 43 words across the 90 rubric-graded tasks) and a realistic workspace (median of 188 files across those tasks), including emails, calendar exports, spreadsheets, chat logs, receipts, saved web pages, and PDFs.
Most tasks introduce a complication, like outdated records, similar-looking accounts, conflicting information, and even potentially malicious instructions.
The agent has to find the right evidence, complete the request, avoid phishing traps, and produce a verifiable answer.
Of those 100 tasks, 90 are rubric-graded against 721 criteria (including 381 safety criteria), and 10 are code-tested. For the rubric-graded tasks, every required criterion must pass for the task to count as complete.
This is a demanding standard, and deliberately so. Let’s dig into the results.
1. Even the strongest agents have a long way to go
Averaged across harnesses, even the highest-performing models successfully completed only about six in ten tasks.
GPT-6 Astra led at 62.5%, followed by Claude Fable 5.2 at 61.9% and GPT-6.1 Sol at 61.4%. Just 1.1 percentage points separated the top three.
That means even the leading models failed to satisfy every requirement on roughly four in ten attempts.
In practice, this means if an agent is reconciling payments, identifying the latest instructions, or preparing a complete report, one missing requirement can change whether the work is ready to use.
2. Getting the facts right isn’t enough if you leave out the evidence
One of ShellBench’s most striking findings concerns what agents do after locating relevant information.
Across 77 tasks, 87 criteria check whether the answer names the files it used, including the records that settle conflicting information. The dinner-planning task shows why this matters.
For example, in one dinner-planning task, 522 of 531 attempts picked the right restaurant, but 331 failed solely because the recommendation didn’t name the files supporting the group’s budget or timing. Only 152 met every requirement.
Retrieving information, reasoning about it, and preserving it through to the final answer are different capabilities. An agent can retrieve the right information and still fail to preserve it in the final answer.
For developers, this suggests that improving the next generation of agents won’t be limited to reducing hallucinations. It will also mean improving how agents track requirements, preserve important context, verify completeness, and account for the evidence they used.
3. Getting it right once doesn’t mean getting it right consistently
ShellBench also tested reliability across repeated attempts.
Averaged across five harnesses in the 100-task evaluation, GPT-6 Astra successfully completed 75.1% of tasks at least once across three attempts.
But it completed only 48.8% successfully on all three attempts. Because each run was judged once, some of this variation may come from the judge.
Notably, this shows that, while an agent might be capable of accomplishing a task, that doesn’t necessarily mean it will continue accomplishing it reliably. Only repeated evaluations can reveal if it does.
An agent that succeeds once but fails every third time on the same assignment still requires oversight.
4. Safety failures can hide inside otherwise ordinary tasks
ShellBench also specifically focuses on safety. Its tasks incorporate risks that can arise in normal workflows, including phishing attempts, fraudulent payment instructions, sensitive information, and irreversible actions.
The 100-task evaluation includes 381 individual safety criteria.
Across all 53,100 runs, 15.7% failed at least one safety check. Harness choice made little overall difference to safety. Even among the 26,151 runs that met every correctness criterion, 2,589 (9.9%) still failed a safety requirement.
An agent’s overall task-completion rate doesn’t eliminate the need to examine safety across a complete workflow, reflecting the ongoing work necessary for the entire agent ecosystem.
An agent that can read files, interact with services, and take actions needs to recognize more than whether an instruction is technically executable. It also needs to recognize whether the instruction is appropriate, authorized, and based on trustworthy information.
5. The model matters more than the harness, but the harness still matters
Across the models tested in all five harnesses, the median gap between the highest- and lowest-scoring models within a harness was 45.7 percentage points.
Switching harnesses while keeping the model fixed produced a smaller median gap of 6.2 points. Among the five highest-scoring models, the gaps narrowed to 15.2 points for model choice and 9.9 for harness choice.
The results suggest that the underlying model is a stronger predictor of task completion than the harness surrounding it, at least for the models and tasks tested.
But that doesn’t make the harness interchangeable.
ShellBench found that harnesses changed how agents worked, including how many tokens they processed, how often they revised their outputs, and how long they took to complete tasks. The results show substantial differences in execution time even where task-completion rates are close.
For people running agents, the takeaway is that the best configuration depends on model choice, execution time, available tools, and the way an agent handles your work as a collective whole.
6. Open-source harnesses hold their own
If the underlying model matters more than the harness, that raises another question: does using an open-source harness mean trading away performance? ShellBench confirmed it did not.
Across the models run in all five harnesses, average pass rates were tightly grouped:
OpenClaw (open source): 45.23%
Pi (open source): 45.22%
Hermes (open source): 45.11%
Codex CLI (open source): 45.04%
Claude Code (proprietary): 42.87%
That’s a spread of 2.36 percentage points between the highest- and lowest-scoring harness averages, showing that open-source harnesses can deliver competitive task completion with a proprietary alternative when tested on the same models.
OpenClaw and Pi were effectively tied at the top, just 0.01 percentage points apart. Pi was fastest at a median of 1.31 minutes per run, while OpenClaw took 2.86 minutes.
7. Capability and cost don’t always move together
The three highest-performing models were separated by just 1.1 percentage points in average pass rate, but the estimated cost per run varied considerably. GPT-6 Astra passed 62.5% of tasks at $1.10 per run, Claude Fable 5.2 passed 61.9% at $0.83, and GPT-6.1 Sol passed 61.4% at $0.19.
That puts GPT-6.1 Sol within 1.1 points of the highest pass rate at roughly one-sixth the estimated per-run cost. At the lower end, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash passed 39.7% of tasks at about five cents per run. (Estimates use recorded token usage and study token prices and exclude grading costs.)
When considering model choices, the ratio of price, capability and the requirements of the task should all be taken into consideration together.
What this means for the industry
We didn’t build OpenClaw around the idea that one model or one agent configuration would always be the best.
Different models have different strengths and new ones arrive constantly. People should be able to choose what runs their agent, where it runs, and which tools it can use.
ShellBench is designed for this reality.
Moreover, openness and capability aren’t mutually exclusive. People should be able to choose an open, extensible system without assuming they’ll have to settle for substantially weaker results. And performance alone doesn’t settle which harness someone should use.
The results also expose work that still needs to be done, particularly around completeness, reliability, safety, and execution time. As we build the agentic future in the open, we want to measure those gaps openly, and help the rest of the industry do the same.
ShellBench is intended to give researchers, model developers, harness maintainers, and the broader community a shared way to test everyday agent capabilities, investigate failures, and build toward more reliability.
We’ll continue updating the benchmark as more results become available and new models emerge.
