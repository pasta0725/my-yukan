European survey reveals 900m galaxies – and possibly secrets of ‘dark universe’
Unprecedented view of cosmos provided by Euclid mission throws up many more cases of gravitational lensing
A mission to create the largest ever 3D map of the universe has revealed more than 900m galaxies in its first major data release together with about 15,000 potential cases of a phenomenon that could help unlock the secrets of the “dark universe”.
Launched in July 2023, the European Space Agency’s 
Euclid mission
 promised to create the largest cosmic map ever made, encompassing a third of the sky and more than 10bn years of cosmic history.
The goal is to shed light on two of the biggest mysteries in the universe: the nature of dark energy and dark matter. These are thought to make up about 70% and 25% of the universe respectively.
Prof Andy Taylor, of the University of Edinburgh, who leads the UK science ground segment for the mission, said while there were bigger surveys, none had the detail and the information content of Euclid. By contrast the Hubble telescope has produced images of incredible resolution, but does not cover as large an area of the sky.
“The technological leap that’s Euclid is that we can keep that high resolution, but now we can basically map huge volumes of the sky, huge volumes of the universe,” Taylor said.

Now, the first major Euclid data release has landed, offering researchers an unprecedented view of the cosmos. “It’s the biggest high-resolution image of the universe we’ve ever had,” Taylor said.
Covering about 14% of the total area of sky that will be mapped by Euclid, the newly released data captures over 900m galaxies as well more than 60m potential quasars: blazing objects at the centre of distant galaxies, powered by supermassive black holes.

It also includes a tranche of candidates for what was previously considered a rare phenomenon: strong gravitational lensing. This is a magnification effect that occurs when one galaxy, often embedded in a galaxy cluster, sits in front of another, distant galaxy.
As Taylor notes, Einstein predicted the gravitational influence of the foreground galaxy would distort space-time, bending the light from the background galaxy and creating the appearance of bright arcs or rings. Crucially, the effect is significantly boosted by the presence of dark matter, which increases the mass and hence gravitational influence of a galaxy.
Taylor said that by studying strong gravitational lensing, scientists may be able to rule out different theories on the nature of dark matter. That is because the lensing is influenced by how this mysterious substance is distributed in a galaxy – that itself depends on its properties.
Stephen Serjeant, a professor of astronomy at the Open University, agreed. “With strong gravitational lensing, you find out how the dark matter is clumping and that’s very diagnostic of the type of matter that it is, because different types of matter can clump in very different ways,” he said.
“So if you’re looking for where the next breakthrough will be in the properties of dark matter and what dark matter actually is, there’s a good chance – I can’t say it’s certain – but there’s a good chance it might come from strong gravitational lensing.”
Taylor noted that while experts previously knew of about 1,000 cases of strong gravitational lensing, the Euclid data contains about 15,000 probable cases, with an estimated 170,000 expected to be discovered by the end of the mission.

A further release of data was expected to land next summer, Taylor added, which will include results on both the distribution of galaxies as well as weak gravitational lensing – subtle distortions in distant objects by dark matter in the foreground.
“We can use that pattern of distortions to actually make a map of where all the dark matter is in the universe,” said Taylor, adding that scientists could then work out its statistical properties and compare the results with different theories. “Depending on the type of dark matter, the type of dark energy, we’re expecting to see slightly different patterns on the sky.”
And with Euclid generating ever-more data, Taylor noted there was plenty to explore.
“It’s opening up that high resolution of the whole sky, the whole universe. So it just means there are plenty of objects that we’ll never have seen before, plenty of research phenomena,” he said. “No human eyeball is able to look at all of the Euclid data any more. It’s now too big.”

A telescope tasked with creating the 
largest-ever 3D map of the universe
 is making good on its promise.
On Thursday (Nov. 12), the European Space Agency (ESA) shared the first major data release from its 
Euclid space telescope
, including observations of more than 900 million galaxies and 60 million black-hole-powered light sources in the early universe.
"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," 
Carole Mundell
, ESA's director of science, said in a 
statement
. "This major Euclid data release represents the largest space-based survey of its kind, laying the foundation of future discovery."
Captured over 13 months, this cornucopia of data covers a slice of sky about 9,500 bigger than the full moon — about 14% of the total sky area that Euclid will survey on its planned six-year mission. Each object in the survey is like a "puzzle piece," with its specific light signature, or spectrum), offering clues about its composition and distance from Earth, according to ESA.
Scientists can now start using the 1.7 million gigabytes of Euclid data to slot those objects into a 3D model of the universe and begin to map out the vast cosmic web that underlies all galaxies. But the data release also includes some stunning space images, which you can enjoy immediately. Here are some of the highlights.
A record haul of "Einstein rings"
A mosaic of 9 gravitational lenses spotted by the Euclid telescope.
(Image credit: ESA/ Euclid Consortium)
Gravitational lenses are nature's biggest magnifying glasses. These "
Einstein rings
" ‪—‬ named after 
Albert Einstein
, who predicted their existence — occur when exceptionally massive objects, like large galaxies, bend the fabric of space-time. This cosmic curvature causes the light emitted by objects behind them to appear magnified, distorted and sometimes duplicated.
In its first data release, Euclid has discovered more than 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates. These cosmic lenses enable astronomers to 
get much clearer views of the deep universe
 than any telescope could possibly see on its own.

"Over 10 years ago, we forecast that Euclid would revolutionise the field of strong lens discovery," Natalie Lines, a Euclid Consortium member working at the University of Portsmouth in the UK, said in the statement. "That day is finally here."
Grand design
The "grand design" spiral galaxy M81, imaged by Euclid.
(Image credit: ESA/ Euclid Consortium)
The Messier 81 galaxy (seen on the right) is one of the brightest in the sky. With fully developed spiral arms stretching to its bright core, M81 is known as a "grand design spiral" galaxy.
While M81 is relatively close to Earth and can be seen with a 
good pair of binoculars
, Euclid's field of view captures it alongside the elliptical galaxy NGC 3077 (bottom left) and crisp clouds of interstellar dust.
Golden Eye
The ring galaxy NGC 1291, imaged close up at Euclid's highest resolution.
(Image credit: ESA/ Euclid Consortium)
Among the nearly 1 billion galaxies charted by Euclid is NGC 1291, also called the Golden Eye galaxy. The galaxy is located 33 million light-years from Earth, and Euclid shows a close-up view of it here at the telescope's highest resolution, according to ESA.
Ring galaxies like this contain a central bulge disconnected from a surrounding ring of stars; astronomers think these galaxies form when a smaller galaxy plunges through the middle of a larger one.
Filling the map
The first piece of the Euclid Wide Survey, covering about 14% of the total sky area that the telescope plans to survey.
(Image credit: ESA/ Euclid Consortium)
This image shows the entire sector of the Euclid Wide Survey, the telescope's mission to map vast swatches of the northern sky. The Wide Survey is accompanied by the Euclid Deep Survey, which will spend about 10% of the telescope's observing time taking repeated observations of three relatively small spots of sky, according to 
ESA
.
With about 85% of its survey left to go, Euclid is on track to collect one of the most comprehensive datasets of the universe ever. Now, it'll be up to the scientific community to make good use of those observations and find something extraordinary.

On Thursday (Nov. 12), the Euclid space telescope released its first major batch of data, capturing the light of more than a billion objects over an area of sky as big as 9,500 full moons. The data release lays the foundation for one of the largest-ever 3D maps of the cosmos and many groundbreaking discoveries about the nature of dark energy and dark matter.
The 
European Space Agency
's (ESA) 
Euclid
 space telescope has been called the "Dark Universe Detective" because of its capability to investigate the dual mysteries of 
dark matter
, the universe's most mysterious stuff, and 
dark energy
, the force accelerating the expansion of the universe. Collectively, these cosmic elements are sometimes referred to as the dark universe.
The new data release, designated DR1-Foundation, was built from 13 months of observations and is accompanied by a multitude of images, including large sky mosaics. It marks the first major release of mission data since 
Euclid launched in mid-2023
.
The core of the Messier 81 galaxy group as seen by Euclid. 
(Image credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin)
"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," ESA's Director of Science, Carole Mundell 
said in a statement
. "This major Euclid data release represents the largest space-based survey of its kind, laying the foundation of future discovery in a new regime for astronomy and demonstrating the excellence of European technology, science and international collaboration."
What's in the Euclid release
The ESA put into raw numbers the sheer number of celestial objects viewed by the Dark Universe Detective and contained in DR1-Foundation. That includes light spectra from 160 million sources, over 900 million galaxies, and 60 million candidates for 
quasars
, regions of space dominated by feeding 
supermassive black holes
.
"This is a change in scale from Euclid's previous releases, which included a few days of observations over around 60 degrees squared of sky," ESA's Euclid project scientist Valeria Pettorino said. "We're now releasing more than a year of data covering a significantly larger area. This unlocks groundbreaking science."
NGC 1291, a striking ring galaxy lying around 33 million light-years away from us, as seen by Euclid. 
(Image credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin)
Euclid can see such a vast number of cosmic objects thanks to the fact that its space-based position helps it avoid interference caused by Earth's atmosphere.
This includes clusters of galaxies and even larger superclusters. The study of these objects, some of the largest structures in the cosmos, can reveal how matter came together across the larger network of gas, dust and dark matter called the 
cosmic web.
"Euclid doesn't just look far back in time  — it does so across huge areas of sky," Euclid Consortium member Barbara Sartoris of JL Lagrange Laboratory, Nice, France, explained. "This gives us an unprecedented view of large samples of ancient galaxy clusters that were previously out of reach
. 
"We will explore these in the coming months to learn how these giants grew, and decipher what they reveal about the dark universe."
The Horologium supercluster of galaxies as seen by Euclid. 
(Image credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin)
Another exciting element of the new Euclid data release is the 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates catalogued by the space telescope. Gravitational lensing is an element of the universe first explained in Einstein magnum opus,
general relativity
, in 1915.
General relativity says that objects with mass cause the very fabric of space to warp, and that gravity arises from this warping. But there is another consequence. When light passes this curvature, its usually straight path is curved. That means when light from a single background source passes an object with great mass, like a galaxy or galaxy cluster, a strong gravitational lens, it can arrive at our telescopes at different times.
As a result of this strong lensing, background sources can be greatly amplified. It is an effect that the 
James Webb Space Telescope
 (JWST) has been using to great effect to study galaxies in the early universe.
Gravitational lenses observed by the Euclid telescope. 
(Image credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by N. Lines and T. Li)
Thus, the prospect of mapping 15,000 strong gravitational lenses is monumental, especially in studying the rate at which the universe is expanding and thus the effect of dark energy.
"Strong lensing provides unique insight into the dark universe, allowing us to 'weigh' individual galaxies, including their invisible dark matter, and explore how the universe is expanding," Natalie Lines of the University of Portsmouth, U.K., and member of the Euclid Consortium, said. "Over 10 years ago, we forecast that Euclid would revolutionize the field of strong lens discovery. That day is finally here."
The best is yet to come
Arguably, the most exciting thing about DR1-Foundation is that Euclid is just getting started. The area of sky covered from spring 2024 to spring 2025 represents just 14% of Euclid's target for its entire six-year survey.
That means whatever scientists discover from this data release could be the tip of the iceberg in terms of how the Dark Universe Detective will change our understanding of the cosmos.
The area of sky covered from spring 2024 to spring 2025, just 14% of the mission’s target for the entire six-year survey. 
(Image credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA; ESA/Gaia/DPAC; ESA/Planck Collaboration)
"DR1-Foundation includes raw data, calibrated images, catalogues, and spectra over a huge sky area, with enough statistics touncover even rare objects," Valeria said. "It will be used to map the large-scale structure of the universe in unprecedented detail.
"So much discovery is yet to come."

The ESA's Euclid has already teased us with some early results. In the past it's given us a small, 60 square degree of the sky created with a few days of observations. Those images showed individual targets from the Horsehead Nebula to the Perseus cluster of galaxies. 
But now we have data from more than one year of the telescope's observations, named DR1-Foundation. DR-1 Foundation covers a sky area as large as about 9,500 full moons, and captures light from more than one billion objects. It took about 13 months to gather all this data. 
Understanding dark energy and dark matter means studying as much of the Universe as possible. The effects of dark energy and dark matter are felt at large scales, and by constructing an image of galaxy superclusters and the filaments they exist on, as well as the voids between those filaments, Euclid will lead to new understandings of both dark energy and dark matter.
There are other efforts to map this structure, some dating back decades. DESI, the Dark Energy Survey Instrument, has created the largest 3D map so far, but Euclid's will be larger. Euclid's will have more depth and volume, and since it's space-based while DESI is ground-based, it won't have to deal with atmospheric interference and will have a higher resolution.
This data release is from the Euclid's Wide and Deep Surveys, two surveys that will form the backbone of its 3D map. Even though the data covers 1900 square degrees of the sky, they only represent about 14% of the sky the space telescope will cover in its six-year mission. 
This image shows Euclid's Wide Survey in light yellow and its Deep Survey in darker yellow. The Deep Survey are targeted regions within the Wide Survey. They're overlain on a star map from Gaia and a dust map from Planck. Image Credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA; ESA/Gaia/DPAC; ESA/Planck Collaboration. Licence: CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO or ESA Standard Licence
The Wide Survey is Euclid's primary campaign and will cover almost half of the galactic sky. It's an imaging and spectrographic survey in visible and near-infrared. It's the telescope's main cosmology survey and will cover a large volume to constrain dark energy and dark matter. 
The Deep Survey serves a different purpose. It'll find fainter objects like dwarf galaxies, low-mass galaxies, and tidal tails, as well as high redshift galaxies and quasars. It will serve to calibrate the Wide Survey, and will provide its own, separate science based on its higher density of sources. 
Basically, the Wide Durvey provides the greater volume, and the Deep Survey looks more closely and gives more depth. The two combined are needed for a cosmological survey. 
Part of the release includes Euclid's image of the core of the Messier 81 galaxy group. This image shows part of that, centered on the Messier 81 galaxy, the group's largest member. The smaller NGC 3077 is on the left, which is also a member, as are many other small members spread across the image. Wispy blue clouds, sometimes called 'galactic cirrus clouds' are also visible. An irregular dwarf galaxy is also visible in diffuse blue to the left. Image Credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin. Licence: CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO or ESA Standard Licence
Euclid also delivered an image of NGC 1291, a stunning ring galaxy about 33 million light years away. It's also called the Golden Eye galaxy, and it has an inner ring and an outer ring, as well as an inner bar. It's inner and outer rings may be because of a merger with a gas-rich galaxy in the past. 
This is Euclid's image of NGC 1291, a ring galaxy that faces us head-on. It's about 33 million light years away, and highlights Euclid's power. Image Credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin. Licence: CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO or ESA Standard Licence
Euclid also captured images of Abell 3128. It's 800 million light years away and contains hundreds of individual galaxies. Massive clusters like this one are important probes for dark energy and dark matter, but especially for dark matter. They serve as important gravitational lenses and reveal a lot about where dark matter is concentrated. They can help cosmologists test Lambda-Cold Dark Matter, which is the standard Big Bang theory of the Universe. They also help Euclid calibrate itself. 
This is the Abell 3128 cluster, part of the Horologium supercluster. Horologium is home to more than 30 smaller clusters, and Abell 3128 is one of its richest members. Abell holds spirals and ellipticals, and a gravitational lens is in the middle, warping and magnifying the light from a distant background galaxy. The image shows only a fraction of Abell 3128, and also part of another galaxy cluster named ACT-CL J0330-5227; while in reality the two clusters are separated by a huge distance of around 5 billion light-years, they overlap here in our line of sight. Image Credit: ESA/Euclid/Euclid Consortium/NASA, image processing by J.-C. Cuillandre and E. Bertin. Licence: CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO or ESA Standard Licence
We're only at the appetizer of the visual and scientific feast that Euclid will deliver. By the time it's completed, its 3D map of the sky will be visually entrancing and full of science. It's already discovered a new comet, the most ancient quasar in the Universe, and new images of the Milky Way's over-populated center. 
It's off to a strong start and will only get better.
