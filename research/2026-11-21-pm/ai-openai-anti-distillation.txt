OpenAI reports adaptive extraction attacks on protected reasoning fell from 54.0% to 0.2% with updated safeguards.
Exact reasoning traces transfer cyber capabilities more effectively than summaries even when the underlying tasks are permitted.
Defending reasoning requires controls spanning request classifiers tool-call arguments conversation history and account-level campaign enforcement.
OpenAI on November 20, 2026 
announced stronger defenses
 against adversarial distillation, reporting that the average success rate for adaptive attacks extracting protected reasoning from an earlier request fell from 54.0% on GPT-6 Astra with original safeguards to 0.2% on 
GPT-6.2 Astra
 with updated safeguards.
For extraction from the current request, the corresponding average fell from 22.9% to 0.9%, OpenAI reports. The adaptive black-box evaluation aggregates results across six attacker models, with automated red-teamers adjusting their approach as the system responds and seeing only what an external user would see. The strengthened protections cover GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol, and selected improvements are also being applied to 
GPT-6 Astra
.
Protected reasoning is the intermediate chain-of-thought a model generates while working toward an answer. These traces can contain information omitted from final responses, making them especially valuable to adversarial distillation, which uses a frontier model’s outputs without authorization to train another system. The resulting model may reproduce dual-use capabilities without the safeguards that constrained the original.
What the Cyber-Distillation Study Found
OpenAI’s internal study compared two training runs from the same pretrained student model, the learner in a setup where a teacher model produces the training data. One run used exact teacher reasoning traces; the other used the user-visible baseline of final responses, reasoning summaries and tool interactions. The fine-tuning data consisted primarily of Astra-level teacher trajectories on coding and defensive cybersecurity tasks. Students were evaluated after supervised fine-tuning and again after lightweight reinforcement learning in cybersecurity and coding environments.
After supervised fine-tuning, the exact-trace student generally outperformed the summary-trained student, OpenAI reports. After reinforcement learning, the exact-trace student scored higher at the largest tested inference budget on both reported benchmarks, while the summary-trained student scored higher at lower budgets.
OpenAI interprets the results as evidence that protecting exact traces can limit cyber-capability transfer even when the underlying tasks are permitted under its usage policies, while saying it will continue studying transfer across different student models and training recipes. Across many such interactions, exact traces can teach skills that generalize beyond the teacher’s original tasks.
Request Controls and Campaign Enforcement
At the request level, adversarial training teaches models to recognize extraction attempts and refuse to disclose protected reasoning while preserving legitimate explanations and assistance. Input classifiers look for extraction attempts, while output classifiers inspect assistant messages and tool-call arguments in streaming and non-streaming responses. Tool-call coverage matters because reasoning can appear in a tool’s arguments without appearing in the final answer; output classifiers can block suspected leaks before they reach the caller.
The updated API ties protected reasoning in GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol to the conversation history that produced it. A model can build on earlier reasoning as messages are added, but rewriting an earlier message drops the reasoning that followed it. Older model families with weaker safeguards cannot read the two models’ protected reasoning, restricting reuse across model-family boundaries.
Campaign-level defenses use request and account signals to investigate coordinated extraction, and attacks uncovered during investigations become examples for safeguard training and testing. OpenAI said weekly enforcement actions against unauthorized distillation campaigns in 2026 have ranged from thousands to tens of thousands of accounts, with the rate increasing significantly during summer 2026. The company tracks abuse prevalence and detection-to-enforcement time, aiming to reduce the data attackers obtain, raise campaign costs and shorten response times.
The announcement follows OpenAI’s 
September 30, 2026 disclosure
 of a coordinated campaign first observed on July 1, 2026. The earlier report described 16,000 attempted extraction requests from more than 4,000 users on July 24 and 25, 2026, attributed a core cluster to individuals associated with Moonshot AI, and said it was unclear whether all operators belonged to one actor.
Coordination Beyond OpenAI
OpenAI shares indicators of compromise with other model providers and works with deployment partners on safeguard requirements and coordinated enforcement. Its recommendations for other developers include checking assistant messages and tool-call arguments, restricting encrypted-reasoning reuse across conversations and models, retaining successful attacks as regression tests for future safeguards, and pairing request controls with campaign investigations. The company plans to share more about recently disrupted campaigns.
OpenAI has told Congress it supports the Chip Security Act, the AI OVERWATCH Act and the MATCH Act, alongside broader legislation that would give the U.S. government more tools to identify and sanction foreign actors illicitly extracting capabilities from American AI models.
The company argues that export controls and distillation defenses address different parts of that risk: export controls limit access to hardware needed to train frontier models, while distillation defenses make it harder to acquire some of the same capabilities from models already trained.
For model providers, the practical security boundary extends beyond final answers to how reasoning is reused across conversations, exposed through tool calls and monitored across accounts. The study also suggests that permitted coding and defensive-cybersecurity tasks can create a capability-transfer risk, making interaction-wide protection and campaign enforcement parts of the same defense.

リクエスト単位の対策
モデルの振る舞いへの防御的な学習。
 敵対的な学習により、モデルは推論の取り出しの試みを見分け、保護された推論の開示を拒否する一方、説明や支援を求める正当な依頼への回答は保つ、と公式は説明しています。学習の例は、内部の自動レッドチーム、調査、外部の研究者や業界パートナーからの責任ある開示で見つかった、成功した攻撃から来ています。
分類器。
 入力の分類器で、保護された推論を取り出そうとする試みを検知し、出力の分類器で、アシスタントのメッセージやツール呼び出しの引数に、保護された推論が漏れていないかを見ます。ストリーミングでも、そうでない応答でも使います。ツールの引数だけに推論が出て、最終の回答には含まれない場合があるため、出力の分類器は、呼び出し側に届く前に疑わしい漏えいを止められる、としています。
APIの制限。
 保護された推論を、やり取りやモデルをまたいで再利用できる範囲を制限します。9月の公式発表では、暗号化された推論を別の会話へ移し、人が読める形で明らかにさせようとする攻撃が説明されていました。公式は、利用者、ワークスペース、組織、モデル系統の境界をまたぐ保護を強め、他人の暗号化された推論を露出させ得る経路も閉じた、としています。
GPT-6.2 AstraとGPT-6.2 Solでは、保護された推論を、それを生んだ会話の履歴に結び付けます。新しいメッセージが加われば、モデルは以前の推論の上に積み上げられます。一方、途中のメッセージが書き換えられると、APIはその後に続いた推論を破棄します。この扱いは、公式のAPIドキュメント「
Keep the prefix unchanged
」でも、接頭辞の変更に続く推論項目を破棄すると説明されています。加えて、対策の弱い古いモデル系統が、GPT-6.2 AstraとSolの保護された推論を読めないようにしている、とも書かれています。

許可のない不正な蒸留のキャンペーンをシステムが検知すると、公式は関係アカウントを調べます。違反を確認した場合は、方針と利用規約に沿って、そのアカウント網に対して執行します。各キャンペーンの調査から得た知見で、似た動きの検知も上げていく、としています。ほかのモデル提供者と侵害の指標を共有し、相手のサービスでも関連する不正を調べられるようにする、とも書かれています。

Combatting adversarial distillation
How we’re addressing safety risks of distillation and lessons we’ve learned for strengthening safeguards across the industry.
Share
As frontier AI models become more capable, safeguards play an increasingly important role in how they can be used responsibly. Adversarial distillation creates a path for bad actors to reproduce these capabilities without appropriate safeguards. An actor may use a frontier model’s outputs without authorization to train another model with similar capabilities, then deploy it without comparable protections. For instance, an adversary might distill a model’s cyber capabilities and use the resulting model to help carry out cyberattacks that the original model would have refused to assist with or that its safeguards would have blocked.
We’re 
sharing how OpenAI detects and disrupts adversarial distillation, how we’ve strengthened protections for our models’ protected reasoning, and what we’ve learned that can help strengthen defenses across the industry.
Our approach to adversarial distillation 
combines adversarial testing, model training, classifiers, and API-level protections with investigations into coordinated attempts to extract model capabilities. These updated safeguards 
align more closely with those we use for biology and cybersecurity.
We place particular emphasis on preventing attackers from extracting our models’ protected reasoning: the intermediate steps a model generates internally while working toward an answer, known as its chain-of-thought. These traces can contain information the final answer leaves out, making them especially valuable training data for another model.
W
e’ve strengthened our defenses against protected reasoning extraction in
 GPT‑6.2 Astra and GPT‑6.2 Sol. In an internal adaptive evaluation using 
strong automated red-teaming
, 
we tested whether attackers could recover protected reasoning the model had generated while answering an earlier prompt. The average attack success rate fell from 54.0% against GPT‑6 Astra with its original safeguards to 0.2% against GPT‑6.2 Astra with its updated safeguards.
Why distillation is a safety issue
Distillation is a common machine-learning technique in which one model learns from data produced by another. For example, a model can learn from another model’s answers, its reasoning, or 
its judgments about which answers are better and why
. Beyond intellectual property concerns, unauthorized distillation poses 
serious
 safety risks. Through adversarial distillation, a second model can learn advanced frontier capabilities without inheriting the safeguards that govern how the original model can use them.
The concern is especially clear in dual-use domains like cybersecurity and biology. A model that helps defenders find vulnerabilities in software could also help an attacker exploit them; a model that assists with biological research could also help someone develop a harmful pathogen. If such capabilities were transferred to a model with weaker safeguards, it may provide assistance that the original model would have refused, or that the original safeguards would have blocked.
The importance of protecting exact reasoning traces for cyber safety
Our models help users with coding and defensive cybersecurity tasks. A second model trained on many of those benign outputs may learn skills that also apply to offensive cybersecurity tasks. To better understand this, we study how distillation, especially involving reasoning traces, may transfer risky cyber capabilities.
In this setting, the model that produces the training data is called the teacher, and the model that learns from it is called the student. In an internal study on how cyber capabilities transfer, we used the content normally visible to users—final responses, reasoning summaries, and tool interactions—as our baseline for distillation, and tested how much additional cyber capability transfers when the student has access to the teacher’s exact reasoning traces.
We started each training run from the same pretrained student model. We created a supervised fine-tuning dataset consisting primarily of trajectories from Astra-level teacher models working on coding and defensive cybersecurity tasks. Each trajectory includes all of the steps a model takes as it works through a task, including any tool interactions and its final response. We evaluated each student after fine-tuning and again after a lightweight round of reinforcement learning in cybersecurity and coding environments.
After supervised fine-tuning, the student trained on exact reasoning traces generally outperformed the student trained on reasoning summaries. After reinforcement learning, the exact-trace student still scored higher at the largest inference budget tested on both benchmarks shown, while the summary-trained student scored higher at lower budgets.
1
Our cyber distillation results suggest that protecting reasoning traces can limit cyber capability transfer, even when the underlying task is one allowed under our usage policies
. Across many such interactions, exact reasoning traces can teach a student skills that generalize beyond the tasks the teacher originally performed. We will continue studying how much additional capability transfers across different student models and distillation training recipes.
Defenses that reinforce one another
We defend against adversarial distillation at two complementary levels of our safeguard stack. At the request level, model behavior, classifier checks, and API controls limit reasoning trace extraction. At the campaign level, we look for patterns in request-level and account-level signals that can reveal coordinated extraction attempts. In turn, attacks uncovered in investigations become examples for training and testing our safeguards.
Our defenses against adversarial distillation combine safeguards for individual requests with campaign detection and enforcement. Findings from investigations and automated red-teaming help strengthen those safeguards.
Protecting individual requests and responses
D
efensive training for model behavior
Adversarial training teaches models to recognize reasoning extraction attempts and refuse to disclose protected reasoning, while preserving responses to legitimate requests for explanations and assistance. Adversarial training examples come from successful attacks identified through internal automated red-teaming, investigations, and responsible disclosures of potential security weaknesses from outside researchers or 
industry
 partners.
Classifier safeguards
We use input classifiers to detect attempts to extract protected reasoning, and output classifiers to detect leaks of protected reasoning in assistant messages and tool-call arguments. These safeguards apply to both streaming and non-streaming responses.
Tool-call coverage is important because a model can expose protected reasoning in a tool’s arguments without including it in its final answer. Output classifiers can block suspected leaks before that content reaches the caller.
API-level protections
API-level protections restrict how protected reasoning can be reused across interactions and models. 
Our September disclosure
 described attacks that moved encrypted reasoning from one conversation into another and asked the model to reveal the reasoning in user-readable form. We strengthened protections across user, workspace, organization, and model-family boundaries, including closing a pathway that could expose another user’s encrypted reasoning.
With GPT‑6.2 Astra and GPT‑6.2 Sol, we tie a model’s protected reasoning to the conversation history that produced it. As new messages are added, the model can build on its earlier reasoning. If an earlier message is rewritten, our API 
drops the reasoning that followed it
⁠
(opens in a new window)
. We also prevent older model families with weaker safeguards from reading GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol’s protected reasoning.
Investigating and disrupting coordinated campaigns
When our systems detect unauthorized adversarial distillation campaigns, which often include coordinated attempts to extract our models’ reasoning, we investigate the accounts responsible. When we identify violations, we enforce against the network of accounts, consistent with our policies and terms of service. Through investigations of each campaign, we generate insights that allow us to upgrade our defenses in order to better detect similar activity. We also share indicators of compromise with other model providers so they can investigate related abuse on their own services.
This work extends beyond our own platform. 
Our consistent-standards policy
 establishes shared standards for safeguards and coordinated enforcement across providers offering our models. We work closely with partners where appropriate to uphold these standards and strengthen protections across deployments.
S
haring relevant findings across industry and government where appropriate can help disrupt campaigns and enable model developers and providers to better identify and defend against attempts to distill frontier models
.
Measuring progress
Resistance to reasoning extraction
To measure how well our request-level safeguards work together, we test whether attackers can still extract protected reasoning in a setup that closely mirrors production deployments of our models and safeguards. In static evaluations, we replay a fixed set of previously successful attacks to see which still expose protected reasoning. In adaptive evaluations, automated AI red-teamers adjust their approach based on how the system responds, which can uncover weaknesses the static tests miss.
We also vary what testers can see. Black-box testing gives them the same view an external user would have, while white-box testing gives them additional information about the system to help them develop stronger attacks. In our adaptive black-box evaluation, we tested whether attackers could recover protected reasoning the model had generated while answering an earlier request or the current one. On GPT‑6.2 Astra with its updated safeguards, the average attack success rate was below 1% for each attack type. Both rates were substantially lower than on GPT‑6 Astra with its original safeguards. We’re now applying selected safeguard improvements to GPT‑6 Astra as well.

Campaign detection and enforcement
We have seen multiple unauthorized adversarial distillation campaigns on our platform this year, and shared initial findings in our September 
report
. Our weekly enforcement actions against these campaigns have ranged in scale from thousands to tens of thousands of accounts, with the rate increasing significantly this past summer.
In order to measure the effectiveness of our efforts, w
e also track the prevalence of distillation abuse, as well as the time between detection and enforcement. Our objective is to constrain the volume and value of the data distillers are able to obtain, to increase the cost of distillation campaigns, and to shorten the time needed to respond to new attacks. We continue to improve these defenses through adversarial testing and investigations.
The national security implications of adversarial distillation
Adversarial distillation also poses a national security risk. A foreign actor that reproduces advanced capabilities from a U.S. model can deploy them without the protections its developer put in place. In February, we 
briefed
⁠
(opens in a new window)
 Congress on activity indicating that DeepSeek was attempting to distill U.S. frontier models. In September, we 
reported
 disrupting a campaign to extract our models’ protected reasoning and attributed a core part of that activity to individuals associated with Moonshot AI, the developer of Kimi. We plan to share more soon about recent campaigns we’ve disrupted.
We support legislation that would give the U.S. government more tools to identify and sanction foreign actors that illicitly extract capabilities from American AI models. We’ve also told Congress we support the Chip Security Act, the AI OVERWATCH Act, and the MATCH Act. Stronger export controls can limit adversaries’ access to the hardware needed to train frontier models. Distillation defenses make it harder to acquire some of the same capabilities from models already trained. Both efforts help maintain a U.S. lead in frontier AI, giving developers more room to slow a release when they need time to test safeguards.
How others can build on this work
Supporting the safe proliferation of frontier capabilities is an important goal of our anti-distillation work. Our experience so far suggests several useful steps that other model developers and platforms can take:
Protect private reasoning across the full interaction. Check assistant messages and tool-call arguments for leaks, and restrict how encrypted reasoning can be reused across conversations and models.
Keep successful extraction attack logs found through red-teaming, production monitoring, or responsible disclosure as regression tests. Where appropriate, and 
consistent
 with 
applicable
 privacy policies, use those examples to improve model training and classifiers. When evaluating updated defenses, replay the original attacks and test whether an adaptive red-teamer can still find a way through those defenses.
Combine request-level mitigations with campaign-level investigation and enforcement.
Coordinate safeguard requirements and enforcement with deployment partners, and share expertise with other AI model developers, to ensure a high floor of distillation safeguards across the industry.

. We’ll continue working with researchers, developers, and partners to share what we learn about emerging attacks and effective defenses.
As we make frontier AI broadly available, our defenses against adversarial distillation aim to reduce the risk that its capabilities are reproduced without adequate safeguards, supporting our mission to ensure that artificial general intelligence is deployed safely and benefits all of humanity.
