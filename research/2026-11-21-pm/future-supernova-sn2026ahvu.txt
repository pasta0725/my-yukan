A new supernova has been spotted exploding in the spiral 
galaxy
 NGC 1433, about 50 million light-years from Earth.
What is it?
Researchers have 
discovered a new supernova
 called SN 2026ahvu. A Type Ia supernova, this event took place in a system with two stars orbiting one another (also known as a binary system), in which typically one star is an exploding 
white dwarf
.
During most type Ia supernovae, the white dwarf pulls material away from the other 
star
 until enough accumulates on its surface to create an explosion.
Finding galaxies where stellar explosions are taking place isn't a rarity. However, this 
supernova
 is unique in how early it was captured. If the supernova were starting to dim, researchers might infer that the event was ending. However, 
according to details shared
 when it was first spotted, as well as later observations, this supernova was discovered while getting brighter.
This indicates that the supernova was likely caught early in the process of exploding, providing researchers with a unique opportunity to observe the event as it is taking place. By exploring observations of the galaxy before, during and (in the future) after the supernova event, researchers can investigate this type Ia supernova and how it affected the system in which it took place.
Why is it incredible?
Scientists 
made this discovery
 using ATLAS, or the Asteroid Terrestrial-impact System, which is a robotic astronomical survey made up of telescopes across both hemispheres.
ATLAS is designed to spot incoming near-Earth objects like 
asteroids
 before they can reach Earth. It's essentially an asteroid impact early warning system.
But just because that's what the system is designed for doesn't mean that's all it can do. The system can also spot other objects moving through space and, in this instance, it was able to capture observations of this supernova.
Following its initial discovery, other observations of the supernova were made using NOIRLab's Dark Energy Camera (DECam) and Gemini South telescope.

Astronomers have observed and captured images showing the explosion of a star in a distant galaxy.
The exploding star – known as a 
supernova
 – is named SN 2026ahvu and is located in a spiral galaxy 50 million lightyears from Earth.
Catalogued NGC 1433, the spiral galaxy was observed and imaged by the Dark Energy Camera and the International Gemini Observatory.
The supernova has been caught early – there are 'before' and 'after' images of the galaxy – and it's still approaching its expected maximum brightness.
What's more, the team behind the observations say amateur astronomers should be able to spot the supernova using small to medium telescopes.

An unfolding cosmic explosion
SN 2026ahvu is giving astronomers an opportunity to study a stellar explosion as it happens.
NSF NOIRLab, which operates the Dark Energy Camera, says it's coordinating observations across its facilities and inviting other observatories to join the study.
The supernova's home is NGC 1433, a barred spiral galaxy 50 million lightyears away, which can be seen from Earth in the Southern Hemisphere constellation Horologium.
New images of supernova SN 2026ahvu from the Dark Energy Camera and the Gemini South telescope show how the explosion suddenly appeared as a bright point of light in the galaxy.
Continuing observations show the supernova is changing in appearance from night to night.
Discovery of SN 2026ahvu
Supernova SN 2026ahvu was discovered by the famous Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System (ATLAS), which is perhaps most famous for its discovery of comets, notably its recent discovery of interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS.
The automated observatory found SN 2026ahvu on 2 November 2026.
It was then confirmed to be a young Type Ia supernova, which is an explosion that happens when a white dwarf – the dense remnants of a dead star – pulls in matter from a nearby companion star.
Type Ia supernovae are important because they're 'standard candles'. They always burn with the same brightness, so astronomers can observe how bright they appear from Earth and compare it with how bright they really are, then use that to work out how far they are and thus measure distances across the cosmos.

When discovered, SN 2026ahvu's apparent 
magnitude
 (brightness) was about +17. Within days, that had brightened to +13.5 (in astronomical magnitude, the lower the number, the brighter the object).
The supernova can be seen to the right and slightly below the galaxy’s centre and is expected to reach a maximum brightness of magnitude +10.5 to +12.1 between 18 and 23 November 2026.
This is giving astronomers a rare opportunity to witness the development of the explosion and gather evidence as the expanding debris evolves.
'Before' and 'after' images are allowing astronomers to compare the surrounding environment before and after SN 2026ahvu's explosion.
The studies could even help astronomers learn more about Type Ia supernovae and how they can be used to measure the 
expansion of the Universe
.

A new supernova has been spotted in NGC 1433, a spiral galaxy about 50 million light years away. That's pretty close for supernova observations, and it's an opportunity for detailed follow-up study. It's not a core-collapse supernova, where a massive star has burned through its fuel and collapses and explodes. It's a Type Ia supernova. That means it's happening in a binary star, where the exploding star is a white dwarf.
In type Ia supernova, a white dwarf, called the accretor star, draws material away from another star called the donor star. Material builds up on the surface of the white dwarf until it breaches the 
Chandrasekhar limit
, about 1.44 solar masses. Then the white dwarf explodes and is obliterated. A type Ia supernova can also occur in a double white dwarf binary, where the two stars merge and explode. 
This supernova is named SN 2026ahvu, where the suffix ahvu is a chronological identifier. It signifies that the supernova is the 41,855th transient discovered in 2026. But even though thousands of transients and supernovae have been found this year, this one is still particularly noteworthy for its relative proximity to Earth, and because it was detected early.
SN 2026ahvu was discovered on November 2nd by ATLAS, the Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System. ATLAS is designed and optimized to detect incoming near-Earth Objects shortly before they reach Earth, but it can also detect other transients. 
New transients are recorded on the Transient Name Server (TNS), a transient registry sanctioned by the International Astronomical Union (IAU). The TNS also hosts AstroNotes, where short notes are posted describing new transients when they're discovered. 
The AstroNotes for SN 2026ahvu reads: "ATLAS operates with 4 x 0.5m telescopes in Hawaii (Haleakala and Mauna Loa), Chile (El Sauce) and South Africa (Sutherland), and a fifth unit in Tenerife, Canary Islands. We are robotically surveying the whole sky with a cadence of 1 day between -50 and +50 and 2 days in the polar regions, weather permitting. While carrying out the primary mission for Near-Earth Objects, we search for and publicly report stationary transients to the IAU Transient Name Server." 
"We report a new transient source, most likely a supernova in the galaxy NGC 1433. We are submitting AstroNotes for transients that are either within 100 Mpc, or have some other interesting feature to bring to the community's attention, such as bright nuclear transients, slowly rising or rapidly fading objects."
In this case, the SN's brightness is rising, and that's important for observations. It gives astrophysicists an opportunity to observe the supernova from an earlier point in its evolution. When it was first discovered, its apparent magnitude was 17. A few days later, it was at 13.5. (Lower apparent magnitude means the object is brighter.)

The before images are from the Dark Energy Survey, DECam's primary job. The after image was taken after ATLAS detected the supernova and alerted other astronomers. When supernovae are detected, astronomers are alerted and other telescopes watch closely. In this case, there are new images of SN 2026ahvu from the Dark Energy Camera (DECam) and the Gemini South telescope. 
Type Ia supernovae are typically brightest for about 6 months, but their brightness inevitably drops over time. Their peak brightness lasts several days and is powered by the decay of an unstable nickel isotope, Nickel-56 (
56
Ni.) Then the decay of ⁵⁶Co → ⁵⁶Fe creates a weaker tail of light. 
This simple schematic shows the typical light curve for a Type Ia supernova, relative to the Sun. The peak is primarily due to the decay of nickel-56 (56Ni), while the later stage is powered by 56-Cobalt to 56-Iron, which is stable. Image Credit: By Xenoforme~commonswiki (attribution as on the source page); SVG reconstruction: PJ Geest. - This file was derived from: SNIacurva.png, CC BY-SA 3.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/page/201323073
About 2 or 3 months after the initial explosion, the ejecta will have thinned out and expanded enough to form a nebula. At that point, when astronomers look at the spectra, they're seeing the composition of the entire exploded star, not just its core. It's a fairly well-understood progression, but there's always room for more observations and more refinement. 
Type Ia supernovae are important because they serve as 
standard candles
 in the cosmic distance ladder. It's the system astronomers use to find distances to celestial objects that are way too distant for more direct measurements like parallax. So the more opportunities they have to study Type Ia supernovae, the more accurate the cosmic distance ladder is. 
Beyond their scientific importance, supernova remnants are among the most visually stunning objects that exist. 
This is the Crab Nebula, probably the most well-known supernova remnant. The Hubble captured this image of the nebula, showing it less than 1,000 years after its progenitor exploded. So it's possible that our descendants will see something similar when they observe SN 2026ahvu reads a millennium from now. Image Credit: By NASA, ESA, J. Hester and A. Loll (Arizona State University) - A Giant Hubble Mosaic of the Crab Nebula, Public Domain, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/page/516106
More images and observations, included detailed study of how the explosion's light changes over time, will follow. The supernova is also within reach of astrophotographers, so there could be some fantastic images arriving soon.

NSF NOIRLab-operated Facilities Capture Brightening Nearby Supernova
New images from Gemini and the Dark Energy Camera showcase supernova 2026ahvu in coordinated cross-NOIRLab observing effort to track the explosion’s evolution
12 November 2026
×
Newly discovered supernova SN 2026ahvu in the nearby spiral galaxy NGC 1433 bedazzles new images from the Dark Energy Camera and the International Gemini Observatory. Caught early and still approaching its expected maximum brightness, SN 2026ahvu gives astronomers a valuable opportunity to study a stellar explosion as it unfolds. NSF NOIRLab is coordinating observations across the facilities it operates, working with its partners to maximize scientific return, and inviting other observatories to join the effort.
A star’s explosive demise has added a shiny new feature to NGC 1433, a barred spiral galaxy approximately 50 million light-years away in the southern constellation 
Horologium
 (the Pendulum Clock) 
[1]
. New images of supernova 
SN 2026ahvu
 from the U.S. Department of Energy-fabricated Dark Energy Camera (
DECam
) and the 
Gemini South telescope
 capture the explosion against its galactic surroundings, recording an event that is changing in appearance from night to night.
DECam is mounted on the U.S. National Science Foundation 
Víctor M. Blanco 4-meter Telescope
 at Cerro Tololo Inter-American Observatory (
CTIO
) in Chile, a Program of NSF NOIRLab. Gemini South is one half of the 
International Gemini Observatory
, an international collaboration led by the NSF, in partnership with Canada, Chile, Brazil, Argentina, and the Republic of Korea, and operated by NSF NOIRLab.
The Asteroid Terrestrial-impact Last Alert System (ATLAS) discovered the object on 2 November 2026. Initially designated AT 2026ahvu, it was subsequently confirmed through 
spectroscopy
 to be a young 
Type Ia supernova
 — an explosion of a white dwarf that has accreted matter from a close companion star — and received the designation SN 2026ahvu 
[2]
. At discovery, its 
apparent magnitude
 was approximately 17; within days, observations reported it at magnitude 13.5 
[3]
. 
The supernova, visible to the right and slightly below the galaxy’s center, is expected to reach a maximum brightness of magnitude 10.5–12.1 between 18 and 23 November 
[4]
. Its discovery well before reaching peak brightness, combined with its relative proximity, gives astronomers the rare and valuable opportunity to witness the early development of the explosion and gather evidence that can quickly disappear as the expanding debris evolves. This is one of the nearest Type Ia supernova observed since 
SN 2011fe
 was discovered in the Pinwheel Galaxy (Messier 101) in 2011.
Type Ia supernovae arise from the thermonuclear destruction of 
white dwarfs
, the dense remnants of stars like our Sun. Although astronomers use these explosions to measure cosmic distances, important questions remain about the stellar systems that produce them and precisely how they ignite. Observations during the earliest stages can help distinguish between possible explosion scenarios and search for signs of interaction with a companion star or surrounding material.
DECam also captured images of NGC 1433 before the explosion during observations for the Dark Energy Survey (
DES
), a survey led by DOE’s Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory, conducted from 2013–2019, to investigate the nature of dark energy. DECam’s images allow astronomers to compare the supernova’s environment pre- and post-explosion. Combined with the sharper Gemini imaging that provides a more in-depth view of the system, these observations demonstrate how combining NOIRLab facilities enables deeper investigation into rapidly changing phenomena in the night sky.
NOIRLab is 
coordinating follow-up observations
 with Blanco/DECam, Gemini, and the Southern Astrophysical Research (
SOAR
) Telescope on Cerro Pachón in Chile. Combining these facilities gives astronomers a more complete picture of the supernova. Repeated imaging tracks how its brightness and color evolve, while spectroscopy shows what the expanding material is made of and how fast it is moving. By following the explosion from its first days through its peak brightness and into its long fade, researchers can learn about the system before it exploded and the mechanism of the explosion. Together, these clues help astronomers refine Type Ia supernovae as a tool for measuring the expanding Universe.
Telescopes on the ground and in space are already turning their attention to SN 2026ahvu. NOIRLab invites other observatories to contribute complementary observations that would extend coverage across wavelengths and time zones. Coordinating these efforts can capture changes that any single facility might miss and help astronomers maximize what can be learned from this nearby explosion.
The event is also within reach of amateur astronomers. At its reported brightness, SN 2026ahvu can already be seen in small to moderately sized amateur telescopes under favorable conditions, particularly from southern locations where NGC 1433 rises high in the sky 
[5]
. To the eye, the supernova appears as a faint point of light, offering observers the chance to witness the changing glow of an exploding star in another galaxy.
The new DECam and Gemini images capture one moment in that unfolding story. Over the coming weeks, observations from professional and amateur astronomers will help trace its evolution, gathering clues to the physics of stellar explosions and the cosmic distance markers that help us measure our Universe.
Notes
[1] This release adopts an approximate distance of 50 million light-years for NGC 1433. Published distance estimates differ.
[2] ATLAS recorded the 
discovery
 on 2 November 2026 at approximately 04:20 UTC (MJD 61346.181), through a cyan filter. Spectroscopic observations with the Keck II telescope’s DEIMOS instrument on 3 November classified the object as a young Type Ia supernova.
[3] On astronomers’ magnitude scale, smaller numbers indicate brighter objects.
[4] The predicted magnitude and date of maximum brightness are estimates and may be refined as observations continue. The intrinsic brightness of Type Ia supernovae is well known; so much so that they are used as standard candles to measure distances. Thus, observing the maximum apparent brightness of 2026ahvu will improve our knowledge of the distance to NGC 1433. Maximum brightness can occur at different times in different observing filters.
[5] Visibility depends on location, telescope aperture, sky darkness, and atmospheric conditions. Imaging can reveal the supernova with smaller telescopes than are generally needed for visual observation. Its coordinates are right ascension 03h 41m 55.511s and declination −47° 13′ 39.39″ (J2000). Its southern location makes it difficult or impossible to observe from far northern latitudes.
