(status 200)
An artistic representation of the steps of the recent stellar merger
NASA / ESA / CSA / STScI / Leah Hustak (STScI); Science: Viraj Karambelkar (Columbia)
In 2023, astronomers realized that a red giant star in our neighboring 
Andromeda galaxy
 was doing something strange. Sure enough, two years later, the astronomical object exploded.
The researchers rushed to observe the phenomenon, and they sifted through older data to piece together what had happened. Analyses suggest the huge star had gobbled up its smaller stellar companion, marking the first direct observation of this kind of star merger. The findings were described November 18 in the journal 
Nature
.
The eruption, called AT 2025abao, occurred because the two stars shared what’s called a common envelope. Some stars approaching the end of their lives balloon to a huge size, entering a giant phase. As an object 
expands
, its outer layer of gas—or envelope—engulfs anything orbiting it, resulting in odd interactions.  
However, common envelopes are tough to observe because they happen over relatively short timescales of months to years, and usually, just a few stars exist in this phase at a time within a galaxy, writes 
Jason Nordhaus
, an astrophysicist at the Rochester Institute of Technology who did not participate in the work, in 
commentary
 accompanying the study.
Quick fact: Common phenomenon
Experts estimate that nearly a third of all stars go through a common envelope phase at some point during their lives, reports 
New Scientist. 
To investigate the strange event, which occurred in October 2025, researchers obtained data captured just 47 days later by NASA’s 
James Webb Space Telescope
. They also had follow-up observations from other ground- and space-based instruments, as well as data from roughly the past 30 years, which revealed the big star had started brightening in 2018.
Using all that data, the team was able to “reconstruct what happened with quite significant confidence,” 
Sven Buder
, an astrophysicist at the Australian National University who did not participate in the study, tells the 
Australian Broadcasting Corporation
’s Jacinta Bowler.
Computer simulations hinted that the two stars had orbited each other for millennia. Before the eye-catching explosion, the primary star—dubbed “Big Bao” after the eruption’s name—had become enormous. It was seven times more massive, 350 times the radius and 18,000 times brighter than our sun. Its companion, “Little Bao,” on the other hand, probably contained 0.7 times the mass of our sun and was smaller and fainter.
The smaller star spiraled into the bigger one’s envelope, and the two objects took about seven years to unite, which is quite quick in astronomical terms. The merger occurred when the red giant dragged its companion into its core, resulting in a dramatic event resembling “a bomb exploding inside a living star,” study co-author 
Kishalay De
, an astrophysicist at Columbia University, says in a 
statement
.
      Researchers studied the merger, which happened in the Andromeda galaxy, using data from several telescopes.
NASA / ESA / CSA / STScI / Viraj Karambelkar (Columbia), Kishalay De (Columbia); Image Processing: Alyssa Pagan (STScI); Acknowledgment: DSS, Robert Gendler
“We were able to see all different phases of this process—we were able to see the quiescent system, we were able to see the moment the stars touched each other, we were able to see the inspiral, and we were able to see the big outburst in 2025,” lead author 
Viraj Karambelkar
, also an astrophysicist at Columbia University, tells 
New Scientist
’s Leah Crane. “So we now know how this process works far better than we ever had before.”
Now, however, the system is too dusty for scientists to observe the aftermath directly, and it will likely remain so for years, reports 
Science News
’ Meghan Bartels. In the meantime, Karambelkar says they can work to “catch more of these systems even before they erupt.”

Astronomers Watch One Star Swallow Another
By: 
Monica Young
November 19, 2026
 0
Get Articles like this sent to your inbox
For the first time, astronomers have followed the years-long infall of a star as it spiraled into its giant stellar companion — ultimately splashing into its core.
NASA / ESA / CSA / STScI / V. Karambelkar (Columbia) / K. De (Columbia); Image Processing: Alyssa Pagan (STScI); Acknowledgment: DSS /Robert Gendler
Celestial fireworks mark stellar mergers. Two stars might start out circling each other in stable orbits but once they come too close together, one inevitably subsumes the other. The energy released flings out dust-forming gas, giving 
novae a distinctive red hue
 — a phenomenon astronomers have spotted before.
But now, for the first time, astronomers have caught the whole process, collecting observations covering the full, seven-year-long infall of a tiny companion into a red giant star in the Andromeda Galaxy. Viraj Karambelkar (Columbia University) and colleagues report the results in the November 19th 
Nature
.
This event, dubbed AT 2025abao, is unique, says team member Morgan MacLeod (Center for Astrophysics, Harvard & Smithsonian). “We’ve seen stellar mergers before, but we're always seeing the ‘outburst’ at the tail end,” he notes. “Here we got to see the infall in much more detail.” (MacLeod wrote "When Stars Collide" for 
Sky & Telescope
's 
June 2024 issue
.)
The same team led by Karambelkar first noticed an unusual brightening of the red giant star in our sister galaxy in data from Near-Earth Object Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer (NEOWISE). Archival observations showed it had started brightening years ago, back in 2018. (Observations dating back to 1989 showed the star to be not at all variable before that point.) The brightening, they argued, marked the first phase of a giant subsuming a companion star.
The primary star is typical as giants go, but then again, giant stars are weird. This one has seven times the Sun’s mass but spans hundreds of times the Sun’s width. That means that, as the dense stellar core continues to fight gravity by fusing helium atoms, the outer envelope expands outward. The star bloated to the point that it blended into its companion, a star that the researchers estimate to be less than three-quarters of the Sun’s mass.
Once the smaller companion began interacting with the giant star’s outer atmosphere, friction began seeping away its energy and tightening its orbit. As gas changed hands between the stellar pair, the system gradually brightened over several years. By mid-July 2025, when NASA’s 
SPHEREx
 mission imaged the system in mid-July 2025, the already colossal primary star had more than doubled in size.
Hubble's 2004 image of the luminous red nova V838 Monocerotis shows an expanding halo of light around the distant star. The illumination of interstellar dust comes from the red supergiant star at the middle of the image, which gave off a flashbulb-like pulse of light in 2002, likely following the merger with a smaller companion.
NASA / Hubble Heritage Team (AURA / STScI) / ESA
Around lower-mass stars that live billions of years, mergers take place over shockingly short timescales — only a few days. Around bloated, massive stars, though, a companion’s infall takes longer. Years pass as the giant star’s thin outer layers slow the companion in its orbit. Yet, while seven years may sound like a long time for the companion to fall in, that time represents less than 10 orbits around the more massive star.
In October 2025, the system finally erupted, marking the merger as the companion star fell all the way into the red giant’s core. The 
Zwicky Transient Facility
 spotted the eruption. The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory and the Gemini North telescope on Mauna Kea, Hawai'i, also returned fast-turnaround images of the event in November.
The energy released was enough to eject the giant star’s outer layers like some kind of spherical parachute. The James Webb Space Telescope took a turn watching the system in December 2025, catching dust forming in this cooling, out-flowing cloud of gas.
This illustration shows the four major events of a stellar merger, known technically as common envelope evolution. At top left, the two stars are in a stable orbit, surrounded by dusty gas pushed out by the primary star's stellar wind. At top right, The less massive companion star’s orbit began to slow as it encountered gas that was part of the hefty primary star’s atmosphere, and the companion started to spiral into the supergiant’s core. As the companion fell inward between 2018 and 2025, the system brightened. Bottom left shows a sudden eruption in October 2025 that marked the companion's splash into the giant stellar core, ejecting the system's outer layers. Bottom right shows how, In the months after the eruption, gas from the star’s ejected envelope freely expanded at the poles, but not at the equator, where previous ejecta from the more massive star was concentrated.
NASA / ESA / CSA / STScI / Leah Hustak (STScI); Science: V. Karambelkar (Columbia)
Throughout the merger, MacLeod notes, as soon as something happened, the light from that event came out to tell us about it. When stellar mergers happen around lower-mass stars that are still on the 
main sequence
 (that is, the stars are still fusing hydrogen in their cores), everything happens more quickly and in a more compact space — so compact that light takes time to escape. “Everything blurs together on the timescale that it takes light to diffuse out of the ejected gas, which might be something like days to weeks — in other words, as long or longer than the infall itself.”
“What makes this event new, or special, is that it came from a much more extended giant star, with a lower density envelope,” MacLeod adds. “The whole process proceeds slower, but light diffuses out faster to reach us — we get a more instantaneous picture of what’s happening.
“For me, that's what makes this new discovery exciting.”
The observations confirmed theorists’ ideas of how such stellar mergers should happen, says Jason Nordhaus (Rochester Institute of Technology), who wrote a 
perspective piece
 that appeared in the same issue of 
Nature
. Those ideas include 
convection
, the boiling motions of gas that complicate the physics within stellar interiors. The observations of Karambelkar’s team confirm those theories, suggesting convection helps transport energy outward toward the giant star’s surface.
“Karambelkar and co-workers’ discovery has come at a remarkable time,” Nordhaus writes in conclusion. “Upcoming wide-field surveys will take deep snapshots of the sky at repeated intervals, generating unprecedented amounts of time-series data. Facilities such as the Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope should therefore routinely discover common envelopes in hundreds of nearby galaxies.”

概要
　前田啓一 京都大学大学院理学研究科教授や米コロンビア大学などの国際研究グループは、私たちの天の川銀河の隣のアンドロメダ銀河にある連星系で発生した恒星合体現象を観測し、その全貌を明らかにすることに成功しました。
　この連星系は太陽の約7倍の質量を持ち、非常に進化した恒星である漸近巨星分岐星（Asymptotic giant branch star；AGB星）と呼ばれる段階に達した大きな星（主星）と、太陽の約0.7倍の小さな星（伴星）からできています。観測によると、伴星が2018年から約7年間にわたってゆっくりと渦を巻いて主星のガスに落ち込んで徐々に明るくなっていったあと、2025年10月に主星の中心部と合体し、その衝撃で主星の外層ガスの約10%が猛スピードで外へ吹き飛ばされ、一気に約100倍も明るくなる大爆発を起こした結果、高輝度赤色新星と呼ばれる星になる劇的なプロセスが起きていたことがわかりました。
　このような主星が伴星を飲み込む現象は「共通外層進化」と呼ばれます。前田教授らは、コロンビア大学による連星系の特定のあと、京都大学理学研究科附属天文台岡山天文台が持つ「せいめい望遠鏡」を使って2025年10月に起きた爆発を観測し、共通外層進化の結果として恒星合体が起こった可能性を報告しました。その後、米航空宇宙局（NASA）のジェームズ・ウェッブ宇宙望遠鏡による詳細な観測などにより、連星進化における重要な過程を初めて明らかにしました。本研究結果は、天文学の基礎である恒星進化の理解において大きな未解明問題であるとされる、共通外層進化および恒星合体過程の理解に向けた重要な成果として、2026年11月18日に国際学術誌『Nature』に掲載されました。

								NASA Data Clinches First-of-Its-Kind Stellar Merger							
In December 2025, observations from NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope provided the final evidence that allowed researchers to fully reconstruct what happened before, during, and after the merger of a binary star system nicknamed Bao in the Andromeda galaxy.

Researchers recently identified a first of its kind: A star slightly less massive than the Sun spiraled into and merged with a puffy supergiant star at the outskirts of our neighboring Andromeda galaxy. They nicknamed the newly single, potentially “rejuvenated” star Bao. Their NASA-funded discovery was then bolstered by quick-turn observations from NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope that were carefully cross-referenced with extensive data from telescopes in space and on the ground.
“Stellar mergers are very, very difficult to observe in real time because they are hard to predict,” said Viraj Karambelkar, the first author of a new paper in Nature published Wednesday and a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University in New York. “However, we noticed something unusual was going on in 2023 and continued to monitor the system to see what it would do. Then, in 2025, it erupted! Webb observed Bao only 47 days after the stars’ eruption peaked, the earliest ever follow-up of an event like this. The telescope’s data helped us characterize what the system looked like before, during, and immediately after the merger, which is absolutely mind-blowing.”
Image: AT 2025abao in the Andromeda Galaxy
In December 2025, observations from NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope provided the final evidence that allowed researchers to fully reconstruct what happened before, during, and after the merger of a binary star system nicknamed Bao in the Andromeda galaxy.
Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI, Viraj Karambelkar (Columbia), Kishalay De (Columbia); Image Processing: Alyssa Pagan (STScI); Acknowledgment: DSS, Robert Gendler
Before their transformation, the two stars spent millennia placidly orbiting one another as a binary pair. But once the smaller star came into contact with the surface of the giant star, their “unification” happened after only seven orbits (about seven years). Other known binary star mergers, featuring two smaller stars, occurred after thousands of orbits.
“We thought an outburst from a such an extended star would take years,” said co-author Nadia Blagorodnova, an associate professor at the University of Barcelona in Spain. “One day, its brightness started rising and within a week, it peaked!”
“When a companion star begins to interact with the outer surface of the primary star, it produces a subtle effect — a slow brightening, which we clearly saw in Bao’s data,” said co-author Kishalay De, an assistant professor at Columbia University in New York. “As the companion continues its orbit, it keeps peeling off the primary star’s outer layers, and that gas flows away into the surrounding space.”
This is the beginning of a high-stakes showdown some binary stars go through, known as a common envelope evolution. The gas between the stars at this stage is known as a common envelope because its outer layer is shared between them. In this system, the primary star engulfed the smaller star and the companion plummeted, spiraling through the envelope as it was robbed of orbital energy by drag. While Webb’s data proved the stars merged, a merger wasn’t the only possible outcome for this system.
In other systems, both stars survive the process — shedding a lot of mass along the way, but remaining independent in tight orbits. Over time, such surviving pairs can transform into 
neutron stars
 and 
black holes
, explode as 
supernovae
, produce ripples in space-time known as 
gravitational waves
, and create millisecond 
pulsars
.
Hitting “replay”
The team reconstructed what happened to Bao by updating their ongoing simulations. They found the model that matched Webb’s mid-infrared observations, which detailed the dust shed by the more massive primary star over centuries.
The team concluded that prior to the eruption, Bao’s primary star dwarfed the Sun: It was seven times as massive, 350 times the radius, and 18,000 times brighter — making “Big Bao” an appropriate nickname. 
“It was an absolute beast of a star, completely outshining its companion in the data,” said co-author Karel Temmink, a Ph.D. student at Radboud University in Nijmegen, Netherlands, who led the simulations.
Their modeling resulted in the first mass estimate for the smaller, now demolished, companion. Compared to our Sun, they found that “Little Bao” was likely less massive (about 0.7 solar masses), with a slightly smaller radius and fainter.
After revealing the masses of the pair, the model showed there might be leftover energy for Little Bao to “flee” the scene. However, further analysis determined that Little Bao was dragged into Big Bao's core. Their encounter was “almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star,” De said.
What does the new star — now simply Bao — look like today? Only future observations will identify how its “heft” settles.

Reconstructing what happened
While Webb’s data allowed the team to measure how much mass was ejected during this process and how efficient the process of common envelope evolution was, Karambelkar’s team also used observations that span ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared light from several telescopes to watch the events, often as they unfolded. (In fact, they 
published their first paper
 right before the eruption in 2025.)
Bao first caught the team’s attention in February 2023, when they saw an unusual variable star in data from 
NASA’s retired NEOWISE
 (Near-Earth Object Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer) mission. Before 2018, the object had a consistent luminosity. Observations from the 
Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer (WINTER)
 in September 2024 showed that Bao was not only beginning to brighten, but also spew dust.
When 
NASA’s SPHEREx
 (Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer) serendipitously saw Bao fade rapidly in visible light and brighten in infrared light during a routine survey in July 2025, the researchers knew it was an indicator of unusual activity. The 
Zwicky Transient Facility
 detected the eruption on Oct. 17, 2025, watching the stars rapidly brighten and radically heat up.
In November 2025, the team received fast-turn observations from 
NASA’s now-retired Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory
 and 
NOIRLab’s Gemini North telescope
 in Hawaii. Webb’s observations were taken on Dec. 24, 2025, as part of a just-in-time 
Director’s Discretionary Time program
.
New path for future research
The team’s analysis of Bao, formally known as AT 2025abao, offers an approach to reconstructing the events: They conducted their analysis and made observations of the stars’ merger in real time. That allowed them to measure how efficient common envelope evolution is. In other cases, astronomers have worked to carefully reverse-engineer events that may have happened hundreds or even millions of years ago.
The new finding comes about just as NASA’s recently launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is about to conduct vast 
publicly available surveys
. Its 
Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey
 will yield a huge number of new transient events that could benefit from similar analyses. “There’s going to be a large influx of similar discoveries just ahead,” Karambelkar said.
