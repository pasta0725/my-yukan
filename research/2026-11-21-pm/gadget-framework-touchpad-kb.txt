===== https://www.pcworld.com/article/3287093/frameworks-all-in-one-keyboard-wants-to-be-the-go-to-pick-for-makers.html
(status 200)
Framework is one of my favorite PC makers thanks to its repair-friendly ethos and surprisingly open communication with customers. And I am, for my sins, PCWorld’s resident keyboard nut. So the moment I heard Framework was making a keyboard, my ears perked up like a dog that’s heard the can opener. 
Only to have them droop back down again. The Framework Keyboard is not mechanical, as I first assumed. But that doesn’t mean it’s 
bad
, it’s just aiming for a different market. Specifically, the kind of market that needs a keyboard and mouse (touchpad) all in one device. The Framework Keyboard is a lot like the Logitech K400 and similar designs, ideal for PCs in odd places where a mouse won’t fit, like a home theater PC. Or indeed, 
the Steam Machine
. 
Framework sent me a pre-production unit ahead of the planned Q1 release. The look is basic bordering on utilitarian, with slate-gray plastic, no backlighting, and only a device-switching button and a power toggle on top of the regular keyboard keys. The only splash of distinction comes from flipping the keyboard over, where you’ll see a smoke-transparent bottom housing that shows off its guts. That’s very cool, even if I would have preferred a splash of orange or purple. The lack of keyboard feet for an adjustable typing angle is significantly less cool, but I suppose this keyboard is intended to be used in a lap most of the time. 
Michael Crider/Foundry
And…yeah, that’s about it as far as the use case goes. It’s a standard wireless design with multi-device Bluetooth and dongle-based wireless. It has all the standard keys you’d find on a laptop board, plus a function row. It has no separate mouse buttons, the multi-point touchpad “clicks” instead. It’s a little wide because of the form factor…um, it has a USB-C port for charging or data? Yeah, I’m struggling to say much more about the physical design, because it’s 
just 
a keyboard with a touchpad. 
But if you thought Framework wouldn’t have something up its sleeve for its first input accessory design, you’ve got another thing coming. Like the company’s laptops and desktop, you can take this thing apart easily with a Torx screwdriver. Six little black screws are all that stand between you and the inside. From there you can unplug a ribbon cable, and it’s easy to see how the separate components like the battery, charging and control board, main keys, and touchpad can be replaced with a little more screwdriver work. 
Michael Crider/Foundry
That’s about all you can do with this repair-friendly approach, as there’s no real upgrades for these parts. But Framework does intend to sell these components individually, along with releasing Open CAD files, firmware, and schematics for all necessary parts. The idea is that the Framework Keyboard could become the de facto slim, laptop-style board for creators. 
This keyboard also works with the 
ZMK firmware system
, a familiar interface for mechanical keyboard nerds. That makes it much easier to program than most regular keyboards, if they support that feature at all. 
Michael Crider/Foundry
Oh, one last cool thing: Framework sent me a “cradle” for the dongle, which fits into a standard Framework laptop or desktop modular USB-C port. With this extra adapter, the dongle sits flush with the case. Very cool, and something only Framework could do. It’s a separate purchase, and I haven’t been told how much yet. 
Here’s the downer: Framework plans to sell this board for $100 USD when it releases in early 2027. That’s not unreasonable for a repairable design with these components, especially considering its open nature. But it is about double what you’d pay for a similarly equipped combination board and touchpad, even from a major brand like Logitech. And though the keyboard Framework sent me is still a pre-production unit, its rather basic and mushy typing doesn’t make up the difference. Since it already has a steel backing, I can’t see this improving too much in a couple of months.
Michael Crider/Foundry
If all you really need is a keyboard and touchpad for a PC or other gadget in a tricky spot, I don’t see a reason to pay double. The 3D printing folks might 
love 
it, and they already love Framework…but I think even they have cheaper options if they want these parts, not least of which are the parts that Framework already sells. I’d be a lot more enthusiastic about it if the price were knocked down 20 bucks or so. 
Framework’s Keyboard is up for pre-order now, with a refundable $50 deposit. It costs $100 in the U.S., $139 in Canada, €119 in the European Union, and £99 in the UK. Availability is “Q1 2027,” so you’ll have some time to work out if it’s worth the price for you or not.

===== https://tech.yahoo.com/computing/articles/tried-frameworks-99-keyboard-killed-161722270.html
(status 200)
Framework Wireless Touchpad Keyboard - Credit: Joseph Maldonado
The first Framework laptops arrived in 2021, and in just five years, the company has proven itself a worthy competitor, especially in the modular 
13-inch
 and 
16-inch
 categories. But Framework isn't stopping at laptops, broadening its horizons with the upcoming Framework Wireless Touchpad Keyboard ($99.00). Designed to replace the cheap 
keyboards
 found in many home entertainment centers, it comes with a built-in touchpad, Bluetooth and 2.4GHz wireless connectivity, and a USB-C port for wired use. Although it's not as modular as its laptop cousins, the keyboard has a replaceable battery and uses open-source ZMK firmware so you can make it your own.
Framework reached out ahead of the keyboard's debut next year to give me some hands-on time with a preproduction unit. Even in its early state, the keyboard has impressive depth beneath the surface. At first glance, it's a handy 2-in-1 cordless keyboard, but DIY enthusiasts and custom keyboard junkies will love its open-source firmware and the potential it holds.
Wait, Where's the Mouse? It's Built In
Out of the box, the keyboard looks unassuming: a simple gray chassis with white legends, measuring 0.6 by 14 by 4.6 inches (HWD) and weighing just 1.06 pounds. In terms of layout, it resembles a 
65% keyboard
 but with a touchpad that adds a few extra inches of width.
Advertisement
Advertisement
Framework states that the keys are lifted straight from its laptops. They're chiclet-style, low-profile keys with just enough tension to make typing satisfying, likely thanks to the thin steel plate tucked beneath them. It isn't as thin as, say, an 
Apple Magic Keyboard
, but as someone who has never been a fan of Apple's wafer-thin boards, it won me over fairly quickly. 
The touchpad measures 2.7 by 3.3 inches, which gave me enough space to fit three fingers comfortably. The pad is smooth and responsive, supporting multi-finger gestures on Linux and Windows, as well as most Mac gestures in Mac Mode. It took a few strokes to drag the mouse from one side of the screen to the other on my 32-inch 
monitor
, which proved tiring over time, so I was eager to increase the sensitivity, but I couldn't—at least not on this preproduction unit.
Turning it over, the keyboard sports a see-through casing and two rubber strips that run its length. There are no adjustable feet, but the top half of the back is slightly elevated, giving it tilt. While I prefer adjustable feet, most thin keyboards I've tested lack additional height adjustments, so the bump is better than nothing. It's not dissimilar to the 
Logitech Signature Slim Solar+ K980 Keyboard
 in that regard, another slim, wireless keyboard. Also tucked away on the back is a 2.4GHz USB-A dongle (more on that in a bit).
In use, the Wireless Touchpad Keyboard's layout reminds me of 
ergonomic boards
 like the 
RollerMouse
 and 
SliderMouse
, though hardly as avant-garde. The slender, lightweight frame and wireless connectivity make it easy to recommend if you're traveling or need to quickly connect a keyboard and mouse to a device.
Built for the Couch, Great Everywhere Else
Framework designed this keyboard to replace the cheap keyboard-and-mouse combos that entertainment enthusiasts keep in their living rooms for HTPCs (home theater PCs), smart TVs, and devices like the 
Steam Machine
. This is the keyboard to break out when you need to handle prompts that a typical TV remote or game controller can't handle, or can only handle clumsily (think navigating settings menus or typing in-game messages).
These use cases aren't as niche as you might think. I review many 
gaming handhelds
, and one thing they all have in common is that navigating Windows is frustrating. However, thanks to the keyboard's included touchpad, I didn't need to fish around for a spare mouse or keyboard. I simply connected the Framework to access a mouse 
and 
keyboard, effectively killing two birds with one board.
Advertisement
Advertisement
I got excited thinking of the other possibilities. I play Final Fantasy XIV across multiple platforms, and I think the Framework would make an excellent keyboard to pair with the 
PlayStation 5
. Although the MMO is very playable with a PlayStation 5 controller, chatting with friends and hopping between menus is still best done with a keyboard and mouse. While I couldn't connect the keyboard wirelessly due to the PlayStation's lack of Bluetooth 6.0 support, Framework's wired option provides a quick, easy way to navigate menus and communicate with party members without clearing desk space for a separate keyboard and mouse.
The keyboard is pretty versatile. You can connect via a USB-C cable or wirelessly using Bluetooth or 2.4GHz. The keyboard supports up to four different wireless connections at once, so you can easily hop between devices with a press of the channel switch button located just above the touchpad. 
A Keyboard You're Actually Allowed to Hack
Although the keyboard isn't as modular as any of the Framework laptops, it offers a fair bit of customization not normally found in a board of this type. For example, you can easily remove the 1,200mAh rechargeable battery. In terms of battery life, Framework states that the keyboard will last more than a month with daily use and more than a year when idle. My preview unit came with a USB-A Expansion Card (available for purchase separately in early 2027, though the price isn't confirmed), so you can use the 2.4GHz USB-A dongle on a system with USB-C ports.
What the keyboard lacks in physical modularity, it makes up for in software. What really sets the Framework apart from other keyboards is its customization options. The Framework uses ZMK Studio, an open-source remapping tool accessible in your 
web browser
—no download required. What's more, because the firmware is built on an open-source fork of ZMK, advanced users can dive deeper and edit the keyboard's config directly, changing anything the tool doesn't already cover and giving them complete version control.
Advertisement
Advertisement
Framework plans to take this a step further. The keyboard's control board schematic, pinout, and bootloader will be published on GitHub. So if you buy the control board, you can mimic the Wireless Touchpad Keyboard's functionality and essentially make it the "brain" of your own 
custom keyboards
. The framework will also enable open-source surface CAD, allowing you to design and 3D print mounts and accessories.
However, much of this is still in early stages, so I wasn't able to see how it fares in a real-world scenario. Framework also promises that updates will be available via a web page (accessible through Google Chrome or Microsoft Edge), but the updater is still in development.
Early Verdict: Worth the $50 Deposit?
As of this writing, Framework plans to release the Wireless Touchpad Keyboard in Q1 2027 for $99.00, and you can preorder it now with a fully refundable $50 deposit.
Even at this early stage, the Framework Wireless Touchpad Keyboard shows plenty of potential. Its open-source firmware and swappable parts will appeal to tinkerers and the custom keyboard crowd, while its built-in touchpad and multi-device support make it a great fit for people like me who want a single universal keyboard for working and playing across many devices. A few rough edges remain, like the lack of touchpad sensitivity controls, but there's still time for Framework to smooth them out before launch, and I look forward to getting my hands on the finished product.
