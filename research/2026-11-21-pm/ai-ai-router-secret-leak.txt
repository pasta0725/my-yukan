Dragos says it sent about two million AI prompts seeded with canary secrets to widely used AI services between July and November, and found evidence that at least three providers had been breached. HD Moore, the firm’s chief research officer, published the results on 
19 November
, and Dragos says none of the three providers knew about their breach until it told them.
We seeded 2m canaries into popular AI services and tracked where they were stolen and abused. 
This identified:
– Three compromised providers

November 19, 2026
Anthony Ronning, the CTO of Maple AI, quoted Moore’s post on X and wrote that Zero Data Retention (ZDR, the setting that tells a provider not to store a prompt) is a lie, and that people should use inference built on trusted execution environments (TEEs, hardware-sealed areas of a server that its operator can’t look into). Maple sells that kind of service. Dragos’s report doesn’t mention TEEs.

November 20, 2026
What Dragos did
Dragos built a service that gives every prompt its own hostnames, URLs and credentials, then watches for anyone using them. Moore says the work began in July with 
Thinkst Canarytokens
. The AWS keys in the prompts are real keys tied to empty accounts with full logging, so any attempt to use one shows up. Dragos also traced about 2,000 routes through AI routers, which forward a request to one of several model hosts, by sending slightly broken requests and reading the nested error messages. Several hundred routes included a system it couldn’t identify, and a handful sent its test image to a company that wasn’t on the advertised path.
Three providers were breached
Dragos says attackers were stealing data from three hosting providers, and the earliest incident dates to mid-July. The largest involved a couple of hundred of its AWS keys, each sent in exactly one request, all inside a few short windows in late October. Every request Dragos sent during those windows was stolen, including some the provider rejected before a model ran. Dragos says that points to copying at the provider’s front door rather than deeper in the inference stack.
In the other two breaches, some of the stolen requests were marked ZDR. The attackers tested the bait keys, sometimes within hours, then used them to list S3 buckets, search Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, and try to switch on Bedrock models and add their own users. Dragos counts tens of thousands of AWS API calls. It says one attacker appears to have compromised two of the providers at once, and that on 1 November the attacker added ARTEX, an LLM-driven penetration-testing framework, which made only a handful of calls.
The first provider thought the activity was a misconfiguration of internal tooling, then a partner data problem, until the investigation showed an attacker had compromised its internal credentials tool. Dragos says that provider still hasn’t disclosed the incident publicly, and stolen data led to attempted logins as of late October. Dragos hasn’t named any of the three.
Dragos’s five months of canaries come down to four numbers.
Where the ZDR setting didn’t hold
Dragos says a privacy setting only binds the first company that receives the request. Sellers on an inference marketplace, where the lowest price wins the traffic, only had to turn on request logging to read customer prompts, according to Dragos. About a day and a half after two prompts went through one seller’s offer, someone on a home connection opened both secret URLs and started guessing usernames at the login page. A Mac on a home line in France typed root SSH passwords taken from two prompts by hand. Prompts for the same model sent through a different seller were never touched, so Dragos says the leak follows the seller, not the marketplace.
The clearest ZDR failure involved a Chinese AI lab that Dragos doesn’t name. Its systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half the prompts sent to its models between July and October, including about two-thirds of the requests marked no data collection or ZDR, usually two or three days later. US hosting providers that resell the lab’s models triggered the same scraper. Twenty-four days after Dragos sent a prompt to a Chinese provider, the secret link from it was posted in a Feishu chat and opened within two minutes from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. Dragos says whoever shared it was reading a stored copy of the full prompt.
Two large US providers, also unnamed, showed their own habits. One kept crawling a secret URL more than two months after a single call to its search endpoint, and another runs a scraper that comes back days after each request, whichever router carried it.
A privacy flag only binds the first hop, Dragos says.

NEAR AI runs its cloud inference in TEEs. In its 
9 November incident report
, the company says an attacker used a remote code execution flaw in SGLang, the open-source inference engine on its servers, to run code on one inference server and reach internal systems. It says the attacker could have seen requests processed on that server on 8 November, including confidential-mode requests, and took account details for about 100 users and data from 18 agents on a legacy deployment that wasn’t in its TEE. A TEE guards data from the host, not from bugs in what runs inside it.
What Maple says it does
Ronning is Maple’s co-founder and CTO. Maple’s 
proof page
 says prompts are encrypted on the user’s device and decrypted only inside an AWS Nitro Enclave running open-source code, and that the app checks a signed fingerprint of the enclave before it sends anything. It lists four things the proof rests on: Amazon’s Nitro hardware, the published code, Maple’s signing key and the model’s own enclave. The page also says a matching fingerprint shows Maple approved that software, not that it was built from the public source, and that the browser version of the check is served by Maple on each page load.
Maple publishes its client and server code, which fits the condition in Ronning’s post about providers whose code you can verify. NEAR AI publishes its stack too, and that didn’t stop the SGLang breach. Dragos doesn’t say whether it tested Maple.
What Dragos recommends
Dragos says none of this means stopping use of AI services, and that where prompts go is a decision the lowest price will otherwise make for you. Its five steps:
Host it yourself, if you can afford it.
 It’s the only option where nobody else reads your prompts. Dragos prices the hardware from US$6,950 for an NVIDIA DGX Spark to US$570,000 or more for an eight-GPU server.
Go directly to a first-tier provider.
 That gives one contract and one privacy policy, and it can still leak through data-sharing agreements or a breach.
Use routers with care.
 Pick the upstream providers yourself, check that privacy settings change where requests go, and stay away from reseller marketplaces.
Seed your own canaries.
 Put a fake credential in the context your agents already send and treat any use of it as an incident. A free Thinkst Canarytoken is enough to start.
Turn on secret filtering
 where a provider offers it. Dragos says it can’t hurt but isn’t a cure, because plenty of sensitive prompt data isn’t shaped like an API key.
Phala has said it will publish a full root cause analysis. Dragos says it’s working with about a dozen providers and expects the list to grow, and that stolen keys and secret URLs are still being accessed months after the first incidents.
Update, 20 November: Dragos responded to our questions after publication, with answers attributed to Moore and passed on by David Garcia. On the TEE question, Dragos said: “As long as clear-text prompts and responses are flowing through third-parties, I’m not sure how helpful TEEs will be. We need something much closer to E2E to the GPU, and even then, security hardware that other users can program is difficult (speculative attacks, uncleared VRAM, etc).” Dragos said it won’t name the three breached providers and hopes “they all notify the public directly.” It also said it won’t release the canary service, its code or its data, because the data contains raw secrets and provider names, and sharing the code would make the service less useful. It pointed to Phala’s public notice as the one it knew of from a provider that received its test requests.

Dragos researcher HD Moore hid unique, trackable secrets in about two million prompts sent to AI services between July and November 2026 and 
published what happened to them on 19 November
. The secrets were stolen in breaches at three AI providers, read by marketplace resellers who had switched on request logging, and fetched by providers from prompts marked Zero Data Retention. Dragos names none of the three.
In brief
None of the three breached providers knew about its breach until Dragos told it. One has since published a preliminary security notice.
In the largest breach, every request sent to one provider during several windows in late October was copied, including requests the provider rejected before a model ran.
Some stolen AWS keys were tested within hours, and the keys were used in tens of thousands of AWS API calls. On 1 November the attacker added 
ARTEX, the AI penetration testing tool named in the FBI advisory of 13 November
.
Sellers on an AI inference marketplace read customer prompts by listing models cheaply and turning on standard logging. No exploit was involved.
A Chinese AI lab fetched secret links from more than half of the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of those marked for no data collection.
What did the Dragos canary study test?
The study tested what happens to the contents of an AI prompt after the service has answered it, by planting secrets that raise an alarm when anyone uses them. Each prompt carried canary tokens: unique web links and DNS names, passwords, Git repository addresses, and AWS keys tied to empty accounts with full logging. Every token traced back to one prompt, one model, one route and one privacy setting, so a login attempt or a fetched link identified the request it came from.
Moore, who is chief research officer at Dragos and the creator of Metasploit, also built a way to trace the path a request takes. Of about 2,000 routes traced, several hundred passed through systems the researchers could not identify. Dragos has notified about a dozen providers and expects that number to grow.
A canary shows that a prompt leaked, without showing which company in the chain leaked it, a limit Dragos acknowledges. It describes the three breaches as activity indicating exposure and does not name the providers, so nobody outside Dragos and those companies can check the findings yet.
How many companies see an AI prompt?
A single prompt can pass through three or four companies before it reaches a model, and each of them can read the prompt and the answer in full. Many applications send requests to a router, a service that offers one API address and forwards each request to one of many models. A router can hand the request to a second router, which hands it to a hosting provider, which runs the model. Inference marketplaces are open routers where the seller with the lowest price gets the request.
A customer can mark an account, a key or a single request for no data collection, and Zero Data Retention (ZDR) is the strictest setting. Dragos found that the flag binds only the first company to receive the request, and that routers often forward flagged requests to providers that do not apply the same control.
Researchers at UC Santa Barbara used planted credentials against routers in April. Of the 
428 AI API routers they tested
, nine injected malicious code into responses and 17 used AWS credentials the researchers had planted.

What happened at the three breached AI providers?
Attackers inside three AI hosting providers copied customer requests, and none of the three knew until Dragos told them. The earliest breach began in mid-July. That provider first blamed a misconfiguration in its internal tooling, then a partner that processes data for it, and then found that its own internal credentials had been compromised. It had not disclosed the incident publicly by late October, and data stolen from it was still producing login attempts then.
The largest breach took a couple of hundred AWS keys, each of which Dragos had sent in a single request to a single provider during a few short windows in late October. Every request in those windows was stolen. Some had been rejected before any model ran, which tells Dragos the attacker was copying requests as they arrived. One adversary appears to have been inside two of the providers at once, working from a single rented server. Some of the stolen requests in the other two breaches were marked ZDR, which limits what a provider stores and offers no defence once an intruder is inside its systems.
A key planted on 21 October was first used the next day and was still in use three weeks later. With the keys, the attacker listed S3 storage buckets, searched AWS Secrets Manager and Parameter Store, tried to switch on hosted models in Amazon Bedrock, created Bedrock API keys and added new IAM users.
The tooling changed over time, from a script posing as Chrome on Windows to the AWS command line on Kali Linux. On 1 November the attacker added ARTEX. Here it made only a handful of calls, to confirm that keys the attacker already held still worked. Dragos says two of the user-agent strings ARTEX left in the AWS logs appear on no public indicator list and recommends adding them to detections.
How did marketplace resellers read customer prompts?
Resellers on an AI inference marketplace read customer prompts by listing popular models at the lowest price, switching on standard request logging and waiting for traffic. They exploited no vulnerability and bypassed no control. Dragos describes the sellers as individuals on home internet connections, and says the shadier marketplaces commonly bill the inference itself to stolen AWS credentials or consumer API keys.
About a day and a half after two prompts went through one seller, someone on home broadband opened both secret links in a desktop browser and began guessing usernames. Prompts sent to the same model through a different seller were never touched. In another case, a person in France using a Mac took passwords from two prompts and typed root SSH login attempts by hand, trying one password four times in two minutes. The same Mac later tested several AWS keys.

Did providers honour Zero Data Retention?
Some providers kept and used prompts that customers had marked Zero Data Retention or no data collection, according to Dragos. The clearest case is a Chinese AI lab. From July to October its systems fetched the secret links in more than half of the prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of the requests that carried a privacy flag. The scraper usually arrived two or three days after the request. US hosting providers that resell the lab’s models set off the same scraper, so prompts sent to a US company flowed back to the lab. A Git client on another cloud also tried to clone private repositories named only in prompts sent to this lab’s models.
Two large US providers also came back for links long after answering. One was still crawling a secret link more than two months after a single call to its search endpoint. The other’s scraper returned days after each request, whichever router carried it.
Twenty-four days after one request was sent, its secret link was posted in a chat on Feishu, the workplace messaging app, and opened within two minutes from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. The Dragos post describes that request in one place as going to a Chinese provider and in another as sent to a US-based provider with the ZDR flag set.
Which AI providers were breached?
Dragos has not named the three breached providers, the marketplace or the Chinese lab. The few companies named in its post appear in examples of how requests are routed, and none is described as breached. One of the three has published a preliminary security notice, Dragos says, without identifying it. Customers of AI hosting providers and routers therefore have no public way to tell whether their prompts were among those copied, beyond watching for a notice from their own supplier.
How else do AI prompts reach criminals?
Infostealer malware is a second route to the same data, and it needs no breach at the provider. A stealer copies the passwords and session cookies saved in the browsers on an infected computer, and whoever buys that 
stealer log
 can often sign in to the victim’s AI account and read its history. The 1 November 2026 snapshot of the Ransomnews infostealer dataset holds 4,008 OpenAI logins registered with work email addresses at 319 large organisations, 442 of them at 20 government bodies, according to the 
November report
. Ransomnews covered that route in 
its piece on shadow AI and stealer logs
.
Attackers also search for the keys themselves: Microsoft reported on 5 November that they were using AI agents to 
search public GitHub repositories for exposed secrets
, API keys for AI services among them.
What should organisations do?
Dragos advises shortening the chain between the user and the model and planting canaries in whatever AI agents can read. Its post also says: “None of this means that you should stop using AI services.” Its recommendations, in order, with two from Ransomnews at the end:
Host the model yourself if you can afford the hardware. Dragos calls this the only option where nobody else reads your prompts, and prices suitable machines from $6,950 for a desktop unit to $570,000 or more for an eight-GPU server.
Otherwise go directly to a first-tier provider, which leaves one contract and one privacy policy to review.
If you use a router, choose the upstream providers yourself, check that privacy settings change where requests go, and avoid reseller marketplaces.
Put canary credentials into the context your agents and pipelines send, and treat any use of one as an incident. AI agents will send any credential they can read, which is 
one of the ways agentic AI widens an attack
.
Turn on the secret-filtering features providers offer, knowing they miss sensitive data that is not shaped like an API key.
If you do host a model yourself, put it behind a login. A September count by Mysterium VPN found 
36,769 self-hosted AI endpoints open to the internet, 2% of which asked for one
.
Check whether employee logins for AI services are already in stealer logs with 
StealerCheck
, then reset those passwords and end the sessions.
Frequently asked questions
Which AI providers were breached?
Dragos has not named them. It says three AI hosting providers were compromised between July and November 2026, that none knew until it was told, and that one has published a preliminary security notice.
What is a canary token?
A canary token is a fake secret, such as a web link, password or cloud access key, that has no legitimate use and alerts its owner when anyone touches it. Dragos put a different one in each prompt so that any use pointed to the exact request that leaked.
What is Zero Data Retention?
Zero Data Retention, or ZDR, is a setting under which an AI provider agrees not to store a customer’s prompts and responses after answering. Dragos found it binds only the first company that receives a request, and other companies further along the route may not apply it.
Does Zero Data Retention protect prompts in a breach?
No. ZDR limits what a provider stores after answering. In the Dragos study, requests marked ZDR were among those stolen from two of the breached providers.
Is it safe to put passwords or API keys in AI prompts?
No. Some AWS keys stolen from the Dragos prompts were tested within hours, and one was still being used three weeks later. Dragos also warns that AI agents will include any credential they can read in the prompts they send.
What is an AI router?
An AI router is a service that gives developers one API address and forwards each request to one of many models, often hosted by other companies. Every company a request passes through can read the full prompt and the response.
Should companies stop using AI services?
Dragos says no. It recommends sending prompts to as few companies as possible, avoiding reseller marketplaces, and planting canary credentials so that a leak is noticed.

How can you protect your AI supply chain?
HD Moore, Chief Research Officer, will be regularly sharing work from the research team he leads at Dragos. Topics will include xOT, IT, LLMs, PQC, and more. Their findings will sharpen defenders’ understanding of exposures and vulnerabilities emerging across the threat landscape.
We sent nearly two million AI prompts containing unique, trackable secrets to AI providers, then watched where those secrets surfaced. What we found revealed security breaches, prompt theft, and widespread failures to protect customer data.
Over a five-month period from July through November 2026, we submitted approximately two million prompts containing unique URLs and canary token credentials to widely used AI services and monitored what happened after inference. Our investigation uncovered three significant findings:
Evidence of provider compromises.
 We observed activity indicating that data from at least three AI providers was exposed. Our seeded test prompts were used in attempts to access our controlled test systems and canary AWS accounts.
Unauthorized access to customer prompts by inference marketplace providers.
 Multiple resellers operating through an AI inference marketplace were able to view and retain customer prompts using standard logging capabilities, without exploiting a vulnerability or bypassing platform controls.
Failures in privacy and data-handling practices.
 We observed multiple cases in which providers appeared not to honor customer privacy settings or published policies. Examples included prompts submitted to U.S.-based providers being transmitted to Chinese AI organizations, unique URLs being accessed months after a single inference request, and one seeded link ultimately appearing in an internal company chat in China.
Following inference data
We wanted to understand what happens to inference data after it leaves the customer’s environment.
To answer that question, we created millions of unique canaries, including URLs and credentials that would never be used by legitimate systems. Any attempt to access those assets would indicate that someone, somewhere in the AI ecosystem had seen the original prompt.
The results exposed a fundamental disconnect between how AI services are marketed and how inference data is handled in practice.
Why does AI inference data matter?
Most folks picture AI inference as a question and a response. Text goes in, GPUs juggle some bits, and text comes back out. What complicates this is how the data is actually treated. 
AI providers commonly store prompts and responses for operational, training, safety, abuse-monitoring, or business purposes. Depending on the provider, that data may be retained for days, months, or indefinitely. It may also traverse multiple organizations, including model developers, cloud operators, inference platforms, and third-party resellers.
The prompts and replies – the inference data itself – are often more valuable than the GPU resources used to process them. They frequently contain confidential source code, internal communications, strategic plans, customer information, credentials, and other sensitive business data.
How do AI routers expose your prompts?
AI routers work by offering a single API endpoint that forwards your request to a pool of available models, sometimes at different hosting providers, with unified billing. Routers are great at cost reduction and providing flexibility without managing multiple accounts and credentials. The path to the inference server often passes through more than one router before reaching another provider, and from there the specific server hosting the target model. There is risk in this process – every hop can see the entire prompt and response.
Some routers are open marketplaces where the seller with the lowest price gets the request. It’s common for sellers to offer AI inference at deep discounts, and they can do this by pooling requests through unmetered consumer-plan subscriptions or by finding other low-cost paths to the specific model (startup credits, etc.). In the shadier marketplaces, it’s also common to see inference that is billed to stolen AWS credentials or consumer API keys. If a seller wants to siphon customer request data, they can list popular models at the lowest price, route the traffic, pay the difference, and then collect the data from the platform logs.
These providers offer privacy controls – customers can set privacy flags at the account level, for a specific API key, or in each request. The strictest setting is known as Zero Data Retention (ZDR), which specifies that the prompt and response are not stored. The routers should only forward these requests to providers that offer the same controls. The reality is quite different – providers often ignore these settings, either intentionally or due to bugs in their processing flow, and prompts not covered by ZDR are fair game for resale into a massive ecosystem of buyers and sellers.
Three compromised companies
The most serious finding was also the most direct. We found attackers actively stealing data from three different hosting providers. Although our prompts were bait, they were processed along with real customer requests, and those were almost certainly copied too. The earliest incident dates back to the middle of July, and none of the three providers knew about their breach until we notified them.
The largest breach involved a couple hundred of our AWS keys. Each key was sent in exactly one request to one provider, all within a few short windows in late October, and every request we sent during those windows was stolen. Some of the stolen keys came from requests that the provider rejected before a model ever ran – this tells us that the attacker was copying requests at the provider’s front door, and not somewhere deeper in the inference stack. In the other two breaches, some of the stolen requests were marked as ZDR. A privacy flag doesn’t help when the attacker is already inside.
Once the attacker had our credentials, they followed a predictable path. First, they tested each key to see if it was live, in some cases within a few hours of our request. Next, they went looking for data, listing S3 buckets and searching Secrets Manager and Parameter Store. Finally, they tried enabling hosted models in AWS Bedrock, creating Bedrock API keys, and adding their own IAM users to keep access. In total, our bait keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls.
Figure 1. One stolen AWS key. We seeded it on October 21, it was first used the next day, and it was still in use three weeks later. Names are pseudonyms and addresses are partly masked.
One adversary appears to have compromised two of these providers at the same time. Secrets that we sent to those two providers were used together, almost immediately, from a single rented server. The attacker’s tooling changed over the three weeks that we watched it – it started with a script pretending to be Chrome on Windows, moved to Boto3 running inside OrbStack, and later switched to the AWS CLI on Kali Linux.
On November 1, the same attacker added 
ARTEX
, an LLM-driven penetration testing framework. In our case, ARTEX did very little – it made a handful of calls to confirm that keys the attacker already held were still live. Two of the user agents it sent, 
artex-agent and artex-judge-r121, 
don’t appear in any public indicator list that we could find, and are worth adding to your detections.
Figure 2. Tools that were used against our planted secrets, including the AI pentest agent (first row) and the Kali environment (aws-cli row).
The good news for defenders is that these tools tend to announce themselves – both Kali and ARTEX showed up in the user agent that AWS records in CloudTrail. The bad news is that this self-identification was only present in a small fraction of traffic.
Marketplace resellers stole customer prompts
The second finding didn’t require an exploit at all. The marketplace sellers that we caught only had to list a model at the lowest price, enable request logging, and wait. What stood out is how personal it was – these were individuals, working from what looked like home internet connections and personal computers, trying to break into our canary servers one login at a time.
In one case, we sent two prompts through a single seller’s offer. About a day and a half later, someone on a home broadband connection opened both secret URLs in a desktop browser and started guessing usernames at the login page. Prompts for the same model that went through a different seller were never touched, which tells us that the leak follows the seller and not the marketplace as a whole.
In another case, an attacker working from a Mac on a home broadband line in France took the passwords from two of our prompts and tried to log in as root over SSH, typing each attempt by hand. The same Mac later tested several of our AWS keys in a single session.
Figure 3. One of the root SSH attempts. The password from our prompt was used four times in two minutes, and the route trace shows the two routers that the prompt passed through.
Most of those keys had been sent through the marketplace, but one had gone to a completely different router. It turned out that this router was quietly forwarding some of its traffic to the marketplace – another example of an undisclosed hop.
Privacy settings and retention policies were ignored
The third finding is different from the first two, as nobody used a credential. Instead, the secret URLs in our prompts were scraped, repeatedly, and the git repositories they referenced were targeted for cloning – all by companies that were never supposed to see the prompt in the first place, and often in direct conflict with the privacy settings on the request.
The clearest example involves a Chinese AI lab. This lab’s systems fetched the secret URLs from more than half of the prompts that we sent to its models between July and October, including roughly two-thirds of the requests marked as “no data collection” or Zero Data Retention. The scraper typically showed up two or three days after the request.
This wasn’t limited to requests sent to the lab directly. Several U.S. hosting providers resell this lab’s models, and prompts sent to those providers triggered the exact same scraper – the prompts were flowing back to the lab. A customer who picked a U.S. provider specifically to keep their data in the U.S. didn’t get what they paid for. Later on, a git client on a different cloud started trying to clone the private repositories referenced in our prompts, but only for prompts that used this lab’s models.
The large U.S. providers have their own issues. One continued to crawl a secret URL more than two months after we used it in a single inference call to their search endpoint. Another runs a scraper that comes back for our URLs days after each request, regardless of which router we used to reach it.
Figure 4. One day of incidents, grouped by source network. The same scraper networks show up against prompts that we sent through several different routers.
The strangest incident involved a company chat. Twenty-four days after we sent a prompt to one Chinese provider, the secret link from that prompt was posted into a Feishu chat channel (Feishu is known as Lark outside of China). Within two minutes, the link was opened from three desktops and three phones in Beijing and Shanghai. Our disclosure to this provider only included the first eight characters of that link, so whoever shared it was reading from a stored copy of the full prompt, more than three weeks after the request was sent to a U.S.-based provider with the ZDR flag.
Figure 5. The secret link shared in a chat and then opened in the mobile app. The git client is the earlier clone attempt.
Zero Data Retention is supposed to mean something. In practice, these prompts were retained, shared with other companies, and read by people.
How do canary tokens detect prompt leaks?
A canary token is a secret that only exists in one place – if anyone ever uses it, you know that place has been exposed. We started in July by seeding prompts with 
Thinkst Canarytokens
, and every request carried an AWS key, a DNS name, and a URL that had never been seen anywhere else. Tokens started firing within days, and the tokens from July are still firing today.
The proof-of-concept worked so well that it made sense to build a dedicated service just for seeding LLM prompts. This service generates a unique seed, and uses it to derive unique hostnames, URLs, and credentials for every prompt and then runs the services that those credentials point to – DNS, web, git, SSH, databases, mail, and a long tail of older protocols. The AWS keys are real keys tied to empty accounts with full logging. Every request that touches a token is tied back to the exact prompt, model, router, and privacy setting that carried it. Since each unique component can be tied back to its parent seed, we’re able to get precise timing and sequencing; we know what DNS server they use, how long it took to reply, and every piece of the inbound connections (TCP, TLS SNI, HTTP Host headers, URLs, credentials etc.). 
Figure 6. The console overview, showing incidents and seeded prompts per day, split by the router that handled the prompt.
Canaries tell us that a prompt leaked, but not where in the chain, since a router typically reports a single upstream provider, and that provider is often just another router. To figure out who was really handling our requests, we built something like traceroute for AI inference. The first part is simple – we include an image URL in the request that points back to our servers, and every system that fetches it gives away an IP address, an HTTP client, and a timestamp.
The second part takes advantage of how these services are stacked. We send small, slightly broken requests, and since each router tends to wrap the error from the hop behind it in its own format, a single response can contain three or four nested “envelopes.” Matching each layer against a signature database gives us the list of hops, the software at each one, the network they use, and often the company operating it.
So far, we have traced about two thousand routes. Several hundred of those included a system that we couldn’t identify, and a handful sent our image to a company that wasn’t on the advertised path at all. The traces also turned up middle layers that the route never mentions – one marketplace seller quietly adds about a thousand hidden tokens to every prompt, and another fetches images through a shared consumer subscription account.
Figure 7. An example of LLM route tracing, showing a path from SurplusIntelligence to Anthropic. The OpenRouter hop was discovered by tracing and was not disclosed in the marketplace
What we did about it
Every incident is stored with its evidence – the original prompt, the route it took, every connection that followed, and our notes. The first incident was mid-July; the provider first thought this was a misconfiguration of internal tooling, then a problem with partner data processing, until the investigation uncovered evidence that the attacker had compromised their internal credentials too. This provider has still not publicly disclosed the incident and the stolen data continued to lead to attempted logins as of late October.
For the second batch of intrusions in late October, we reached out as soon as we confirmed that the data provided evidence of third-party access, and one of these providers has already published a preliminary security notice. 
For the less severe issues like data sharing (but not credential abuse), we started notifying providers in early November. Each report included the list of stolen requests along with unique strings that they could search for in their own logs. We are now working with about a dozen providers and wouldn’t be surprised to see this expand.
Reactions have been mixed. The router that was forwarding traffic to the marketplace told us that they stopped, and several providers have responded and started their own investigations. On the other hand, stolen keys and secret URLs continue to be accessed, even months after the initial incident.
How can you protect your AI supply chain?
None of this means that you should stop using AI services. It does mean that where your prompts go is a decision, and if you don’t make it, the lowest price will make it for you. Keep in mind that a privacy flag only binds the first hop, and that AI agents will happily send whatever they can read, including credentials.
Host it yourself, if you can afford it.
 This is the only option where nobody else gets to read your prompts, but the hardware isn’t cheap. A Mac Studio with 512 GB of memory starts at 
$22,299
, NVIDIA’s DGX Spark now costs 
$6,950
, a DGX Station starts at about 
$105,000
, and an eight-GPU B300 server lists for 
$570,000
 or more. For most teams, this only makes sense for the most sensitive work.
Go directly to a first-tier provider.
 One company, one contract, and one privacy policy to read. This can still leak through data sharing agreements or a breach at the provider, but it is the smallest exposure you can buy.
Use routers with care.
 Pick the upstream providers yourself instead of accepting the default pool, and verify that your privacy settings actually change where your requests go. Stay away from reseller marketplaces – some of that capacity comes from stolen keys, and a few of these services only accept cryptocurrency for a reason.
Seed your own canaries.
 Add a fake credential to the context that your agents and pipelines already send and treat any use of it as an incident. A free Canarytoken from Thinkst is enough to get started today.
Enable secret filtering.
 Some providers offer settings that will automatically scrub known credential formats from prompt data. This can’t hurt, but it’s not a panacea – there is plenty of sensitive data in prompts that isn’t shaped like an API key.
