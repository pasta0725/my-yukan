The third largest US commercial bank joins JPMorgan and Bank of America in putting AI agent mishaps on the customer's tab

Citibank has rewritten its rules for the AI age, and the short version is this: if your AI assistant messes up your bank account, that is on you.
The third largest commercial bank in the United States changed its terms of service this week. Customers who hand their banking access to third-party AI tools now carry the risk if something goes wrong, 
even if they get hacked
.
That puts Citi in step with 
JPMorgan
 and 
Bank of America
, the two largest banks in the country, which rolled out similar policies last month.
What Citi actually changed
The update came as a new section in Citi’s online user agreement. It deals specifically with third-party AI agents.
The core instruction is simple: customers should not share their login credentials. The more consequential part is what happens if they do.
Under the revised terms, any access or transactions carried out by an AI agent will be treated as authorized by the customer. The customer is held accountable for resulting problems, with only limited legal exceptions.
Advertisement
AI agents have become capable enough that this question was going to come up eventually. These tools range from models built by frontier AI labs to apps designed purely for personal finance.
Given a customer’s bank login, they can review spending patterns and weigh investment choices. They can also offer advice and, increasingly, act on it by moving money and paying bills.

The big banks move as a pack
Citi is not freelancing here. Between mid-October and late October 2026, several of the largest US banks spelled out where liability sits when AI tools touch customer accounts.
JPMorgan Chase revised its Digital Services Agreement in late October 2026. The new language bars third parties, AI agents included, from accessing customer login information.
There is a notable carve-out, though. JPMorgan still allows read-only access through regulated open-banking platforms.
Bank of America took a similar line in late October 2026. It told customers that using AI tools with their credentials means they take responsibility for whatever happens in the account.
Wells Fargo
 followed with an updated Online Access Agreement, effective November 24, 2026. Its language classifies transactions initiated by AI agents as authorized by the customer.
Add Citi, and four of the country’s biggest banks are now restricting AI agent access to customer accounts. Executives at these institutions have also stressed the risk of unauthorized transactions by third-party agents and the importance of keeping a human in the loop.
Why the liability shift matters
The phrase “considered authorized by the customer” carries a lot of weight. In banking, whether a transaction counts as authorized often decides who absorbs the loss when something goes sideways.
By defining agent activity as customer-authorized up front, banks are drawing a bright line before disputes ever arise. If an AI tool makes a bad payment, or gets compromised and drains an account, the bank’s terms already say who owns that outcome.
The pattern across the banks also hints at a preferred path forward. JPMorgan’s exception for read-only access through regulated open-banking platforms suggests the industry is not banning outside tools outright. It is steering them toward channels the banks can see and control.
Read-only access lets a tool analyze your finances without ever holding the keys to move money. Credential sharing gives the tool the same power you have, and that is precisely what the banks are trying to discourage.

Citibank has updated its user agreement to make customers responsible for activity by third-party AI agents they authorize to access their accounts, including activity by hackers if their credentials are exposed.
The third-largest commercial bank in the United States changed its terms this week. AI agents can be given access to bank accounts and other financial portals to analyze spending and investments, offer advice, and handle transactions or bill payments. Banks say they will not be responsible for reversing intentional or unintentional transactions made by automated assistants.

As third-party AI tools become more widely available, we want customers to understand the risks associated with sharing banking credentials or authorizing those tools to access their accounts,” a Citi spokesperson told TechCrunch. “We recently updated our Online User Agreement to provide greater clarity about those risks and to reinforce that customers who choose to grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activities conducted through those agents.
 - Citi spokesperson
Citi’s updated terms also say customers are responsible if an AI tool leaks their credentials or is hacked, including for anything hackers do with their accounts. The bank says customers can use AI agents it provides without additional liability.
JPMorgan and Bank of America, the country’s two largest banks, announced similar policies last month. PNC, the eighth-largest bank, changed its terms on Nov. 18. Citizens also emailed customers this week warning that they are responsible for anything their AI assistant does, according to an email seen by TechCrunch.
For AI companies, personal finance offers a way to add services for customers and earn fees. But sharing access to bank and brokerage accounts raises trust concerns. Anthropic’s personal finance tool, launched earlier this month, is described as “read-only” and “can’t move, manage, or invest your money.”
Banks also see AI companies’ efforts to enter personal finance as a competitive threat. Markets briefly reacted to the prospect that AI tools could help consumers avoid fees or low-interest deposit accounts that bolster bank margins. If AI agents become a major way for people to make financial decisions, banks will fight to control that channel.
The dispute reflects a broader tension: AI companies say their agents will change how people interact with service providers, while those providers push back. Similar disputes have emerged in car-hailing and food service, and their outcomes will help determine which AI investments pay off.

===== https://startupfortune.com/citi-pnc-and-citizens-tell-customers-they-own-any-loss-an-ai-agent-causes/
(status 200)
If you hand your banking login to an AI assistant and it gets tricked or breached, your bank's terms now say the loss is yours. You'll want to read the agreement before you connect anything.
Citibank updated its Online User Agreement this week to say customers who give third-party AI agents access to their accounts are responsible for what those agents do, according to TechCrunch. PNC changed its terms on Wednesday, November 18, and Citizens Bank emailed customers the same warning this week. JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America had already moved in October.
Citi's own explanation, quoted by TechCrunch, is that it wanted to "provide greater clarity about those risks and to reinforce that customers who choose to grant account access to third-party AI agents are responsible for activities conducted through those agents." That's the polite version. The sharper detail is that Citi's terms also put the loss on you if an AI tool leaks your credentials or is hacked, and a criminal then uses them.
The answer to "are banks liable if my AI agent gets hacked" depends on one word. Under Regulation E, which implements the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, a consumer's liability for an unauthorized transfer is capped, and the cap is low if you report quickly. Banks that define everything an agent does as authorized by you take those transactions out of that category before a dispute starts. Wells Fargo's Online Access Agreement, effective November 24, says every transaction by a customer's delegates, AI agents included, counts as authorized, "including those you did not want or intend." JPMorgan's October terms likewise put the consequences of giving an agent your credentials on the customer.
No court or regulator appears to have tested this reading, and no US agency has publicly weighed in, so whether those clauses survive a real dispute over a drained account is open. Frankly, a bank deciding that a stolen password is a customer instruction is a big claim to make in a contract.
JPMorgan tests AI agents that trade currency with no human in the loop
JPMorgan and Hyundai Card ran a simulated test in which Hyundai's agent asked for euro top-ups and JPMorgan's Kinexys unit executed them within preset limits. A multi-agent follow-up is planned for the first half of 2027, with no commercial launch date. -

JPMorgan's October move was more blunt. CNBC reported on October 13 that Chase is blocking outside agents such as Muse and Instinct from reaching accounts directly. Bank of America, for its part, asked customers on October 27 not to share their banking password with an AI assistant, TechCrunch reported at the time.
What an agent can actually get wrong
The risk isn't hypothetical hand-wringing. A browser agent that logs in for you can read a malicious web page, and a prompt hidden in that page can tell it to send money somewhere else. A shopping agent can buy the wrong thing. A finance assistant that stores your username and password becomes a single place where all of it can leak. The bank can't tell from the login whether you or your software is on the other end, and the new terms settle that doubt in the bank's favor.
Anthropic's recently launched personal finance tool takes the opposite route, according to TechCrunch. It's marketed as read-only, with no ability to "move, manage, or invest your money." That's the design the banks seem to prefer.
The squeeze on agent-payment startups
If you're building an agent that pays bills or moves money, this is a distribution problem as much as a legal one. Your product depends on a bank letting your users' credentials through, and now the bank's contract tells those users that you are the risk they own. Few customers will accept that for a convenience feature. Startups that can offer scoped, revocable access through an open-banking API, with no passwords handed over, are in a stronger position than ones that log in as the user.
For the bigger banks, the incentive is plain. They collect nothing extra for the risk, and the agents' makers carry no obligation to them. Passing the loss to the account holder costs them a paragraph of legal text.
Wells Fargo's new terms take effect on November 24, which is the next date to watch, along with whether any regulator weighs in on how Regulation E treats an agent's actions. Until then, the practical rule from these banks is the same: if you let an agent in, you're signing for everything it does.
