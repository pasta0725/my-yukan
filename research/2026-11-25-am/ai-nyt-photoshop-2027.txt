The Times bars Photoshop 2027 on assignments because hidden AI features can tag photos as AI-generated.
C2PA provenance metadata is machine-readable and invisible yet can flag generative-AI involvement that photographers never see.
The directive's deeper meaning is editorial because a photo's provenance record now counts alongside its visible edits.
The Verge reported on November 24, 2026
 that 
The New York Times
 had directed freelance photographers not to use Adobe Photoshop 2027, release 28.0.0 or subsequent updates, on Times assignments because its AI features may not be apparent to users and can produce metadata identifying photographs as AI-generated.
The email from Times photography leaders Meaghan Looram and Josh Haner says the features use AI “in ways that may not be apparent to users.” Its requirement applies to all contributing photographers working on Times assignments; it does not extend to every Photoshop version or to other editing software. The purpose is to keep those tags from appearing inadvertently in Times photographs. Neither 
Adobe
 nor the Times immediately responded to a request for more information.
The report recounts the Times’ previous statement that it does not retouch or digitally manipulate photographs, while allowing minimal color adjustments to reproduce an image accurately, and describes AI metadata as a blunt account of post-production that risks readers’ trust in a newsroom where editing is kept to a minimum.
What Photoshop 28.0 Adds
Adobe’s November 2026 release notes
 identify the desktop update as version 28.0 and show AI spread across guided editing, whole-image transformation and photographic cleanup. The AI Assisted Editor is intended for quick adjustments, with an option to switch to Pro Editor for precise controls and layered editing. An AI Assistant in beta gives step-by-step guidance for editing tasks.
Prompt to edit accepts natural-language instructions to transform an entire image and generate preview variations, while Generative restyle uses a slider to control the intensity of an adjustment. Firefly Upscaler 2 is designed to preserve faces, textures and fine details when images are enlarged for large-format printing or high-resolution delivery.
Several additions can alter the scene itself. Generative Recompose adapts images to new canvas sizes and aspect ratios by repositioning subjects and adapting backgrounds. Relight can change lighting or introduce new sources after the shot, and the upgraded Remove tool detects and removes related shadows, reflections, ripples and other environmental traces. Which specific Photoshop feature prompted the Times’ directive remains unidentified.
How C2PA Provenance Metadata Works
C2PA metadata is machine-readable provenance information, not a visible watermark, and does not change the appearance of content. 
Adobe’s transparency documentation
 describes a gradual rollout throughout August 2026 across Creative Cloud, Document Cloud, Firefly and CX Enterprise applications. Supported workflows automatically attach metadata to qualifying content created or edited with generative AI, with no additional action required.
The metadata may identify the AI system and version, the model, whether the content was generated or edited with generative AI, creation or modification timestamps, and a unique identifier for each use. It is designed to remain associated with content through compatible Adobe and third-party workflows. Adobe does not control how external services interpret, display or use the information after content leaves its applications, so downstream presentation is separate from the provenance record itself.
That mechanism makes the Times’ restriction about more than visibly manipulated photographs. A photographer may regard an adjustment as limited while a compatible platform reads a record of generative-AI involvement. The directive sets a clear software boundary for Times assignments, but its broader significance is editorial: the assessment of a photograph includes both the visible adjustment and the provenance description delivered with the file.

Common questions
The New York Times has told its contributing photographers to stop using Adobe Photoshop 2027 on Times assignments, and the trigger is Photoshop AI metadata. 
According to The Verge
, the instruction covers release 28.0.0 and every update after it. The paper’s concern is that some Photoshop functions use generative AI “in ways that may not be apparent to users,” which can leave a photograph tagged as AI-generated.
Meaghan Looram and Josh Haner, two photography leaders at the Times, signed the email. Their wording is blunt: “At this time, we are requiring that all contributing photographers avoid using Adobe Photoshop 2027 (release 28.0.0 and subsequent updates) when working on assignment for The Times.” The report does not say which features caused the problem, and it gives no effective date.
The release in question is the same one Adobe showed off in November, and we covered its lighting tools in our 
Adobe MAX 2026 Photoshop and Lightroom roundup
. This piece sorts what is confirmed from what is guesswork, then looks at what the decision means for editors, freelancers and anyone who builds thumbnails, 
title cards
 and stills for video. Facts below were checked against The Verge’s report, Adobe’s help and release pages, and C2PA’s own announcements as of November 24, 2026.
What the Times told its photographers
The directive is narrow in scope and firm in tone. It applies to contributing photographers, the freelancers and outside shooters who deliver images under a Times assignment. It names one product, Photoshop 2027, and one version range, release 28.0.0 and anything newer. It does not ban Adobe, 
Creative Cloud
 or earlier Photoshop versions by name.
The Verge says it saw the email. The Times has not, in the sources reviewed here, published a separate public statement, and Adobe has not been quoted responding. That leaves the email itself as the primary document, relayed through one outlet.
The stated reason is about metadata, not image quality. Per the email as reported, using the affected features can result in metadata that identifies photographs as AI-generated. A newsroom that publishes documentary photography cannot afford a file that carries that label, even when the photographer only meant to clean up a stray blemish or adjust a highlight.
One more detail matters for planning. The word “avoid” is broad, yet the email is about assignment work. Nothing in the report suggests the Times is telling photographers what to run on personal projects or for other clients.
Which Photoshop release is affected
Adobe released Photoshop 2027 as version 28.0.0 on November 10, 2026, during Adobe MAX. It replaces the Photoshop 2026 line, which ran as version 27.x. Coverage of the launch from LooksLikeFilm and PiXimperfect lists the headline additions: Relight, Generative Restyle, an updated Remove Tool and Quick Compare. Adobe’s own announcement also mentions an AI Assisted Editor and Markup for guiding AI image generation and edits.
Photo: Luca Sammarco / Pexels
Adobe’s help pages describe image generation in this release using Adobe and partner models, including Firefly Image 5, Flux.2 Pro and Gemini. A beta build, version 28.1, adds an AI Assistant for natural-language edits. Under a literal reading of “subsequent updates,” that beta falls inside the Times restriction too, though the email as reported does not mention beta builds.
Adobe lists Photoshop at $22.99 a month on an annual plan billed monthly, according to the reported plan pages. Photoshop is also bundled into Creative Cloud plans that include it. No price change tied to this story has been reported.
A naming wrinkle is worth flagging. Adobe’s release notes label the November build version 28.0, while the Times email and launch coverage say Photoshop 2027. Both labels point at the same software.
Why Photoshop AI metadata is the sticking point
Content Credentials, built on the C2PA standard, are signed metadata that record what a tool or service says happened to a file. The record can include the application, the AI model, whether content was generated or modified, and the date. Adobe began rolling out C2PA metadata support across its applications in August 2026, so Photoshop AI metadata is a fresh feature of the editing stack, not a long-standing one.
The key point is what the label does and does not mean. Content Credentials do not prove an entire photograph was generated by AI. The same metadata can flag AI-assisted modification, such as a small generative fix on one corner of a frame. For a news desk with a no-manipulation policy, that distinction offers little comfort, because a reader or a verification tool sees a flag either way.
That is the Times’ practical problem. A photographer may not know that a given tool wrote a credential, and the Times says some functions use generative AI in ways that may not be apparent. A ban on the whole release is a blunt but easy-to-audit rule. A feature-by-feature rule would need the Times to know exactly which tools write metadata, and the paper has not said it does.

The directive and the reason
Looram and Haner wrote that using the features in question “can result in metadata identifying photographs as AI-generated.” That sentence is the whole logic of the rule. It does not accuse Adobe of hiding anything. It says the Times cannot control the label once a photographer opens the app.
The standing policy behind it
The Verge’s report also repeats the paper’s long-running photo rule. The Times says: “We do not retouch or digitally manipulate photos, but may make minimal adjustments to color to ensure we are accurately reproducing the image.” That policy predates this Photoshop restriction, and it should not be read as the start date of the ban. It does explain why the Times treats an AI flag as a policy breach and not a cosmetic annoyance.
How Adobe’s Content Credentials work in 2026
Adobe says qualifying generative-AI content created or modified in its apps automatically carries durable, machine-readable C2PA metadata. Its 
generative AI content labeling FAQ
 lays out the rule and, importantly, ties the automatic label to the generative tools. Tools outside that category fall outside the automatic label.
Photoshop’s documentation still tags its Content Credentials panel as beta, in an Adobe page last updated October 27, 2026. The Times email does not say which Photoshop 2027 tools worry the paper, so a reader cannot tell whether the concern is the automatic label on generative tools or something else in the app.
One more rule shapes the practical options. Adobe’s generative-AI licensing terms, as documented in its November release notes, prohibit customers from intentionally removing, altering or disabling provenance or metadata tags, including C2PA metadata, on content made with Adobe’s generative features. Stripping a credential to dodge a newsroom rule would break those terms, and it would also defeat the reason the Times asked photographers to steer clear.

Market impact: freelancers, newsrooms and Adobe
Freelancers feel this first. A contributor who pays for Photoshop and updates on release day now has a version they cannot use for one client. The practical fix, staying on the 2026 line or using another tool for Times work, costs time and may cost a second subscription. The sources do not say whether the 2026 line stays easy to install for new downloads, so plan around that gap.

Newsrooms feel it as a verification question. The AI flag does not tell an editor how big the change was, only that a generative path was used. That pushes desks toward either blanket rules like this one or toward asking photographers for raw files and edit logs. The Times chose the blanket rule.
Adobe faces a trust problem on top of a product push. In the same month it shipped Photoshop 2027, Adobe announced a collaboration with Google DeepMind to explore more AI image-editing capabilities, beginning with Photoshop. That announcement does not confirm a specific Google model is already inside Photoshop 2027. It does show the Photoshop AI toolset is growing, which makes per-feature clarity more valuable for press clients.

Historical context: no-retouching rules meet provenance tags
News photography has policed manipulation for decades, and the Times’ wording on retouching is among the plainest. What changed in 2026 is that the policing can now happen inside a file. Our 
editing software archive

C2PA also tracks adoption. According to its deployment guidance, reportedly published in May 2026, 54 products from 31 companies had passed conformance, with more than 120 additional companies expressing interest. What this means for video creators
The Times email concerns still photographs, but video makers share the pipeline. Thumbnails, title-card backgrounds, lower-third portraits and stills for documentary edits often pass through Photoshop. If a client or a platform later reads provenance data, a generative cleanup on a single still can follow the file into the timeline.
Four habits cost little and help. Keep an original copy of every still before any editing. Note which tool touched each asset. Ask a client up front whether it has an AI policy, and which tool versions it allows. Check whether your export keeps or drops metadata, since credentials travel with the file only when the format and workflow preserve them.
