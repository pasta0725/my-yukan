The BCI Pioneers Coalition has published “
OUR BCI: Recommendations for Ethical Implanted BCI Research
,” developed with academic researchers from discussions involving six implanted BCI research participants.  The document addresses 13 areas, including consent, compensation, neural-data privacy and care after a trial.  It calls for agreements that clarify control and ownership of implanted components, external hardware, software and participant-specific decoders before implantation.
For device developers, the recommendations extend to maintaining useful function when trials end or commercial support disappears.  They propose funding arrangements for maintenance and explantation, plans for transferring responsibilities when investigators move, and designs that reduce vulnerability to obsolete hardware or abandoned software.

On November 10, Ian Burkhart, founder of the BCI Pioneers Coalition, a group of people who have lived with implanted brain-computer interfaces, released 
“OUR BCI: Recommendations for Ethical Implanted BCI Research,”
 a 13-page document for researchers, sponsors, ethics boards and companies. It calls for consent to be treated as an ongoing conversation, limits on confidentiality agreements that could stop participants from reporting safety concerns, fair pay and credit for participants’ contributions, treatment of neural data as participants’ personal information, clear agreements on who owns hardware and software, and support after a trial ends. It took about a year to develop, with researchers from York University and the University of Ottawa, and was funded by a Canadian SSHRC grant.
The coalition plans to review the recommendations every 24 months. We could not find the document itself online; details come from The Debrief’s report.

People Living With Brain Implants Publish Foundational Recommendations for Ethical BCI Research
People Living With Brain Implants Publish Foundational Recommendations for Ethical BCI Research
View press release (PDF)
FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE
BCI Pioneers Coalition’s “OUR BCI” puts participant autonomy at the center of brain-computer interface trials, giving researchers and industry a foundation for ethical research as the field moves to formalize its standards
Columbus, Ohio, November 10, 2026 — 
The BCI Pioneers Coalition, a community led by people who have taken part in implanted brain-computer interface (iBCI) trials, today released 
OUR BCI: Recommendations for Ethical Implanted BCI Research
. A living document, written from the perspective of the people who volunteer for iBCI research, the recommendations set out a foundation that researchers, sponsors and companies can use to run trials ethically, built on participant autonomy.
Developed through facilitated discussions held between October 2025 and October 2026, with researchers at York University and the University of Ottawa, the recommendations cover 13 areas across the life of a trial: from how people first learn a trial exists, through recruitment, consent and the research itself, to post-trial care. The work was funded by a Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council of Canada (SSHRC) Partnership Engage Grant.
The recommendations arrive as neuroethics research increasingly treats participant autonomy as central to ethical neural-device research: recognizing participants as key contributors of knowledge whose insights should shape protocols and device design, treating consent as an ongoing process for long-term implants, and holding that obligations to participants continue after a trial ends. New ethics guidance for investigational implantable neural device studies likewise stresses respect for participants’ autonomy. Participants are also beginning to shape that research directly: Coalition founder Ian Burkhart co-authored a 2026 study in the journal Neuroethics on the psychological experience of living with a BCI. (See Research background, below.)
Recent events underline the need to formalize how the field works. The World Economic Forum’s Global Future Council on Neurotechnology recently published Industry Best-Practice Commitments and a proposed NeuroTrust Index for neurotechnology companies, a sign that expectations for neurotechnology are being written down. OUR BCI launches a living set of expectations for research in the field: the autonomy and recognition of the people who take part in research. 
“Participant autonomy is a mandatory prerequisite of ethical research. These recommendations are the foundation we are offering every research team and every company running a BCI trial: start with the people who live with the device, and build from there,” said 
Ian Burkhart
, founder of the BCI Pioneers Coalition. 
WHAT PARTICIPANTS ARE ASKING FOR
Consent as a continuing conversation.
 Plain-language materials, time for questions, a plan to reconfirm consent when a study changes, and regular independent check-ins with someone outside the research team.
NDAs with limits.
 No confidentiality agreement should ever stop a participant from raising safety, rights, or misconduct concerns with doctors, lawyers, regulators, or ethics boards, and any restrictions should be precise and time-limited.
Fair recognition.
 No out-of-pocket costs, fair compensation for participants’ time, and credit for intellectual contributions, including the chance to help draft and review publications.
Brain data that belongs to the person.
 Neural data should be treated as the participant’s personal information, with researchers as stewards, a right of access, and a say in how it is shared.
Clarity on who owns the device.
 Ownership and control of implanted hardware, software, and decoders should be settled before surgery, and a company’s property interest alone should not decide whether a device is removed or switched off.
A plan for the end of the trial.
 Device support for a reasonable period after a trial, escrow funds for explantation, and a plan if investigators move or a study ends early.
“We are not just only a participant, but we’re also making the science actually happen,” one Pioneer contributor says in the recommendations, which include first-person accounts from participants alongside the guidance.
“Consent is not a signature you collect once. In this new era of participant autonomy, it is a conversation that continues for as long as the device is part of someone’s life,” said 
Galen Buckwalter
, a BCI Pioneers lead and trial participant. 
“These foundational recommendations crucially come from people who have lived experience in implanted BCI research, and other research participants are invited to contribute to this living document to build an ethical future for BCI research. The next step is a binding code of practice, informed by this lived experience,  that ethics boards, funders and regulators can enforce,” said 
Jennifer Chandler
, Canada Research Chair in the Law and Ethics of Brain Technologies at the University of Ottawa. 
A CALL TO THE FIELD
The Coalition calls on investigators, sponsors, manufacturers, funders, research ethics boards and regulators to adopt the recommendations as a foundation for their trials: documented plans, budgets and participant-facing procedures that put participant autonomy first. It asks everyone now writing standards for neurotechnology to build on them, and offers Pioneers as advisers.
OUR BCI is a living document. It reflects the deliberation of six Coalition members and will be reviewed every 24 months as more participants contribute. The Coalition invites people with lived experience of iBCI trials to take part at 
info@bcipioneers.org
.
The full recommendations and an infographic of the 13 categories are available at 
https://bcipioneers.org/our-bci-recommendations
. 
ABOUT THE BCI PIONEERS COALITION
The BCI Pioneers Coalition is a digital forum founded and led by people with lived experience participating in implanted brain-computer interface trials. It provides peer support to current and prospective participants and brings the participant perspective to the scientists, companies, and policymakers shaping the field.
ABOUT THE OUR BCI PROJECT
OUR BCI: Outlining user-defined goals for responsible innovation in implanted brain-computer interface technologies
 is a partnership between the BCI Pioneers Coalition and researchers Amanda van Beinum (York University) and Jennifer Chandler (University of Ottawa), funded by SSHRC Partnership Engage Grant #892-2023-1099. Pioneer leads: Ian Burkhart, Galen Buckwalter, Scott Imbrie and Buz Chmielewski.

CONTRIBUTOR BIOS
RESEARCH FACILITATORS
Jennifer A. Chandler 
is a Professor of Law and Canada Research Chair (Tier 1) in the Law and Ethics of Brain Technologies at the University of Ottawa, Canada. Her research explores the legal, ethical and human rights implications of advances in neuroscience and neurotechnology, including brain-computer interfaces, neuromodulation therapies, and the use of brain data to infer mental states. She leads an international, multidisciplinary project examining freedom of thought and expression, mental privacy, and equality in relation to communication neuroprostheses. A Fellow of the Canadian Academy of Health Sciences, she received the International Neuroethics Society’s Steven E. Hyman Award for Distinguished Service to Neuroethics in 2024.
Amanda van Beinum
 is an Assistant Professor of Sociology at York University. Her research examines the social, ethical, and political dimensions of medical innovation, with a particular focus on emerging neurotechnologies and new approaches to mental healthcare. Drawing on medical sociology and science and technology studies, she investigates how medical technologies shape individual experiences, transform healthcare systems, and influence ideas about health, disability, autonomy, and what it means to be human. Her work uses critical qualitative and community-engaged approaches to examine questions of equity, power, and access in the development and implementation of new medical technologies.
BCI PIONEER LEADS
Ian Burkhart
 is a neurotechnology advocate, researcher, and founder and president of the BCI Pioneers Coalition. After a 2010 diving accident caused a C5 spinal cord injury, he became the first person to restore movement to a paralyzed limb using an implanted brain-computer interface linked to functional electrical stimulation. His experience as a long-term BCI trial participant informs his work advancing participant-centered research, ethics, access, and policy. Burkhart has co-authored research on implantable BCI clinical trials, serves on the FDA Patient Engagement Advisory Committee, is past president of NASCIC, and was named a 2025 MIT Technology Review Innovator Under 35.
Scott Imbrie
 is a BCI Pioneer, research participant, and advocate for people living with spinal cord injury. After a 1985 car accident caused a cervical spinal cord injury, he gradually regained some movement but retained significant fine-motor limitations. In 2020, Imbrie became the first participant in Chicago—and the fourth in a multisite study—to receive implanted electrodes in motor and somatosensory cortex. At the University of Chicago, he uses neural signals to control virtual and robotic limbs while receiving artificial tactile feedback. Through BCI Pioneers and extensive public speaking, Imbrie brings lived experience directly into neurotechnology research, ethics, and development.
Galen Buckwalter 
is a research psychologist, entrepreneur, musician, and BCI Pioneer whose career spans neuropsychology, psychometrics, virtual reality, and applied behavioral science. He developed eHarmony’s assessment and matching system and later founded psyML. After a spinal cord injury in adolescence, Buckwalter joined a Caltech–USC implanted brain-computer interface study in 2024, receiving six Utah arrays across motor, sensory, and frontal cortices. His work now sits at the intersection of neuroscience, AI, creativity, and neural autonomy. Through BCI Pioneers, he advocates for participant rights, neural-data governance, long-term device support, and participant-centered neurotechnology research and policy development.
Robert “Buz” Chmielewski
 is a BCI Pioneer, research participant, educator, and spinal cord injury advocate. After a C6 incomplete spinal cord injury at age 17, he became a long-term peer mentor and patient-educator in rehabilitation and health-professions training. In 2019, Johns Hopkins implanted six microelectrode arrays bilaterally in his motor and sensory cortices. Chmielewski subsequently demonstrated simultaneous brain control of two advanced prosthetic arms, including feeding himself, while receiving artificial sensory feedback and later experiencing virtual touch in mixed reality. Through BCI Pioneers, he brings decades of lived experience to neurotechnology research, participant advocacy, accessibility, ethical development, policy, and reform.
