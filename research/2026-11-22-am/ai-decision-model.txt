The rise of the AI decision model
 — 
PublicDomainPictures / pixabay
Event log
TypeSafe's Jev costs about 4 cents per million input tokens with no output-token charge
Jev launched September 2026; Google's Gemini Decisions API followed in November
Decision models respond in under a second and return probabilities for every allowed option
At 1 million decisions per day, a 1% error rate means 10,000 mistakes
The EU AI Act requires human oversight of certain high-risk AI decisions
A judgment call that used to cost a large language model several cents now costs 0.000004 dollars. TypeSafe's Jev, a hosted decision model priced at about 4 cents per million input tokens with free output tokens, is pulling an entire category into the enterprise AI stack — and by November 2026, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, Databricks, Snowflake, Cloudflare and AWS had all shipped rivals.
Start with the scene where these models actually earn their keep: a supplier email about a delayed shipment lands in a procurement inbox. A decision model reads it and answers three narrow questions — does it specify a firm new delivery date, does the delay threaten a customer commitment, which escalation category applies. Conventional software then compares dates and stock levels, and procurement rules decide whether to expedite or find another supplier. Everything outside the judgment stays in code that teams can read, test and modify.
What exactly is a decision model?
It's a smart if-statement. You feed it input data — an email, customer record, document, sometimes an image or call recording — plus questions with predefined answer options. It returns an answer and a probability for every allowed option, in under a second. It generates no text.
That matters because most AI in business processes is small judgment, not content generation: is this ticket about billing or a defect, does this invoice match the purchase order, should the agent send that email, which model should handle this prompt. Until now the default was querying an LLM and parsing a one-word answer — slow, costly at scale, and silent on confidence.
Terminology varies. TypeSafe calls Jev a "system one model," after economist Daniel Kahneman's term for fast, intuitive thinking. Others say decision model or small judgment model (SJM).
Isn't this just an ML classifier?
In one sense, yes — spam filters and fraud models have classified inputs for decades. The difference is cost and ease. Traditional classifiers demand thousands of labeled examples, a data science team, a separate model per question and ongoing maintenance. Decision models take plain-language questions, accept documents in various formats, and one model serves scenarios from ticket routing to contract review. Jev's request format has become a de facto standard: users can often switch providers by changing an endpoint, not rewriting an application.
A trained classifier still wins when a question is stable and labeled data is plentiful. Decision models shine when questions change frequently or no labeled data exists.
Who's selling what?
The landscape splits along two axes.
By capability:
General-purpose text and records:
 TypeSafe Jev, OpenAI Decisions API, Microsoft-Decision-1
Multimodal (images, audio, video):
 Google Gemini Decisions API, Cloudflare Clef-omni, Liquid AI d1
Longer reasoning:
 Fastino GLiDE, Respan span
Source-consulting with citations:
 Parallel Decisions API, Perplexity Decider
Industry-specific:
 Superagent Security-One, Nace AI Drex Max Finance
By delivery: major AI platforms (OpenAI, Google, Microsoft); data platforms reachable through SQL, such as Databricks ai_decide and Snowflake Decision; open-weight self-hosted models like Cloudflare Clef and AWS Strands Decider running via Ollama, vLLM or Hugging Face; and specialist APIs, some still on early-access terms with uneven enterprise support.
The timeline compressed fast: TypeSafe launched Jev in September 2026, OpenAI, Databricks, Cloudflare and AWS followed quickly, Microsoft and Snowflake announced in October, and Google shipped the Gemini Decisions API in November.
Where do they pay off?
Use cases span nearly every function — routing tickets and flagging at-risk customers, matching invoices to purchase orders, holding payments when a customer appears coached by a scammer, fast-tracking straightforward insurance claims, scoring security alerts, routing prompts to the cheapest capable model, checking agent actions against permissions before execution, and identifying claim denials worth appealing. A growing pattern: a fraction-of-a-cent check before each AI agent action, reinforcing spending limits and approval rules. Look for decisions that are frequent, narrow, data-heavy, governed and time-sensitive.
What are the risks?
The uncomfortable arithmetic: a well-calibrated model at 90% confidence is still wrong one time in ten. At 1 million decisions a day, a 1% error rate means 10,000 mistakes. A structured answer isn't necessarily a correct one — structure makes errors easier to detect, not to eliminate.
Other traps:
Calibration varies by domain; test against your own labeled cases before setting thresholds
The model sees only the inputs provided — missing records produce confident wrong answers
High-stakes calls in credit, employment, housing, insurance, education, healthcare or legal rights need human oversight; the EU AI Act requires override capability for certain high-risk systems
Practical next steps are unglamorous but proven: start with existing data-platform contracts, integrate through a common interface to avoid lock-in, demand vendor calibration results, and treat thresholds as versioned policy — checking whether 80%-confidence predictions actually prove right 80% of the time.
A layer that answers narrow questions for fractions of a cent doesn't replace agents or LLMs — it decides when they should be trusted.

Speed claims face skepticism
I keep coming back to one small question: why do we ask a model to write a paragraph when all our software wants is a yes, a no, or a number? This is my notebook on how Jev answered it, who answered back, and what I would try first. Written 19 November 2026.
  The short version
On 15 September 2026, TypeSafe AI released 
Jev
, a model that never writes text. You hand it some state (text or JSON) plus typed questions, and it hands back a choice, a score or a probability.
What drew me in is how ordinary the problem is. Agent loops pay frontier-LLM prices, and wait seconds, for tiny calls like routing, gating and relevance checks that software then turns back into an 
if
 statement.
In about eight weeks, Cloudflare, OpenAI, Microsoft, AWS, Google and PostHog shipped their own decision models or APIs, and at least seven open-source projects showed up.
I also want to be fair about the numbers. The headline figures (up to 193.6x faster, 444.6x cheaper) come from TypeSafe's own four workflows. Outside checks are more modest, and one viral demo did not survive being measured. So I read them as a hypothesis to test, not a result.
  Why this needed to exist
Picture an agent harness as a loop: the model proposes, the harness checks, the model carries on. When I look at what the harness actually checks, it is all small stuff. Which skill should handle this? Is this bash command safe? Is that tool result relevant? Should a human see this? Is the agent really done?
Each of those is a bounded judgment, and the usual answer was to ask a general LLM and parse whatever came back. Every decision then carries a model request, output parsing, recovery code for malformed answers, and waiting in line behind the previous call. LangChain's post on building a harness with Jev makes the same argument I find convincing: let the generative model plan and write, and move the small decisions somewhere cheaper.
TypeSafe's founder, Diogo Almeida, is a former OpenAI researcher and co-author of the InstructGPT paper. His bet is that autoregressive text generation is an awkward fit for software that only needs an answer from a fixed menu. The company calls this class 
System One models
 and describes the product as a function call: unstructured state in, typed probabilistic decisions out. The name nods to the Jevons paradox, which I find charming: make a judgment cheap enough and people will start making it constantly.
Most of what an agent harness asks a model is not "write me something". It is "which one?"
This is the launch thread most explainers link to, with the 20-200x faster and 40-400x cheaper framing:
  How Jev works, in plain words
Input:
 a state (any text or JSON) and a map of typed questions you define in code. Version 1.14 added image input.
Output:
 only values from the options you declared, so the answer can never come back malformed. It can still be wrong, and I will keep repeating that.
Primitives:
choice
 (pick one of N), 
score
 (rate against an ordered rubric) and 
noul
 (the probability that a yes/no statement is true).
Parallel, not sequential:
 every candidate and every question in a request is evaluated together instead of token by token.
Calibration:
 TypeSafe says it trains with Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), so a stated 0.8 should be right about 80% of the time for that kind of question.
Access:
 a hosted API at 
POST /v1/systemone
, $0.042 per million input tokens, free output. The weights are not public.

  How the field answered
1. The contract became the standard.
 State plus typed questions, returning probabilities. Cloudflare's Clef is described as API-compatible with Jev. Google's codelab shows 
djev-run

/v1/systemone

2. Several recipes competed for the same dish.
Purpose-built:
 Jev.
Small bidirectional encoders with a decision head:
 Laya and Von, a few hundred million parameters, runnable locally.
LLM fine-tunes with decision heads or LoRA:
 Nimble, Kev, NanoJev, Microsoft-Decision-1.
Pointer heads on a small LLM torso:
 AWS Strands Decider 2B swaps the text head for a tiny scoring head. PostHog Jeeves does the same on a 9B model, with a reasoning chain first.
Training-free wrappers reading option logits from an existing LLM:
 SemIf and Rizzo Flow.
A repurposed general model:
 OpenAI's first Decisions API version constrained GPT-6 Luna to structured answers and tuned it for fast first output. Its team has credited Jev as the inspiration, which I think is a lovely thing to admit.

  Meet the challengers
"Everyone copied Jev" is the lazy summary. When I read the launch notes one by one, each team made a different bet. Here they are in the order they shipped.
  PostHog Jeeves: the one that thinks first (29 Sep)
The bet:
 think before you decide, and give the weights away.
The model:
 an open 9B model built on Qwen3.5-9B with a LoRA adapter and a pointer head. It credits Kev as its inspiration.
The twist:
 it writes a short reasoning chain for each question before scoring the options, then returns calibrated probabilities through a Jev-compatible API.
Score (self-published):
 93.5% on the public JevBench tier, against 86.6% for Jev.
The price of thinking:
 roughly 0.3 seconds without reasoning, and a 3.3-second median (17.1 seconds at p90) with full reasoning chains.
Openness:
 Apache-2.0 weights, MIT code, and training code and data in the repo. You will need a CUDA GPU.
  OpenAI Decisions API (announced 29 Sep, beta 6 Oct, GA 22 Oct)
The bet:
 you already have a giant model, so teach it to answer like a small one.
How it was built:
 it runs on GPT-6 Luna. OpenAI's API product lead has said it was not on the roadmap four weeks before release. The first version used Luna without extra training: constrained to structured answers, answering several questions at once and tuned to return the first decision faster. He credited Jev with inspiring it.
What you get:
 text and image input, preset options, true/false conditions and scores. Latency is reported at 100-180 ms, down from 1-3 seconds, and up to 10x faster than calling Luna through the Responses API. Input is $0.10 per million tokens and output is not charged.
Who tried it:
 early customers included Box, Datadog, Ramp and Speak. Speak's CTO said the time between actions fell from 1.4 seconds to 0.13 seconds.
How it stacks up:
 at its October launch, TechCrunch reported Jev ranking 3rd and OpenAI's model 14th on the Artificial Analysis Decision Index, so the pitch is speed and multimodality more than raw accuracy.
For scale:
 Vercel said nearly 13% of its paid teams tried Jev within a day of it reaching AI Gateway.

  Cloudflare Clef and Clef-flash (1 Oct)
The bet:
 the same API as Jev, open weights, and a way to fine-tune on your own traffic.
The models:
 the first models trained by the Workers AI team, at 27B (Clef) and 9B (Clef-flash), built on frozen Qwen backbones. Weights are Apache 2.0 on Hugging Face and both run on Workers AI.
Same API as Jev:
 switching means changing the endpoint and model name. Clef takes up to 64 questions per call, a 64K context (Jev's is 32K) and up to 4 images in the request.
Speed and price (vendor-reported):
 median latency of 209.3 ms for Clef and 38.8 ms for Clef-flash, against 524.1 ms for hosted Jev including network. Clef costs $0.24 per million input tokens and Clef-flash $0.09, versus Jev's $0.042.
Benchmarks (vendor-reported):
 a Clef model leads on 7 of 10 decision benchmarks, while Jev wins on When2Call and BRIGHT. On Cloudflare's Decision Index, Clef scores 61.21 to Jev's 57.91.

 Cloudflare's threat intelligence team classifies domains with Clef and Browser Run in 2.2 seconds, versus 4.7 seconds for gpt-oss-120b, which returned only two categories.
The bigger idea:
 an RL fine-tuning service. AI Gateway captures your traffic, Workers AI runs rollouts, Containers act as sandboxes, a new Trainer updates the weights and bring-your-own-model redeploys the result. It starts with forward-deployed engineers.
Caveats:
 the numbers are vendor-reported, no calibration figures have been published for Clef, and it costs more per token than Jev. Clef-omni and cheaper, faster variants followed around 9 Oct.

  AWS Strands Decider 2B (1 Oct)
The bet:
 small enough for a laptop, with everything open.
The model:
 a roughly 2B open model on a Qwen3.5-2B torso. The text-generating head is removed and replaced by a pointer head of about 1M parameters that scores the answer options. Later checkpoints added Gemma 4 models up to 26B.
Where it runs:
 locally only, with no hosted API. About 106-115 ms median on an RTX 3090 and about 153 ms on an M3 MacBook for small tasks.

 72.3% of 231 public JevBench tasks, with documented weaknesses on long multi-step documents and unfamiliar scoring rubrics. A rival open model, Mapika decider-2b, reportedly scored 76% on the same benchmark.
Openness:
 Apache 2.0, with weights, training data and scripts released.

  Microsoft Decision-1 (9 Oct)

  What it changed for harnesses
The pattern I see in working setups is almost a mantra: 
code builds the options, the decision model picks, code acts.
 The model is a stateless judge with no memory, tools or text output, so the harness supplies everything else. These are the seats where it shows up:
Router:
 choose which model or agent handles a request.
Gate:
 score a tool call, such as a bash command, before it runs. LangChain's 
AutoModeMiddleware
 packages this.
Selector:
 pick the next tool, skill or browser action from a menu rebuilt after every step.
Verifier:
 check whether the agent really finished, at the claimed "done" point rather than every turn.
Triage:
 send low-confidence cases to a human or a larger model.
Lessons I would borrow from people who have built these:
Always include a "none of these" option, because the model will otherwise pick something.
Put facts it cannot know into the state. One builder reports that adding the current date moved an answer from 0.35 to 0.94.
Batch independent questions in one call, but run dependent ones first.
Decide what "unsure" means before you ship.
Benchmark against a dumb baseline, such as a constant answer. It is humbling and useful.
Of all of these, deciding what "unsure" means is the one I would settle first. Builders have shared a few starting points:
Field note:
 a concrete example from Cloudflare's AI Engineer talk: in its build-failure demo, decision models handle tool selection and log extraction while a large reasoning model only diagnoses. That cut six LLM calls to one and about 40K tokens to 1.8K. It is a vendor demo, but it shows the division of labor I find most sensible.
  Where I stay skeptical
I like this idea a lot, which is exactly why I want to be careful with it.
TypeSafe's launch claims were 20-200x faster and 40-400x cheaper, best case 193.6x and 444.6x across four of its own workflows. Several analysts note these are the high end of selected evaluations.
An academic evaluation (Deußer et al., cited in the DICE paper) tested Jev on 37 datasets: strong zero-shot performance and well-calibrated choice probabilities, but boolean outputs may need per-task threshold tuning and fine-grained classification and rubric judging remain hard.
A widely viewed Jev-as-context-compaction demo (a repost claimed a session fell from nearly 1M tokens to 86K) was measured by Teknium on Nous's compaction eval. At the same budget it tied simple recency. The criticism targeted the compaction strategy more than Jev, but the lesson stays with me: a demo that looks identical whether or not the scoring works proves nothing.
One Vercel engineer's swap of an OpenAI model behind a command-safety classifier for Jev was reported as 5-18x faster with better accuracy. I only found this in a secondary write-up, so verify before repeating it.

  How to start without fooling yourself
Pick one bounded fork in your harness: a router, a gate or a done-check.
Build a small golden set of real cases with labels you trust.
Run it through a decision model, your current LLM and a trivial baseline.
Define an escalation threshold and send everything below it to a larger model or a person.
Measure end-to-end latency, cost and cache behavior, not only single-call numbers.
Keep irreversible actions behind deterministic code. A typed probability is evidence, not authority.
The takeaway:
 a reasoning model plans and writes, a decision model judges at every bounded fork, and plain code holds the authority to act.

Models have been superhuman at chat for years, so where is all the automation?
This has been my driving question for the last four years. At OpenAI, I helped build the methods that made language models useful at following instructions and talking with people. That work ended up as the research behind ChatGPT.  At the time, I thought maybe chat models would lead to AGI, but despite the hype it became obvious to me that there was something really big missing.
After two years in stealth, countless technical challenges, and research breakthroughs‚Ä¶ I am beyond excited to announce that today, TypeSafe AI is releasing our first 
System One Model

We built a new stack entirely focused on automation: with a new model architecture, parallel sampler for maximum efficiency, and training method we call Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD).
Our first public model is

Workflow evals
We made a new type of evaluation to measure how well AI works within code. We don‚Äôt optimize for a ground truth classification or allow the harness and model to change (potentially allowing for overfitting via harness engineering). Instead, we assume there is a correct compute graph (a ‚Äúworkflow‚Äù represented in code) and use the predictions of the largest, smartest, and most expensive external models as reference probabilities.
Rephrased: every model gets the same workflow. We test how they compare to the average of the smartest models (in this case, Astra and Fable).
Jev is off the charts ‚Äì owning the Pareto frontier for almost 2 orders of magnitude. We also compare to models with a generated prompt doing all the logic in their chain-of-thought, but this tends to do significantly worse than using the workflow itself.

We Give A FAQ
Where do the names ‚ÄúSystem One Models‚Äù and ‚ÄúJev‚Äù come from?
We were inspired by Daniel Kahneman, 
Thinking, Fast and Slow
. The model class name draws on the distinction between fast, intuitive System 1 thinking and slow, deliberate System 2 reasoning.
‚ÄúSystem 1 thinking‚Äù has also implied error-prone. For reasons we will get into in the future, we believe System One Models can be made more reliable than their alternatives.
We named Jev after William Stanley Jevons. We expect machine intelligence to follow a similar path to coal, after steam-engine efficiency led to an increase in demand. Every order of magnitude drop in the cost of intelligence unlocks orders of magnitude more use cases.
