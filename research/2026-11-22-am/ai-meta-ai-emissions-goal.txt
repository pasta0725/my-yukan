Meta, the owner of Facebook and Instagram, has said it is reconsidering its 2030 deadline for reaching net-zero emissions because of the artificial intelligence (AI) boom, in the latest sign of a broad corporate retreat from climate pledges.
The company is “re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing and interim milestones, including the 2030 timeframe for our net zero emissions goal”, Rachel Peterson, Meta’s vice president of data centres, and Blair Swedeen, its global head of net zero and sustainability, said in the company’s annual sustainability report on Thursday.

Meta reported that its net carbon-equivalent emissions rose 45 percent in 2025 to 11.8 million tonnes, a figure that is itself based on accounting methods that climate advocates view with scepticism.
“When we set our net zero emissions goal, we anticipated steady growth, falling clean energy costs, and changes in the broader system that would enable progress at the pace we envisioned. The reality has been far more complex,” the executives wrote in the report. They said delivering on Meta’s vision “requires building the infrastructure that powers AI”.
What was the pledge?
In 2020, Meta set a goal of net-zero emissions across its entire value chain by 2030, in line with the Paris Agreement, which aims to hold long-term warming to 1.5C (2.7F) above pre-industrial levels.
Net zero means eliminating emissions as far as possible and buying credits from carbon removal projects to offset those that cannot be eliminated. The value chain covers emissions from sources such as purchased goods and services, as well as capital goods such as equipment, buildings and vehicles.

Data centres’ growing footprint
A study published this week in the journal Nature Sustainability found that 403 of the most powerful data centres in the United States accounted for about 2 percent of the country’s electricity use and a carbon footprint comparable to Denmark’s, the AFP news agency reported.
About 1,200 such “hyperscale” data centres are online, and hundreds more are planned. Globally, data centres used about 415 terawatt hours of electricity in 2024, about 1.5 percent of supply, and that could nearly double by 2030, according to the International Energy Agency.
A wider retreat
During US President Donald Trump’s second term, major American businesses have stepped back from environmental commitments.
The trend is not confined to Big Tech. General Motors, which set a goal of selling only zero-emission vehicles by 2035, backed the Trump administration’s weakening of a fuel economy rule this year.
Walmart acknowledged in 2024 that it would miss its first emissions target. Google, which pledged in 2020 to run on carbon-free energy by 2030, said this year that its emissions were still rising.
Coca-Cola and Pepsi have changed the baseline years for their emissions targets and weakened their ambitions.

Company reported a 45 percent increase in carbon footprint due to AI demand

Social networking firm Meta is reevaluating its 2030 net-zero timeline amid record carbon emission levels, according to its 
latest sustainability report
.
In the report, the company revealed its net carbon footprint increased by 45 percent in the last year, hitting 11.9 million metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (mtCO2e). The company’s location-based emissions inventory was recorded at almost twice that amount, at 21.7 million mtCO2e.
– Getty Images
Rachel Peterson, Meta’s VP of data centers, and Blair Swedeen, its global head of net zero and sustainability, wrote that the company is “re-evaluating our timelines, sequencing, and interim milestones.” That includes the 2030 net zero timeframe and its near-term intensity targets. They said demand for AI infrastructure has grown faster than expected, while interconnection queues, permitting, and transmission buildout have lagged. Meta did not give a new date and said it will share the outcome later.
"The path forward is complex, and the route may continue to evolve, but the outcomes we are working toward remain unchanged: to establish a long-term pathway to net zero emissions, to restore more water than we consume, and to build infrastructure in a way that supports surrounding communities and grids," they said in a statement.
The numbers reflect growing emissions across all the major hyperscalers, with Amazon, Google, and Microsoft all reporting double-digit increases in their latest sustainability reports. 
Google
 saw an 18 percent year-on-year increase, with Amazon reporting a 
16 percent increase
 and 
Microsoft
 revealing that its emissions had grown by 25 percent. Of the hyperscalers, Meta is the only company to announce a reevaluation of its 2030 commitment, acknowledging the rise, which it attributes to the expansion of AI data centers.
Much of the emissions growth is tied to increasing reliance on natural gas to circumvent grid connection delays in the US. The report stated that Meta may deploy on-site gas where utilities cannot deliver power and plans to buy low-methane certificates to offset some of its emissions. The company left the RE100 climate initiative in 
July
 this year, saying it could no longer meet the technical criteria because of investments in new gas power. Last year, the company signed a deal with utility Entergy to fund the construction of 
ten natural gas plants
 to power its up to 5GW Hyperion data center in Richland Parish, Louisiana.
The company reported that its electricity consumption rose 35 percent to 24.8TWh, but claimed it matched all of it with clean and renewable energy, stating that it has contracted more than 30GW of low-carbon power, almost 12GW of which is operational. The most notable deal referenced was 7.7GW in nuclear agreements it signed last year with 
Vistra, TerraPower, Oklo
, and 
Constellation
, which are expected to come online by 2035.
“In 2025, we contracted more than 10GW of clean and renewable energy, bringing our portfolio to more than 30GW globally. Meta was also named one of the top global corporate clean energy buyers for 2025 by BloombergNEF, spanning wind, solar, battery storage, geothermal, and nuclear energy,” 
said

On water, the company reported that it directly consumed 790 million gallons (2.99 billion liters) last year, 246 million (931 million liters) of which were in high-stress areas, and 26.1 billion gallons (98.8 billion liters) were embedded in the purchase of electricity. 
Direct water consumption was down 4 percent year-on-year. However, high-stress consumption surged 24 percent, and electricity-related water consumption rose 37 percent. Notably, Meta is the only hyperscaler to report indirect water use, a growing concern as data centers shift to closed-loop cooling systems that, despite using less water at source, require more electricity to run.
On community concerns, the report stated that Meta pays the full costs of the infrastructure needed to serve it, citing the non-binding Ratepayer Protection Pledge. The company cited its Beaver Dam, Wisconsin, data center as evidence, where it has pledged almost $200 million to support the build-out of energy infrastructure through custom utility rates.
