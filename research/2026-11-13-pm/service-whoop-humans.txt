Whoop
, the maker of screen-less fitness bands, is launching a social network on Thursday that lets its members follow friends and family and share their health data.
The network, called Whoop Humans, will be rolled out in the Whoop app to members at every membership tier at no extra cost. Members can follow anyone on Whoop and choose what to share, from sleep and recovery to workouts and achievements. Followers can react and comment; members can also attach photos and commentary to their data. It’s all opt-in, CEO Will Ahmed tells us.
Whoop already has a social feature, Teams, which a little more than half its customers use, according to the company. But Teams is built around groups and leaderboards, and everyone in a group sees everyone else’s information. Humans is meant to be more intimate. Members decide who follows them and who sees what, so a trainer can track a set of clients without the clients seeing one another’s data, for example.
I found myself questioning how much a spouse wants to know about the other’s sleep score when Ahmed mentioned this particular scenario, so I asked him how else beta members used these newer social features. He said the company learned that people were more engaged when someone reported a low recovery score than a nearly perfect one, suggesting that the new feature may be particularly helpful for understanding when someone is struggling and might need attention.
Indeed, the bigger story here is where Whoop continues to be headed, which is into medical wellness. Like its most direct rival, Oura, the company is pushing faster and more aggressively into the wellness opportunity that an aging population presents.
Whoop had already added FDA-cleared ECG and atrial fibrillation detection, blood pressure insights, blood testing, and medical-record uploads. Asked if older customers are gravitating to Whoop, Ahmed said, without offering specifics, that Whoop has seen “huge growth” among people over 60 over the past 18 months. He also said his was among the first companies accepted into a Medicare program that 
reimburses qualifying wearables
 that can show they improve health outcomes, and that it now has Medicare members.
He added that he expects families to be big users of Humans. (You can imagine adult children checking in on aging parents.)
But the company’s efforts to be thought of as the “brand for personal health” raises questions, naturally, about privacy. Ahmed said members choose which metrics to share, can remove followers, and can switch on “stealth mode” to go dark for a few days without changing their settings. Activities marked sensitive aren’t shared by default, and Ahmed said health alerts and medical information aren’t shared at all. Members can also report any comment or photo that they deem disturbing, and the company said reported content is reviewed.
As for minors, Whoop’s minimum age is 13 for all members (higher in some countries), meaning customers of that age can share their data subject to consents and privacy settings. Asked whether a parent could follow a teenager’s data, he didn’t answer directly and pointed to the privacy controls.
Ahmed also said Whoop doesn’t sell member data. “Our product is not you; it’s the membership,” he said, adding that the company doesn’t license data to third parties. 
He was candid about dealmaking. Asked about Oura’s announcement this week that it will pay 
$625 million for Counsel Health
, a service offering on-demand physicians, Ahmed said he expects “more consolidation” among AI-focused, clinician-focused, and wearable companies, and that Whoop is always on the prowl. Its deep relationship with members, he said, puts it “in a position to be an acquirer.”
He was more guarded about the company’s own finances, declining to share revenue or say whether Whoop is profitable. He did say that the company now has about 1,200 employees, membership on its software platform has increased more than 70% a year for three years running, and that it expects to have nearly 4 million members by year-end.
As for an IPO (the company raised a 
very big round
 earlier this year), he said only that Whoop “will certainly IPO in the future.” He also wouldn’t comment on Oura, which shelved its own IPO of up to $2.2 billion in September citing market uncertainty.
In the meantime, Whoop is widening its reach. The company announced that its devices will be sold through Ulta stores beginning next March (around 500 locations of the beauty retailer to start, and a target to retail in more than a thousand stores eventually). Indeed, Ahmed said Whoop’s female audience has more than doubled from last year.
As for competition, in addition to Google’s new 
Fitbit Air band,
 Whoop may soon have to contend with Apple, which is reportedly developing a 
Whoop-style band
. But Ahmed mostly shrugged off those concerns, suggesting that building a product doesn’t guarantee success. Instead, he said, Whoop’s job is to stay true to the “aspiring” people it serves.

===== https://www.wareable.com/wearable-tech/whoop-humans-social-network-feed-announcement-features-rollout
(status 200)
The in-app feed allows Whoop users to share recovery stats and milestones with their network
Whoop
 has announced a left-field expansion beyond individual health tracking with Whoop Humans, a new in-app social networking feed built around member health data.
Now rolling out to the Whoop app, the platform is pitched by the 
screenless tracker
 brand as a direct counterweight to legacy social networks, which it notes are bogged down by bot accounts, generative AI clutter, and algorithmically driven engagement bait.
The defining hook of Whoop Humans is its verification layer: every active account on the network must be authenticated by a continuous heartbeat recorded via a Whoop device, such as the 
Whoop MG or Whoop 5.0
.
A network built on verified physiological data
Rather than relying on status updates or text posts, the feed centers on physiological metrics pulled directly from the hardware.
Members can selectively broadcast core markers, including Sleep, Recovery, Strain, Heart Rate Variability (HRV), and resting heart rate, giving friends and family direct visibility into their physical condition.
Credit: Whoop
The launch represents a significant commercial shift for Whoop, as it seeks to deepen user retention and expand member lifetime value.
While Whoop Teams already claims 57 percent of active users through group leaderboards, Humans refines that dynamic into one-to-one social feeds.
Users can also post 'Moments'—photo logs from workouts and lifestyle milestones tied to specific performance stats—bringing a familiar social aesthetic to raw biometric numbers.
Balancing social connection with biometric privacy
Broadcasting intimate physiological data carries obvious privacy hurdles—though this is an area Whoop is attempting to head off with granular user controls.
Wearers can toggle sharing permissions metric by metric—broadcasting sleep scores while masking heart rate variability, for instance.
There's also a dedicated 'Stealth Mode' that allows users to temporarily pause all public updates without resetting their profile configuration, while workouts marked as sensitive are suppressed from the feed by default. Whoop also reiterated its long-standing policy that it will not sell or use personal health data for ad targeting.
Whether users actually want their daily recovery scores turned into a social feed remains to be seen, but Whoop Humans makes one thing clear: the brand is betting its next phase of growth on turning health tracking into a shared, interconnected experience.

Whoop Humans is rolling out to subscribers now
It lets you connect directly with family and friends
You get full control over the goals and stats shared
Whoop is expanding its app offerings to let you connect up with friends and family rather than just focusing on your own fitness: it's called Whoop Humans, and it lets you share your stats, goals, and achievements with a selected network of contacts.
It's similar to what you can do 
with a Strava account
, though Whoop is pushing the privacy angle hard: there are granular controls over what's shared and with whom, the option to keep certain stats private permanently or temporarily, and a promise not to "sell or monetize" any of the data collected.
"In a world that's becoming increasingly artificial, we're building something deeply human," says Whoop CEO and founder Will Ahmed. "This is the most verifiably human network ever launched."
