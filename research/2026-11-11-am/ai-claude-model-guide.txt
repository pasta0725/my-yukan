Which Claude model should you build with? Fable, Opus, Sonnet, or Haiku
Which Claude model to pick, what they cost, and how to use them together.

The current Claude lineup has four generally available models: Claude Haiku 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.2. Claude Mythos 5.2 is our most capable model for cybersecurity and life sciences research, available through our verification programs.
If you build on the Claude Platform or work in Claude Code, you may be wondering which model to use for your common tasks. This article will help answer that question and how to think about the trade-offs.

One photo of an alley, rendered in code by Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2. Each image is a Python program the model wrote, calling a drawing engine it also wrote, shown from first layer to finished. Demo by Lance Martin.
TL;DR
The quick answer is that you should start most work on Opus 5.5 at its default effort level, 
medium
.
There's a reason it's the default model in Claude Code. It costs half as much per input and output token as Fable 5.2, and it's the model we recommend starting with for most coding and knowledge work.
From there:
Move down to Sonnet 5.5 for well-scoped everyday work, like a bug fix or a feature built against a spec. Just because you have a clear spec doesn't mean you should use Sonnet, though. A migration or a performance optimization can have clear specs and still be difficult enough to warrant Opus 5.5, or Fable 5.2 for a major one.
Move further down to Haiku 5.5 for the simplest high-volume work, such as classification, routing, and extraction. Haiku 5.5 is also good to use with subagents that work on a larger model's side tasks.
Move up to Claude Fable 5.2 for the hardest technical projects in coding, finance and the sciences. Also move up when Opus 5.5 at 
high
 still falls short, or for long unattended runs.
Before you move models, try adjusting your effort level. It's a finer dial for both cost and capability. Past 
high
, one more effort level can cost as much as a bigger model, so compare both on your own work. If you're past 
high
, test the next model up at a lower effort instead. Conversely, one level down can get you the result you need at a lower rate.
Higher effort helps when the model needs to think longer, call more tools, or check its own work more. Just keep in mind that it also adds latency. Additionally, moving to a higher effort level won't give a model knowledge or skill it doesn't have. For that, you still need to move up a model.

THE TRADE-OFFS
When you choose between models, you're making a trade-off. When you pick a larger model, for example, you are choosing capability over latency. These are the main trade-offs to think through.
Capability and cost per finished task
Generally, bigger models cost more per token and are more capable, but the cost vs capability trade-off isn't universally true at the task level.
A higher price per token doesn't always mean a higher cost per task, or a better result. As we showed in 
Spending your effort
, on Anthropic's runs of Terminal-Bench 3.0, the previous Fable, 5.1, at 
max
 scored 58.0%, while Opus 5.5 at 
high
 scored 58.9% with half the tokens.
On the hardest work, the price gap can close. On FrontierCode 2.0, Fable 5.2 at 
high
 scored about level with Opus 5.5 at 
max
 (45.3% vs. 45.5%) for roughly half the cost per task. So before you raise Opus 5.5 past 
high
 on a hard task, test Fable 5.2. For everyday work, Opus 5.5 at 
medium
 is still the place to start. For benchmark results, see the 
launch post
.
However, the Claude Platform's 
cost and intelligence measurements
 also show the limits of a small model. These generally do well on high-volume work with checkable outputs and are less suited to long agentic loops.
When you compare two models, price the hardest tasks in your set as well as the typical ones, because the hardest tasks carry most of the spend. On one 20-problem search benchmark, two problems carried 43% of the spend.

Latency against accuracy
A faster setup finishes sooner and can miss more. Think through how long a person can wait before you pick your model and effort level. For replies a person waits on, start Sonnet 5.5 at 
low
 or 
medium
.
Often next generation models are faster than their predecessors. Fable 5.2 generates output up to 30% faster than Fable 5.1. Sonnet 5.5 runs 30% faster than Sonnet 5.
Effort level and latency are roughly correlated. Determine if the accuracy you gain is worth the extra latency. For example, on SWE-bench Pro, Opus 5.5 at 
medium
 scored about 2.5 points lower for about 70% of the cost as the 
high
 effort setting. At 
low
 it scored about 8 points lower for about a third of the cost. At 
xhigh
 it scored about 1.4 points higher for 2.5 times the cost of 
high
.
Often providing a means of self-verification is more cost effective and adds less latency than changing effort levels. When a model isn't good enough for a task, take these steps in order:
Check that the model can verify its own work, with a test, a build or a script that calls the endpoint.
Raise effort one level.
Move to the next model up, starting at a lower effort level than you used on the smaller one.
If you can check outcomes automatically, run everything at a low level and re-run only the failures at a high one. On the same SWE-bench Pro subset, Opus 5.5 at 
low
 with failures re-run at 
high
 passed about 97% of tasks for about $0.17 each, against 95.3% for $0.29 with every task at 
high
.
If you need a faster reply from the same model, you can pay for speed instead of giving up capability. On the Claude API, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 both offer 
fast mode
, with higher output speed at premium pricing.
Context against cost
Every model in the current generation has a 1M-token context window. Large context can help the model complete the task but it costs you on every turn, because each turn resends it. That being said, 
on long runs most of your input bills as cache reads
.
Opus 5.5 charges $0.20 per million tokens for a cache read, and Sonnet 5.5 charges $0.10. Fable 5.2 charges the same $0.20, so on a long, cache-heavy run the gap between Fable and Opus comes down to uncached input and output, where Fable costs twice as much per token.
In 
What a task costs on Opus 5.5
, a turn's cache read costs about $0.03 at 150K tokens of context. At 20K it costs about $0.004.
One takeaway from this is that a lean main context matters more the bigger the main model is. Keep the expensive model's context for the decisions, and let cheaper subagents do the verbose reading.
Haiku 5.5 is the only model where the token price varies by prompt length. A prompt of up to 100K tokens bills at the lower tier, and a prompt over 100K bills at five times that rate. The long-prompt tier still costs a quarter of Sonnet 5.5's per-token price. So Haiku 5.5 pays off most on short prompts, where it costs a twentieth of Sonnet 5.5's input price.
When every call needs a long prompt, compare Haiku 5.5 with Sonnet 5.5 on cost per finished task, not list price.

Switch at a natural break
The prompt cache belongs to the model that wrote it. Your first turn on a new model pays the cache-write price on the whole conversation. To make that turn smaller, run 
/compact
 first in Claude Code, or start a fresh session with a short written plan. On the API, open the new model's conversation with a summary of the work so far instead of the full history.
Each thinking block records the model that produced it, and each model reads only some other models' blocks. On the Claude API and Claude Models on Google Cloud (Google managed), Opus 5.5 reads Sonnet 5.5's and Haiku 5.5's thinking blocks. Sonnet 5.5 reads Haiku 5.5's. Fable 5.2 reads Opus 5.5's, Sonnet 5.5's and Haiku 5.5's. On the Claude API, Fable 5.1 reads Opus 5.5's.
A move down a tier loses the earlier reasoning, because a model never reads the blocks of a model above it. Other moves can lose it too. When the new model can't read the earlier model's thinking blocks, the API drops them and the request still succeeds. The new model then works without that reasoning.
Preserved thinking
 lists every case. A move from Fable 5.2 to any other generally available model drops its reasoning.

Escalate hard decisions to an advisor
The 
advisor tool
 (beta) pairs a faster, cheaper executor model with a stronger advisor model. The executor runs the task and can ask the advisor for guidance partway through. Most of the tokens bill at the executor's rate. The advisor helps only when it adds capability the executor lacks and the executor asks for advice, which it does less often at low effort.
Where it might pay off: agentic work where most turns are routine. The platform's cost guide is cautious: of the four pairings in its advisor table, none clearly beat its executor alone, and an advisor can help only when the executor consults it. Measure your executor's consult rate first. Check which advisors your executor accepts. A Sonnet 5.5 executor accepts Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Opus 5 and Sonnet 5.5, for example, and a Fable 5.2 executor accepts only Fable 5.2 or Mythos 5.2.
Where it adds little: one of those pairings was an Opus 5.5 executor at 
high
 with a Fable 5.1 advisor on an internal coding benchmark. It scored 90.1% at $2.92 per attempt. That's 1.7 points over Opus 5.5 alone at 
high
 for about 2.1 times the money. A gain that size is at the edge of run-to-run noise, and about what raising effort would have bought.
Delegate bulk work to cheaper workers
Subagents, above, handle side tasks while one main model keeps the thread. This pattern is for work bigger than one context window. A top-tier model leads, splits the task, hands the pieces to many cheaper workers in parallel, and merges what comes back. On an internal defect-search benchmark over about 22 million tokens of source code, an Opus 5.5 lead over Sonnet 5.5 workers, up to 25 at a time, cost about a third less than Opus 5.5 alone at its default effort. It scored about the same.
On the Claude Platform, 
multiagent orchestration
 in Claude Managed Agents gives you a coordinator agent and a roster of workers. Skip it for one chain of dependent steps or for work that fits in one context window. There, the planning and merging cost more than they save. 
When to fold subagents into your main agent loop
 covers when one agent loop beats a team of subagents.
CHECK COMPATIBILITY BEFORE MIGRATING
Model choice is mostly a question of capability and cost, but you can't always change the model name and nothing else. Every current model supports tool use and image input, but there are differences to test before you move a request from one model to another.
Fable 5.2 extends preserved thinking to every API account.
 The API rejects a request with a 400 error when anything before a Fable 5.2 thinking block has changed, including the system prompt, the tools or an earlier message, unless you opt in to dropping the affected thinking. If you build requests through Claude Code, the Claude Agent SDK or Claude Managed Agents, you don't need to change anything. If your own code edits history, keep it append-only, and test before you switch.
Thinking.
 Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 always think, and a request that turns thinking off returns an error. Sonnet 5.5 thinks by default, and 
between_tools
 limits thinking to the gaps between tool calls. Haiku 5.5 thinks by default, and you can turn thinking off at 
high
 effort or below. On all of them, read content blocks by 
type
, because a response can begin with a thinking block.

Safety fallbacks.
 Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.2 run safety classifiers that most often flag cybersecurity and biology content. In Claude Code, a flagged request can re-run on an older model, and a notice in the transcript tells you which. Fable 5.2 keeps the fallback models that Fable 5.1 uses. Flagged cybersecurity requests fall back to Opus 4.8, and flagged biology requests to Opus 5.
If one system runs several models, build every request for the strictest of them: keep history append-only, send 
tool_choice: auto
 with strict tools, read content blocks by 
type
, and write prompts and tool descriptions precisely enough for the most literal model. Then a step can move up or down a tier without a code change. When the routing rule is simple, such as one model for each step type, let code pick the model rather than asking a model to.
MEASURE ON YOUR OWN WORK
Published results, like the SWE-bench Pro and Terminal-Bench numbers above, get you a starting model. Then test the candidates on your own tasks before you commit.
Pull a sample of real tasks. Write an outcome check for each one, such as a passing test, a closed ticket or a correct row count.
Run each candidate model at two or three effort levels, each in its own session so that the runs don't share a cache. Record the cost of each completed task from the 
usage
 field of each response.

Anthropic Releases a Faster, Cheaper Top Model, but Says Most Users Should Start Elsewhere
AI Safety & Risk
AI Business & Money
Anthropic
Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 on Monday, its most capable models, built for what it calls "the most ambitious, open-ended technical work." The two are the same underlying model. Fable, available on all paid Claude plans and major cloud platforms, carries safeguards that block most cybersecurity and biology research tasks. Mythos, without some of those limits, goes mainly to vetted organizations doing cybersecurity or biomedical work.
Anthropic says the new models are a step above Opus 5.5 in almost all the capabilities it evaluated, and up to 30% faster than the versions they replace. Fable 5.2 costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10 and $50. On a test built from real freelance projects, it did at least as well as a human professional 41.3% of the time, about double Opus 5.5's 21.3%. It didn't win everywhere: Opus 5.5 rated higher on two knowledge-work measures, and Google's Gemini 4 Carbon beat it on a test of business workflows.
In testing, Mythos found a new way to predict the structure of capsids, the protein shells that deliver gene therapies. Anthropic also says that "over 90% of the changes merged into Anthropic's core codebase are now written by Claude (with human guidance)." Testers found it communicates more clearly, Anthropic says, though METR called one of its research reports "extremely lengthy."
The 264-page system card takes up the question safety researchers watch most: is AI speeding up its own development? Anthropic concludes the model does not cross its own threshold, doubling the pace of AI research, but calls that judgment uncertain, one that "becomes more so with each advance." An outside review by METR last week estimated AI is already roughly doubling the pace at Anthropic, measured a different way (D.A.D., November 6). Testing a stronger prerelease version, METR called the model "a substantial step" up on tasks with clear right answers, but saw little evidence of better research judgment. Anthropic staff described it as "good at working towards a precise goal, not great at discovering new scientific facts."
Anthropic still rates the risk of the model behaving dangerously as low, but says its safety testing gives somewhat weaker evidence for that than it did for its previous top model. One reason: Mythos 5.2 may have been aware it was being evaluated in as many as 43% of one set of tests. And when told to deliberately underperform without getting caught, it slipped past a monitor reading its reasoning about 1 in 4 times, the first Anthropic model to do so a meaningful share of the time.
Early reviews are strong, with caveats. Business-school professor 
Ethan Mollick
, who had early access, calls it "almost" AGI. Its business plan would have impressed him coming from his MBA students, he writes, but it missed its own failures, from a 182-page research paper to an 87,500-word rule book its agents wrote for themselves. "Most critically, the AI did not notice these problems itself."
Claire Vo
, host of the How I AI podcast, had soured on Claude but says Fable 5.2 won her back with its speed, creativity and ease of use. "You hooked me back," she told Anthropic. Her caveats: its web designs are tasteful when it has a style guide to follow but ugly when left to its own devices, and she still turns to Opus 5.5 for the things Fable does poorly.
Why it matters:
 In one of Anthropic's tests, the model took on real freelance assignments and did at least as well as a human professional on about four in ten, double the previous model's rate. For anyone whose work comes in projects, like a report, an analysis or a design, that's a sign AI is moving from helping with pieces of the job to sometimes doing the whole thing. And the system card is candid that the tools for checking these models, and for judging whether AI is accelerating itself, are getting less reliable just as the models get stronger. These are the company's own results; outside evaluators will have the next word.

Which Claude Should You Use? Anthropic's Own Guide Says Start in the Middle
AI Adoption
Anthropic
With a new top model out, Anthropic has published a guide to choosing among its four main models, and its advice is not to reach for the most powerful one. "The quick answer is that you should start most work on Opus 5.5 at its default effort level, medium," writes Anthropic's Addy Osmani. Opus 5.5 costs half as much per unit of text as the new Fable 5.2.
From there, the guide says to move:
Down to Sonnet 5.5
 for well-defined everyday work, including ordinary documents, slides and spreadsheets. For a complex financial model, or a document built from a large pile of source material, it says to start on Opus 5.5.
Further down to Haiku 5.5
 for simple, high-volume jobs like sorting, tagging and pulling data out of documents.
Up to Fable 5.2
 for the hardest projects in coding, finance and the sciences, deep research, or when Opus still falls short.
Before switching models, it suggests changing the "effort" setting, which controls how long the model thinks and how much it checks its own work. More effort costs more and takes longer, and it has limits: "moving to a higher effort level won't give a model knowledge or skill it doesn't have." List price doesn't settle cost, either. On one hard coding test, Fable 5.2 matched Opus 5.5 pushed to its maximum setting for roughly half the cost per task. The guide's last word: test the options on your own real work before committing.
For Fable specifically, Anthropic's 
Alex Albert
 suggests asking what's wrong with your idea before it builds anything, telling it up front what needs your approval, and handing it a whole project to run for hours rather than one step at a time. "This is where it shines," he wrote.
Why it matters:
 Most professionals pick an AI model by habit or by whichever is newest. Anthropic is telling customers that its flagship is overkill for most of what they do, which is unusual advice from a company selling it, and a useful reminder that the right setting depends on the task, not the leaderboard. The same logic applies to ChatGPT and Gemini: match the model to the job, and check whether a cheaper one does it well enough.

===== https://promtime.net/p/en/post/raise-effort-before-switching-claude-models-anthropic-says
(status 200)
Image:
claude.dev
Anthropic published a model-selection guide on the 
Claude developer blog
 on November 10. It starts most coding and knowledge work on Opus 5.5 at its default medium effort and tells developers to add a self-check or raise effort before reaching for a bigger model. If your outputs can be checked automatically, the cheapest route the guide describes is to run everything at low effort and re-run only the failures at high. On a SWE-bench Pro subset, Opus 5.5 run that way passed about 97% of tasks for about $0.17 each, against 95.3% for $0.29 with every task at high.
The guide covers the four generally available models: Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2. Mythos 5.2, which Anthropic calls its most capable model for cybersecurity and life sciences research, is available only through its verification programs. Opus 5.5 is already the Claude Code default on Pro, Max, Ultra, Team, Enterprise and the Anthropic API.
Before you switch models
When a model falls short, the guide gives three steps in order. First, make sure the model can verify its own work, with a test, a build or a script that calls the endpoint. Then raise effort one level. Only after that, move to the next model up and start it at a lower effort than you used on the smaller one. Effort sets how long the model thinks, how many tools it calls and how much it checks itself. It adds latency, and it won’t give a model knowledge or skill it doesn’t have.
The returns thin out near the top. On SWE-bench Pro, Opus 5.5 at medium scored about 2.5 points below its high setting for about 70% of the cost, and at low about 8 points below for about a third of the cost. Going up to xhigh bought about 1.4 points for 2.5 times the cost of high. Past high, the guide warns, one more effort level can cost as much as a bigger model.
At that point Fable 5.2 is the alternative to test. On Anthropic’s FrontierCode 2.0 runs, Fable 5.2 at high scored 45.3% against 45.5% for Opus 5.5 at max, for roughly half the cost per task. So before raising Opus 5.5 past high on a hard task, the guide says to try Fable 5.2. A generation earlier the gap ran the other way: on Anthropic’s Terminal-Bench 3.0 runs, Fable 5.1 at max scored 58.0%, while Opus 5.5 at high scored 58.9% with half the tokens.
When you compare two models, price your hardest tasks as well as the typical ones. On one 20-problem search benchmark, two problems carried 43% of the spend.
Which model for which job
Opus 5.5, $4 / $20 per million input and output tokens: everyday engineering and debugging, multi-PR feature work, computer use and vision-heavy workflows. Start at medium, then go to high.
Sonnet 5.5, $2 / $10: well-scoped coding such as bug fixes and spec-driven features, chat, documents, slides and spreadsheets, and repeated agent tasks like review or drafting. A clear spec alone doesn’t qualify a task. A migration or a performance optimization can have one and still need Opus 5.5, or Fable 5.2 for a major one.
Haiku 5.5, $0.10 / $0.50 for prompts up to 100K tokens and $0.50 / $2.50 above that: classification, routing, tagging and extraction at volume, plus subagents that search files, read logs or summarize.
Fable 5.2, $8 / $40: major refactors and migrations, architecture planning, leading many subagents, deep research and quantitative analysis, long unattended runs, and cases where Opus 5.5 at high still falls short.
All four have a 1M-token context window and 128K tokens of maximum output. Default effort differs by model. On the API it is medium for Haiku 5.5 and Opus 5.5 and high for Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.2, which both drop to medium in Claude Code, where 
/effort
 shows the current setting.
Cache pricing changes the comparison on long runs, where most input bills as cache reads. Fable 5.2 charges the same $0.20 per million cached tokens as Opus 5.5, against $0.10 for Sonnet 5.5. On a cache-heavy run, the gap between Fable and Opus therefore comes down to uncached input and output, where Fable costs twice as much per token. Haiku 5.5 is the one model whose price depends on prompt length. On short prompts its input costs a twentieth of Sonnet 5.5’s, and its long-prompt tier still costs a quarter of Sonnet’s per-token price. If every call needs a long prompt, the guide says to compare the two on cost per finished task.
For replies a person waits on, the guide starts with Sonnet 5.5 at low or medium effort. Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 also offer a fast mode on the Claude API, with higher output speed at premium pricing.
Subagents and the haiku alias
A subagent bills at the rates of the model it runs on. In Claude Code you set that model with 
model: haiku
 or 
model: sonnet
 in the subagent’s definition, and 
CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_MODEL
 sets the default for subagents that don’t name one. The guide keeps small models on work where a mistake is cheap to spot, such as finding files, running tests and reading logs. Code edits and judgment calls stay on the main model.
Two details change the costs here. The built-in Explore subagent now runs on your session’s model rather than always on Haiku, and on the Claude API that model is capped at Opus. To keep exploration cheap, define your own subagent named Explore with 
model: haiku
. The haiku alias itself now resolves to Haiku 5.5 on the Anthropic API, which needs Claude Code v2.1.293 or later. On Amazon Bedrock, Google Cloud, Microsoft Foundry and Claude Platform on AWS it still resolves to Haiku 4.5. 
ANTHROPIC_DEFAULT_HAIKU_MODEL
 pins it.
The 
opusplan
 alias does the reverse of the guide’s advice. It plans with Opus and carries out the plan with Sonnet, which puts your code edits on Sonnet, so the guide suggests measuring it on your own tasks before making it the default.
For work bigger than one context window, the guide cites an internal defect-search benchmark over about 22 million tokens of source code. There, an Opus 5.5 lead running up to 25 Sonnet 5.5 workers at a time cost about a third less than Opus 5.5 alone at default effort and scored about the same. The guide says to skip this pattern for a chain of dependent steps or work that fits in one context window. The advisor tool, in beta, pairs a cheaper executor with a stronger advisor and shows thinner gains. An Opus 5.5 executor at high with a Fable 5.1 advisor scored 90.1% at $2.92 per attempt. That is 1.7 points over Opus 5.5 alone at high for about 2.1 times the money, and the guide places it at the edge of run-to-run noise.
What breaks when you switch
The prompt cache belongs to the model that wrote it, so the first turn on a new model pays the cache-write price on the whole conversation. Run 
/compact
 first in Claude Code, or open the new model’s conversation on the API with a summary of the work so far.
A model never reads thinking blocks from a model above it. The API drops unreadable blocks and the request still succeeds, so a move from Fable 5.2 to any other generally available model loses its reasoning without warning.
Fable 5.2 extends preserved thinking to every API account. A request returns a 400 error if anything before a Fable 5.2 thinking block has changed, including the system prompt, the tools or an earlier message, unless you opt in to dropping the affected thinking. Claude Code, the Claude Agent SDK and Claude Managed Agents need no changes. Code that edits history should be append-only.
Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 always think, and a request that turns thinking off returns an error. On every model, read content blocks by type, because a response can begin with a thinking block.
A 
tool_choice
 of 
any
 or 
tool
 returns a 400 error on Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2. Send 
auto
, mark the tool 
strict: true
 and say in the prompt when to use it.
Moving from Haiku 4.5 to Haiku 5.5 means replacing 
budget_tokens
 with an effort level. Non-default 
temperature
, 
top_p
 and 
top_k
 values return a 400 error, and so does a final assistant turn left for the model to continue.
In Claude Code, a request that the safety classifiers flag can re-run on an older model, and a notice in the transcript names it. Flagged cybersecurity requests go to Opus 4.8 and flagged biology requests to Opus 5.
Before committing to a setup, the guide recommends testing on your own work. Take a sample of real tasks, write an outcome check for each, and run every candidate at two or three effort levels in separate sessions. Then plot score against spend. A multi-model setup stays only if it beats every single-model point on that chart. For a quick version in Claude Code, run one task from your backlog on two models with 
/model
 and compare what 
/usage
 reports.

プロンプトのキャッシュは、それを書いたモデルのものです。新しいモデルに切り替えた最初のターンは、会話全体をキャッシュに書き込む料金がかかります。ガイドは、Claude Codeなら切り替える前に 
/compact
 を実行するか、短い計画を書いて新しいセッションを始めることを勧めています。また、上位のモデルから下位のモデルに移ると、それまでの思考の内容は引き継がれません。
作業の途中でモデルを行き来するより、「設計はFable、実装はOpus」のように工程の区切りで分けるほうが、費用の面でも結果の面でも無駄が少ないということです。Claude Codeでのモデルの指定方法は
Claude Codeのモデル設定
にまとめています。
2つのモデルを自分の作業で比べる
ガイドの最後は「自分の作業で測る」です。Claude Codeで手軽に確かめるなら、手元の作業を1つ選び、
/model
 でモデルを変えて2回実行し、
/usage
 の表示を比べる方法が紹介されています。公開されているベンチマークは出発点にすぎず、自分の作業でどちらが安く済むかは試さないと分からない、という立場です。
Fable 5.2はいつ使うべきか
Fable 5.2はOpus 5.5の2倍の単価ですが、ガイドは「1件あたりの費用」で考えるよう勧めています。難しい課題では差が縮まり、FrontierCode 2.0ではFable 5.2のhighがOpus 5.5のmaxとほぼ同じ点数（45.3%と45.5%）を、1件あたり約半分の費用で出したと書かれています。そのため、難しい作業でOpus 5.5をhighより上げようとしているなら、先にFable 5.2を試すべきだ、としています。
ただし、普段の作業の出発点は引き続きOpus 5.5のmediumです。プランごとのFableの扱い（Proは使用量クレジットから引かれる、Max・Ultraは週の上限の50%まで）や、11月16日までのローンチ特典は
Claude Fable 5.2とは
で解説しています。
かわんちゅの場合：モデルの使い分け
普段はOpusを使い、調査や設計のときはFableに切り替えています。Claude Codeではサブエージェントや並列実行も使っていて、/fast と effort レベルも作業によって切り替えています。
編集部の見解
このガイドの価値は、「どのモデルが一番賢いか」ではなく、「足りないときにどの順で手を打つか」を示した点にあると考えます。判断の基準を、副業で使う人向けにまとめると次のようになります。
結果を確かめる手段があるか
。テストやチェックリストを渡せない作業でモデルを上げても、良くなったかどうか自分で判断できない。先に確認の手段を用意する
その作業は何度も繰り返すか
。1回きりの難しい設計ならFableを使っても総額は小さい。毎日回す定型作業は、Sonnetで足りるかを最初に確かめる価値がある
間違いがすぐ分かるか
。ファイル探しや分類のように間違いがすぐ見えるものは小さいモデルに、顧客に出す文章や判断を伴う作業は大きいモデルに残す
向かないのは、「常に最上位のモデルを使えば安心」と考えて全部をFableやOpusのhighで回す使い方です。定額プランでは週の上限を早く使い切り、APIでは費用が大きく膨らみます。逆に、ガイドの表をそのまま当てはめれば、多くの人は今より上限に余裕を持って使えるはずです。
よくある質問
よくある質問
Q
Claudeのモデルは結局どれを使えばいいですか？
▼
A
Anthropicの2026年11月9日のガイドでは、迷ったらOpus 5.5を既定のeffort（medium）で使い始めることを勧めています。範囲がはっきりした作業はSonnet 5.5、単純で大量の作業はHaiku 5.5、最も難しい技術的な作業はFable 5.2です。
Q
Fable 5.2とOpus 5.5はどちらが安いですか？
▼
A
APIの単価はOpus 5.5が入力$4・出力$20、Fable 5.2が入力$8・出力$40（100万トークンあたり）で、Opus 5.5が半分です。ただし難しい課題では、Fable 5.2のほうが1件あたりの費用が安くなる例もガイドに載っています。
Q
結果が物足りないときは、すぐモデルを上げるべきですか？
▼
A
ガイドは、先にモデルが自分の作業を確かめられる手段（テストなど）を与え、次にeffortを1段上げ、それでも足りなければ1つ上のモデルに低めのeffortで移る順番を勧めています。
Q
Claude Codeでサブエージェントのモデルを指定するには？
▼
A
サブエージェントの定義に model: haiku や model: sonnet を書きます。環境変数 CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_MODEL で、指定のないサブエージェントの既定モデルも決められます。
Q
Claude Codeの組み込みのExploreはHaikuで動きますか？
▼
A
ガイドによると、現在のClaude Codeではセッションのモデルで動きます。安く済ませたい場合は、Exploreという名前で model: haiku を指定した自分のサブエージェントを定義する方法が紹介されています。
Q
会話の途中でモデルを切り替えても大丈夫ですか？
▼
A
使えますが、切り替えた最初のターンは会話全体のキャッシュを書き直す料金がかかり、下位のモデルに移るとそれまでの思考の内容は引き継がれません。区切りのよいところで、Claude Codeなら /compact してから切り替えるのが勧められています。
Q
無料プランでもこのガイドは役に立ちますか？
▼
A
無料プランで使えるのはSonnetとHaikuなので、OpusやFableの話は当てはまりません。ただ、effortを上げる前に確認の手段を与える、という考え方は無料プランでも使えます。
