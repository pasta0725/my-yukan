Rocket launches this week
On Monday, Nov. 9
, SpaceX is targeting 10:03 p.m. EST to launch 
USSF-627
 on a Falcon 9 from Space Launch Complex 4 East at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. The mission is a classified payload for the U.S. Space Force. The booster will aim for the droneship 
Of Course I Still Love You
 in the Pacific Ocean.
On Wednesday, Nov. 11
, the state-owned China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation (CASC) is targeting 6:40 a.m. EST for a Long March 2D launch from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwestern China, according to 
Next Spaceflight
. The payload has not been disclosed.
On Thursday, Nov. 12
, ExPace, a subsidiary of the state-owned China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation (CASIC), is targeting 2:50 p.m. EST for a Kuaizhou 11 launch from Jiuquan, according to 
Next Spaceflight
. Kuaizhou 11 is a solid-fueled rocket designed for fast-turnaround launches of small satellites. Its payload is also undisclosed.
Later Thursday, Nov. 12
, SpaceX returns to Vandenberg at 11:23 p.m. EST for 
USSF-317
, another classified Space Force mission on a Falcon 9. The booster is again slated to land on 
Of Course I Still Love You
. 
On Friday, Nov. 13
, United Launch Alliance (ULA) is targeting 12:54 p.m. EST for 
Vulcan Leo 1
 from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. The launch ends a nine-month grounding that followed a nozzle failure during February’s USSF-87 mission; ULA says the boosters now carry a redesigned nozzle. It’s also Vulcan’s first flight for Amazon, carrying 40 satellites for Amazon Leo, the broadband megaconstellation formerly known as Project Kuiper.
Later Friday, Nov. 13
, SpaceX opens a 75-minute window at 5:45 p.m. EST for 
Starship Flight 15
 from Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas. It’s the 15th test flight of the company’s reusable megarocket and only its second trip to orbit, after Flight 14’s success in September. About six and a half minutes after liftoff, the Super Heavy booster should return to the launch tower and be caught by its chopstick arms. SpaceX has made that catch before with the rocket’s V1 and V2 boosters, but this will be a first for the new V3 booster. The upper stage, called Ship, will continue to orbit and release 26 of SpaceX’s next-generation Starlink V3 satellites as it did in September.
You can 
watch the launch live
 on SpaceX’s website.
The next day, SpaceX will try to catch Ship, too. Until now, Ship’s test flights have ended with a practice landing at sea where the vehicle flips upright, fires its engines, and sets down on the water. This time, after about 14 orbits at roughly 171 miles (275 km) up — a flight of more than 22 hours — Ship must run that same landing maneuver over Starbase, settling into the tower’s arms instead. 
SpaceX says Ship will head for the tower only if flight controllers confirm that every system it needs for the catch is prepared. If not, the company can bring Ship down at preplanned spots in the Pacific, with checks built into the flight for splashdowns near Hawaii or off the coast of Chile. If the catch works, Starship will become the first orbital rocket ever recovered in full. 
On Saturday, Nov. 14
, Rocket Lab is targeting 12:30 a.m. EST for 
The Mountain God Rises
, the 100th launch of its Electron rocket, from Launch Complex 1 on New Zealand’s Māhia Peninsula. Electron will deploy a QPS-SAR satellite for the Japanese company iQPS in Rocket Lab’s 11th dedicated launch for the operator. The iQPS constellation uses synthetic aperture radar (SAR), bouncing microwaves off the ground to map Earth’s surface even through rain, smoke, and total darkness.
Last week’s recap
On Monday, Nov. 2, Rocket Lab launched 
The Sea God Surveys
 from New Zealand at 11:41 a.m. EST, delivering another QPS-SAR radar satellite for iQPS. It was Electron’s 99th flight and Rocket Lab’s 10th mission for the Japanese operator. That evening at 7:15 p.m. EST, CAS Space’s 
Kinetica 2 lifted off
 from Jiuquan and placed 18 satellites for China’s SpaceSail broadband constellation in polar orbit. It was only the rocket’s second flight, following its debut in March.
On Thursday, Nov. 5, a Long March 2C 
launched
 from the Xichang Satellite Launch Center in southwestern China with three Yaogan 51 satellites. Yaogan is the label China gives its government Earth-observation spacecraft, which are widely believed to perform military reconnaissance.
Friday, Nov. 6, brought two launches about an hour apart. Galactic Energy’s 
Ceres 2
 put four satellites in orbit from Jiuquan at 6:37 p.m. EST. Then, after several days of delays, SpaceX launched the classified 
USSF-432
 mission from Vandenberg at 7:48 p.m. EST, landing the booster on 
Of Course I Still Love You
.
On Saturday, Nov. 7, Russia’s Aerospace Forces 
launched a Soyuz-2.1b
 from the Plesetsk Cosmodrome in northern Russia. The payload is believed to be a GLONASS satellite for Russia’s version of GPS. The week closed Sunday, Nov. 8, when a 
Long March 12 lifted off
 from the Wenchang commercial launch site on Hainan Island at 3:24 p.m. EST, carrying nine more satellites for Guowang, China’s state-run internet megaconstellation.
Looking ahead
On Friday, Nov. 20, CASC is targeting 6:00 a.m. EST for a Long March 8A launch from Wenchang, likely carrying another batch of Guowang satellites, according to 
Next Spaceflight
.
On Tuesday, Nov. 24, at 2:00 a.m. EST, a Long March 12 is slated to fly another Guowang group from Wenchang, according to 
Next Spaceflight
.

SpaceX will make spaceflight history this week, if all goes according to plan.
The company announced over the weekend that it's targeting Friday (Nov. 13) for the 15th test flight of its 
Starship
 megarocket, which will attempt something that's never been done before.
"The upcoming flight is planned to be the first to return both Starship and Super Heavy to the launch site for catch," 
SpaceX
 wrote in a 
description of Flight 15
. "If successful, this will mark the first full recovery of an orbital launch system and a breakthrough moment in making Starship a reliable, rapidly reusable rocket."
SpaceX's Starship megarocket launches on its 14th test flight on Sept. 28, 2026. 
(Image credit: SpaceX)
Super Heavy is Starship's giant booster, and Starship (or Ship for short) is its upper-stage spacecraft. As SpaceX's description noted, both elements are designed to be fully and rapidly reusable.
"Catch" refers to SpaceX's recovery mechanism for both stages: Each will return to the launch mount at SpaceX's 
Starbase site in South Texas
 and be plucked out of the sky by the tower's "chopstick" arms.
SpaceX has caught Super Heavy
 on three Starship flights to date, but it has never tried the maneuver with Ship. It's a taller order; Ship flies higher and farther than Super Heavy, so its return to Earth is more complex and energetic.
SpaceX founder and CEO 
Elon Musk
 already told us that Flight 15 would likely feature a 
double-catch attempt
. But the newly released mission description gives us a launch date, as well as details about the flight.

Flight 15 is scheduled to launch from Starbase on Friday during a 75-minute window that opens at 5:45 p.m. EST (2245 GMT; 4:45 p.m. local Texas time).
Super Heavy will come back to Starbase for its tower catch about 6.5 minutes after liftoff, if all goes to plan. But Ship will stay up quite a bit longer.
The upper stage will go orbital, something Starship did for the first time 
on Flight 14
 on Sept. 28. And, as on Flight 14, Ship will deploy 26 of SpaceX's next-gen 
Starlink
 V3 satellites, the vanguard of a new megaconstellation that will eventually consist of about 
100,000 spacecraft
, according to Musk.
Composite photo showing the Super Heavy booster of SpaceX's Starship megarocket coming in for a landing on the launch mount during the vehicle's fifth-ever test flight, on Oct. 13, 2024. 
(Image credit: SpaceX via X)
But Ship should stay aloft for much longer on Flight 15 than it did on Flight 14, which ended with a splashdown in the northern Pacific Ocean about three hours after liftoff.
"Starship’s initial return to launch site mission is expected to orbit the Earth at an altitude of ~275 km [171 miles] and complete approximately 14 orbits around the planet over the course of a more than 22-hour flight," SpaceX wrote in its description of Flight 15.
"Similar to Starship’s first orbital mission, the flight profile has been designed with the opportunity to insert into a passively safe suborbital trajectory or safely deorbit at multiple pre-determined areas over the ocean after raising to an orbital altitude," the company added.
So there's no guarantee that Ship will indeed come back to Starbase for a catch on Flight 15; SpaceX will call off the try if anything is amiss. Whatever happens, however, you should definitely tune in. It's not every day, after all, that you can watch a rocket the size of a 40-story building take flight.
