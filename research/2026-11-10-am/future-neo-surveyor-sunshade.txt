Sunshade for NASA’s NEO Surveyor Mission Ready for Its Day in Sun
The large sunshade of NASA’s Near-Earth Object Surveyor mission is inspected by a technician at BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems in Boulder, Colorado, in late July.
BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems
Development of NASA’s Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor mission continues as the spacecraft’s largest component gets closer to how it will look when it launches. Recently, teams completed work on integrating solar arrays onto the spacecraft’s sunshade, a structure primarily designed to block the Sun’s glare. The sunshade will keep the infrared space telescope cool as it searches for near-Earth asteroids and comets while also providing the spacecraft with power.
The 
sunshade structure
 stands 20 feet (6 meters) tall and measures 8 feet (2.4 meters) wide at its base. Its two flat panels, joined at a 23.5-degree angle, were covered with 864 interconnected solar cells earlier this year. This specialized solar array can produce up to 2 kilowatts of power and will generate all the required electricity to charge the onboard batteries that power the spacecraft’s systems.
When in space, 
NEO Surveyor
 will detect some of the hardest-to-find asteroids and comets, some of which may be located in the direction of the Sun — a region of sky where ground-based optical telescopes have a harder time searching. The sunshade’s design is key for blocking the glare of sunlight from entering the telescope’s 20-inch-wide (50-centimeter-wide) aperture as it searches for the infrared emissions coming from the surface of asteroids and comets that are heated by sunlight. By determining their location and motion through space, the mission’s data will help NASA better understand the number and nature of potentially hazardous near-Earth objects.
The telescope’s 
pair of infrared detector arrays
 is tuned to generate detailed images of asteroids and comets within two infrared bands. Each array creates a 16-megapixel mosaic of the sky. Imaging the same part of the sky over the two infrared bands enables the NEO Surveyor science team to determine an asteroid or comet’s temperature, yielding an estimate of the object’s size — key information for assessing near-Earth objects.
Mounted on an assembly ring during inspections in a clean room, the 20-foot-tall (6-meter-tall) sunshade for NEO Surveyor underwent inspections in July after the integration of 864 solar cells that will be the only source of power for the spacecraft’s systems.
BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems
Built by Applied Aerospace in Stockton, California, the sunshade structure was outfitted with its solar cells by SolAero Technologies in Albuquerque, New Mexico, in January. Environmental testing of the sunshade and solar array was completed at a commercial thermal vacuum testing facility in El Segundo, California, in March and it was delivered to BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems in Boulder, Colorado, ahead of schedule in May. Since then, coarse Sun sensors for attitude determination and temperature sensors have been integrated onto the sunshade, and the team completed the functional and performance testing of the solar array.

The sunshade started its journey at Applied Aerospace in Stockton, California, where it was built. It was later moved to SolAero Technologies in Albuquerque, New Mexico, where technicians installed the solar cells onto it in January, giving the array a peak output of 2 kilowatts, which will be enough to charge the batteries onboard the spacecraft. In March, the sunshade passed environmental testing at a facility in El Segundo, California, after which it traveled to BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems in Boulder, Colorado, in May. There, engineers integrated Sun sensors, which will help determine the spacecraft’s orientation, and temperature sensors onto the sunshade, while also putting the solar array through functional and performance testing.
Engineers attach the aluminum telescope for NASA’s NEO Surveyor to the flight base frame at Space Dynamics Laboratory in Logan, Utah, in September 2025 (Image Source: Space Dynamics Laboratory/Allison Bills)
After launch, the spacecraft will travel to the Sun-Earth Lagrange point 1, or L1, located at around 1 million miles (1.5 million kilometers) from Earth. From there, it will survey vast portions of the sky continuously for a minimum of five years, looking for 
near-Earth objects
 (NEOs) not yet discovered. NEO Surveyor will view the same region of sky in two infrared wavelength bands, with each of the telescope's pair of detector arrays generating a 16-megapixel mosaic of the sky. A comparison of the data obtained from these two bands will allow scientists to determine an object's temperature, and, from that, its size. Furthermore, these findings can be paired with the target's location and motion readings, and researchers can then assess which near-Earth objects could pose a threat to Earth.
The bus structure of NASA’s NEO Surveyor, shown here, underwent a round of testing at BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems in Boulder, Colorado, in August 2025 (Image Source: BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems)
The concept of NEO Surveyor originated when Congress directed NASA in 2005 to find potentially hazardous NEOs. It has been seen that observations conducted from the Earth are incapable of spotting these objects, as they can may not be bright enough, may be small-sized, or maybe shielded by sunlight. NEO Surveyor is specifically designed to overcome this by detecting the infrared glow of objects warmed by the Sun, rather than the reflected visible light that ground-based observations depend on. The goal is to give humanity enough advance warning to respond if needed.

(SpaceEnergy) NASA has confirmed completion of functional and performance testing of the solar array integrated into its NEO Surveyor spacecraft's sunshade, advancing the mission's 2kW electrical power system towards launch.
The agency announced on 26 October that engineers had completed testing of the power-generating array following its integration with the spacecraft's sunshade at BAE Systems Space & Mission Systems in Boulder, Colorado.
The 6m-tall sunshade incorporates 864 interconnected solar cells across two flat panels positioned at a 23.5-degree angle.
The array is designed to generate up to 2kW of electricity, providing the spacecraft's sole source of electrical power and charging its onboard batteries.
Unlike conventional spacecraft solar arrays, the panels form part of a structure that also shields the mission's infrared telescope from direct sunlight.
NASA said the sunshade was manufactured by Applied Aerospace in Stockton, California, before SolAero Technologies installed the solar cells in Albuquerque, New Mexico, in January 2026.
Environmental testing of the combined structure was completed at a thermal-vacuum facility in El Segundo, California, in March.
The assembly was delivered to BAE Systems in May, where engineers subsequently integrated Sun sensors and temperature sensors and completed the solar array's functional and performance tests.
NASA did not disclose the precise date of the latest tests or measured electrical output.
NEO Surveyor will operate near the Sun-Earth L1 Lagrange point, approximately 1.5 million kilometres from Earth, searching for potentially hazardous asteroids and comets.

October 27, 2026
 | NASA’s next dedicated asteroid-hunting space telescope has reached another construction milestone. Engineers have integrated solar cells into the sunshade of NEO Surveyor and completed a series of ground checks, NASA reported October 26.
The 20-foot-tall structure is designed to do two jobs: shield the infrared telescope from sunlight and generate power for the spacecraft. Its base measures about 8 feet across. NASA says the two panels meet at a 23.5-degree angle and carry 864 interconnected solar cells, capable of producing up to 2 kilowatts of power.
Why the sunshade matters
NEO Surveyor will look for asteroids and comets by detecting heat they emit after sunlight warms them. That makes infrared observations useful for finding dark objects that reflect little visible light. Its 20-inch-wide infrared telescope is designed to survey regions that are difficult for ground-based observatories, including areas of sky closer to the Sun’s glare.
Keeping direct sunlight away from the optics helps preserve the temperature conditions infrared observations require. The sunshade is therefore not merely a cover: it supports both the telescope’s cooling and the spacecraft’s electricity supply.
What has actually been completed
NASA reports that the solar cells were installed earlier this year. The assembly underwent thermal-vacuum environmental testing in March, was delivered to BAE Systems in Boulder, Colorado, in May, and was inspected there in July. The October 26 update reports further sensor integration and functional testing.
These are 
ground engineering milestones, not observations of asteroids and not proof that the whole spacecraft is ready to fly
. Additional integration and mission-level checks remain. NASA’s current mission page lists launch 
no earlier than September 2027
, a target rather than a confirmed liftoff time.
What scientists hope to learn
The mission will use two infrared bands to estimate the temperatures and sizes of near-Earth objects. Better measurements of asteroid location, motion and size can improve planetary-defense assessments. The project does not imply an asteroid impact is imminent or that a new threat has just been discovered.
