Goldman Sachs Group Inc. has irked some of its staff after it banned most of them from using advanced artificial intelligence tools designed for coding after reviewing productivity and risk concerns.
For months, Goldman allowed bankers to use Anthropic PBC’s Claude Code and Microsoft Corp.’s GitHub for work. Now, the bank is limiting the use of those tools to engineers only, according to internal guidance updated in October and seen by Bloomberg News.
The ban has frustrated some traders, who had used the tools to accelerate aspects of their work such as monitoring client trading flows, modeling market changes and positions, and creating written and graphic content, according to a person familiar with the matter, who asked not to be identified talking about private information.
As recently as July, Goldman Chief Information Officer Marco Argenti touted the “vibe coding” by non-technical employees at Goldman, such as traders and bankers. Those staff members had developed solutions with AI tools and passed on their creations to one of the firm’s 12,000 engineers to improve, he said in an interview.
“We make certain AI tools available to our people to determine the best use cases across different parts of the firm,” said Nick Carcaterra, a Goldman spokesperson. “After a period of experimentation where we assess a variety of factors including productivity and risk management, we put them in the hands of those who are best suited to leverage them for the long term.”
It’s the latest twist as Wall Street irons out just exactly how it wants its thousands of workers using AI models. Banks are spending millions of dollars on AI tools while also trying to figure out how to navigate the increasing concerns around cybersecurity risk, costs and productivity.
JPMorgan Chase & Co. said in July that it was seeking to have employees figure out what model to use for what purpose, helping to avoid using the most expensive model for a task as simple as summarizing an analyst report. Goldman itself has developed an AI assistant that combines models from OpenAI, Anthropic and Alphabet Inc.
At the same time, Wall Street firms are benefiting from the surging financing needs of AI firms. Goldman has been named lead underwriter on Anthropic’s blockbuster initial public offering and has partnered with chipmaker Nvidia Corp. to arrange major debt deals.
In May, John Waldron, the heir apparent to Chief Executive Officer David Solomon, said he viewed Goldman as a “human assembly line” that was ripe for automation.
It’s not the first time that the bank has pulled back on allowing employees to use certain AI tools. Earlier this year, the bank restricted the use of Anthropic’s Claude by its staff in Hong Kong, a location-specific restriction.
The bank’s latest directive doesn’t apply to so-called “strats,” employees who sit at the intersection of trading, quantitative research and software engineering, said another person.

Why is Goldman Sachs pulling the plug on AI coding tools?
A front view of the Goldman Sachs tower | Image Credit: Akshay Sadarangani, Unsplash
  Ankita Sharma 
Tue, November 3, 2026 at 1:27 PM CST
3 min read
GS 
MSFT 
Goldman Sachs is narrowing who can use some of its AI coding tools. After letting bankers and traders experiment with Anthropic's Claude Code and Microsoft's GitHub tools for several months, the bank has limited access to the tools to engineers, according to internal guidance updated in October and reviewed by Bloomberg.
This change reverses a period of experimentation beyond software teams. But it is not a shutdown of AI at Goldman Sachs. The bank says it tested the tools before deciding which workers should keep them, and it continues to use AI elsewhere in its business.
Goldman Sachs is ending an experiment that spread beyond its engineering teams
This restriction covers most bankers and traders who previously had access to Claude Code and GitHub. 
Bloomberg reported
 that revised guidance confines the tools to engineers. The exception is employees known as strats, whose jobs combine trading, quantitative research, and software engineering. They are exempt from the restriction.
The tools were not being used only to write conventional software. Traders had put them to work tracking client trading activity, modeling market changes and positions, and producing written and visual material. Some traders are reportedly unhappy about losing access after incorporating the assistants into their routines.
The policy follows a period when 
Goldman Sachs highlighted experimentation
 outside technology departments. Chief Information Officer Marco Argenti described traders and bankers using AI to turn ideas into working prototypes. Employees could then hand early versions to one of the firm's roughly 12,000 engineers for further development.
Productivity and risk management are behind the decision
Goldman Sachs has offered a broad explanation rather than identifying one incident that prompted the change. Spokesperson Nick Carcaterra said the firm makes selected AI tools available so employees can test possible uses across the business. After assessing productivity and risk management, the bank decides which groups are best placed to use them over the longer term.
This explanation does not establish that Goldman Sachs discovered a security breach, a compliance violation, or a specific failure caused by the coding assistants. The bank has not publicly tied the restriction to any such event. It is also unclear whether the review found that the tools delivered insufficient productivity gains or whether the decision mainly reflected the need for tighter controls.
A prototype may be useful to its creator, but software used in trading or banking workflows can require stricter standards for accuracy, security, and accountability.
Story Continues
Chris Churchman, head of Goldman Sachs's Marquee platform, discussed these challenges in an August interview on the bank's podcast. He emphasized secure environments, traceable AI outputs, and the need for people to support human reasoning rather than replace critical thinking.
The bank wants controlled AI adoption, not an end to AI development
Goldman Sachs's wider AI strategy remains active. Its 2024 annual report said many employees had access to tools, including a developer coding assistant and a natural-language GS AI assistant. The bank has also built an internal assistant drawing on models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, Bloomberg reported.
The latest restrictions do not amount to a ban on those broader AI services. The engineering focus fits other work at the firm. In August, 
Business Insider reported
 that Goldman was adapting AI coding agents to its engineering standards, data practices, and institutional knowledge.
Argenti said developers were working to give these systems the context they need to produce useful work inside the bank. Goldman Sachs is narrowing who can use coding agents directly, while reserving these tools for engineering teams.
Its experiment showed that employees in other roles could create prototypes. The latest policy suggests that turning these experiments into dependable tools requires a different level of oversight.
