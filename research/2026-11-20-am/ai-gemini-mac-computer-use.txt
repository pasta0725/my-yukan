Google
 has given its Gemini app for macOS the ability to actually use your computer. The new feature, called computer use, lets the AI assistant carry out multi-step tasks that span local files, native desktop apps and the web.
According to Google, the feature can help users 
get multi-step tasks done
 without hopping between windows themselves. The company frames it as a way to take the tedious, repetitive parts of computer work off your plate.
Gemini can help you “get multi-step tasks done across your local files, desktop apps, and the web,” Google said.
How Gemini’s computer use works
Under the hood, the assistant performs the same actions a person would. It clicks, types, navigates menus and fills in forms. Crucially, it does this in the background rather than hijacking your screen.
Gemini runs its work in its own background window, and that window streams a live preview into your chat. So you can keep writing an email or editing a spreadsheet while the AI grinds through a chore in a separate lane.
Google suggested a handful of use cases. These include speeding up workflows, filling in forms, building presentations and listing items for sale online.
Google also pointed to tasks like organizing files and batch processing forms.

The feature launched on November 19, 2026. It is rolling out now in English for users in the US.
There is a catch on access. Using computer use requires a paid subscription to Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra, so free-tier users will be watching from the sidelines for now.

Control is the selling point
Users decide which apps Gemini is allowed to touch. Permissions are granted app by app, so you can let the assistant into your browser and presentation software while keeping it out of, say, your banking app.
The live preview doubles as a safety feature. Because you can follow along in real time, you are not left guessing what the AI did after the fact.
Users can also pause or stop an action midway. If Gemini starts heading somewhere you did not intend, you can pull the plug before it finishes.
That combination of scoped permissions, visibility and a kill switch is the core of Google’s pitch. The company is emphasizing security and user control as much as raw capability.
Late to a crowded party
Gemini did not start out with this power on the Mac. Google first released the Gemini macOS app in April 2026, and it already shipped with some basic automation features under the Gemini Tasks banner.
Google layered on more in June 2026 with its 
Spark
 and Tasks features. Computer use is the next step, moving the assistant from helping inside a chat window to acting across the whole desktop.
Google is not first here. 
OpenAI
 and 
Anthropic
 both introduced similar desktop automation tools before Google, which makes this a catch-up move rather than a land grab.
What this means for users and the AI race
The paywall is also worth watching. By tying computer use to Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra, Google is using one of its most practical agent features as a reason to upgrade.
The open questions are practical ones. How well does Gemini handle messy, real-world apps that were never designed with AI agents in mind? How quickly will Google expand access beyond English-speaking US users?
For now, the rollout is limited and paywalled. But the direction is unmistakable: Google wants Gemini to be the assistant that does the work, not just the one that explains it.

 for Mac, allowing the assistant to carry out multi-step work across local files, desktop applications and websites. After a user describes the desired workflow, Gemini moves through menus, clicks and types within apps, and fills in forms rather than merely supplying directions.
Gemini performs the work in an isolated background window, with a live preview in the chat. The user’s main screen and cursor remain free for other activity. Users choose which apps it can access, can watch and pause its progress, and approve key actions before they happen.
Example Workflows Span Local and Online Data
Google’s suggested uses emphasize transferring information between programs. For a shipping quote, Gemini can extract weights, dimensions and addresses from local PDFs and enter them into a multi-box form. It can also gather metrics and screenshots from live dashboards across multiple regions and turn them into a Google Slides presentation.
Creative examples include batch color-grading photos in design software and setting up complex 3D scenes. A resale workflow can analyze clothing photos from a local folder, research pricing, record item details and stage listings on resale platforms, combining local images, web research and listing preparation in one task.
Access Depends on Region, Subscription and Hardware
The feature is rolling out in English globally, except in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, Switzerland and Nigeria. It is limited to personal Google accounts for users aged 18 or older with a Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra subscription.
The Mac must have Apple Silicon and run macOS Sequoia 15.0 or later. Google’s general 
installation requirements
 also call for at least 8 GB of RAM, 200 MB of available disk space for installation and a stable internet connection.
File Permissions Come With a Limited Recovery Window
Local access is narrower than an unrestricted view of the Mac. Google’s 
task documentation
 says Gemini can view only files or directories specifically added to the Connected folders toolbar. Users select Task below the text box, optionally add a Mac folder, and can disconnect an item added by mistake.
With permission, Gemini can view and edit most folders, including Downloads, Desktop, Documents and Photos. It makes requested changes to files in connected folders by default. Folder selection therefore determines which local material enters a task, while the separate app permissions govern the programs involved in the workflow.
When Gemini works with computer files, it creates temporary backups. Users can recover those backups before moving to another task, but they are deleted when a new query or task starts or after 24 hours. That makes the recovery window dependent on what the user does next as well as the clock.
Google cautions that Gemini can make mistakes, permanently delete Workspace or computer files, and send or share files with third parties. It is designed to request permission before those actions.
The recommended precautions include checking file contents and recipients before approving sharing, avoiding tasks involving information or actions the user considers sensitive, and keeping sign-in and payment details out of the task thread.
Public cloud drives add another exposure. Once connected, their files can be accessed, edited, shared and deleted, and Gemini can also share data from other connected folders and apps, signed-in websites and the user’s location with the drive. Anyone with access to that public cloud drive can see data shared there.
The practical trade-off sits in those permissions: the access that lets Gemini finish work across local and online services also determines what it could alter, delete or disclose if it makes a mistake.

Google has introduced computer use in the Mac version of Gemini. Its assistant can now carry out jobs inside desktop programs and websites rather than simply explain the steps. The rollout announced on November 19 starts in English in the United States for paying subscribers.
The feature works in a separate background window. A person can keep using their normal screen and mouse pointer while Gemini moves through a task, with a live view of its progress shown inside the chat.
For Google, this brings Gemini further into the everyday work done on a computer. The announcement covers local documents and installed applications as well as information on the web, putting the Mac itself within reach of the assistant.
Paid plans and Apple Silicon required
Google lists its AI Plus, Pro and Ultra subscriptions as eligible. Access is restricted to personal Google accounts belonging to adults, and the computer must use Apple Silicon with macOS Sequoia 15.0 or a later version.
The company's launch post names the US as the initial market. TechCrunch also reported a US English rollout and said Google had not given a timetable for other countries or platforms. That means this announcement does not establish an Indian launch date.
This is a feature inside the Mac application, not a promise that every Gemini account can now control any computer. The subscription, account, hardware and regional conditions all apply.
Tasks stretch from PDFs to presentations
Google illustrated the launch with jobs that involve moving material between programs. One example takes package measurements and delivery details from PDF documents and enters them into shipping quotation forms.
Another gathers figures and screenshots from online dashboards for a Google Slides presentation. The company also suggested editing groups of photographs, preparing 3D scenes and assembling resale listings from clothing pictures stored on the computer.
These are Google's examples of intended uses, not independent performance tests. They show the sort of work being targeted: a series of small actions spread across files, forms and applications, with a single request connecting them.
App permissions do not remove the risks
Users choose the applications Gemini may access. Google says they can watch its work, pause it and approve important actions. Its Mac help documentation separately says local file access is limited to the folders or files added by the user.
That documentation also sets out limits to recovery. Temporary copies made while Gemini changes computer files disappear after 24 hours or when another query or task begins, whichever happens first.
Google warns that mistakes can include permanent deletion or sending files to other people. According to Google, the system should seek approval before it deletes or shares material. The ability to inspect and interrupt a task therefore remains part of the product, alongside the automation itself.
The bottom line
Gemini's latest Mac addition moves beyond answers towards doing the clicking and typing behind a request. The launch combines background operation with user-selected access and approval controls, but its initial US availability and paid-plan requirements leave a clear boundary around who can try it now.
