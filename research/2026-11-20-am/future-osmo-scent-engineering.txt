Startup Osmo claims AI smell recognition at human level, trained on 10M labels
18.11.2026
·
5
min read
17126
Искусственный интеллект · illustration by the publication
US startup Osmo claims its AI smell model has reached human-level performance and is already helping fragrance brands create new scents.
In recent months, at a laboratory near Newark Airport in New Jersey, about 30 trained experts have been describing smells with words like “nutty,” “peachy,” or “marine”; these notes, combined with chemical analysis, become training data for Osmo’s artificial intelligence model. The startup calls it the first foundation model for smell, and founder Alex Wiltschko’s goal is to “give computers a sense of smell.”
“Scent molecules are really the only language most species on this planet speak,” Wiltschko says. “Most species can’t communicate with sound or sight, so it truly is Earth’s original language. You can remember a smell for life: you catch a scent on the street and you’re back in high school.”
According to Wiltschko, the model has reached “human-level olfactory intelligence”: it detects and distinguishes smells about as well as an average person and creates fragrances at the level of a junior perfumer. The technology is already used in the fragrance industry; Osmo claims that last month its model outperformed in-house perfumers at one of the world’s largest consumer goods companies.
How Osmo collects smells
Wiltschko started Osmo in 2023, spinning it out of his work at Google Brain, Google’s former AI research lab. He is using the same playbook as other AI startups: throw a lot of data at a problem and see what happens. The issue was a shortage of good data: before Osmo, existing databases had fewer than 200,000 human descriptions of smells, and the chemical sensors needed are expensive. The fragrance industry is also reluctant to share its work.
The company built its own dataset of more than 10 million human labels and 800 million “scent tokens” that describe the molecules and concentrations behind a particular odor. Spectrometers and other chemical sensors run around the clock, breaking down the molecular makeup of fruit, flowers, pantry staples, and organs. About 70 trained labelers in the US and abroad spend their days sniffing samples and writing down impressions using a standardized fragrance vocabulary.
The approach reveals unexpected parallels: changing word order can change a sentence’s meaning, and changing the proportions of scent molecules can drastically change the smell a person perceives. According to Wiltschko, the scent tokens of a newborn baby have a huge overlap with those of a fresh plate of cooked Barilla spaghetti.
What has already been done using the AI
The company also makes its own scents using a device the size of a school bus that mixes ingredients according to exact formulas and prints samples. The system accepts text prompts in the style of ChatGPT: in a demo, the model generated three fragrance options for a Portuguese custard tart aimed at men aged 18–23. Had the order been placed, a signature perfume would have been ready about a week later.
Fragrance: retail scents, including products for Procter & Gamble.
Insect control: the Gates Foundation invested $5 million at Osmo’s launch and gave $3.5 million to evaluate molecules that repel disease-carrying insects. Osmo found eight substances that work better than DEET.
Counterfeiting: Osmo worked with online marketplace StockX to spot fake sneakers by chemical composition of materials.

Criticism and limitations
Not everyone accepts Osmo’s claims. Avery Gilbert, a smell researcher and author of What the Nose Knows, has criticized the company on his Substack blog, saying some marketing claims are not documented and its patent filings still rely on human sensory panels and perfumers rather than AI. Osmo representatives did not respond by the time of publication.
Michael Schmuker, a former professor of neural computation at the University of Hertfordshire and a former Osmo consultant, calls the technology an achievement. With colleagues, he built an open-source, reverse-engineered version of Osmo’s 2023 model, but it lacks the volume of data the company has collected since.
Schmuker warns that even with a large amount of data, the model may miss context that matters for human olfaction. The same molecule can evoke different associations depending on the situation: the smell of Camembert partly overlaps with the smell of used nappies, but people don’t confuse the two because of context.
In the controlled environment of a fragrance lab, the lack of context may not matter, but it could become a problem if Osmo wants to use the technology to diagnose diseases by smell, as trained dogs have been taught to do in pilot studies, or to detect spoiled food on a production line. According to Schmuker, this would require much better sensors and more advanced models. Medical diagnostics is the biggest prize.
Our view
We think it matters that Osmo is not just publishing papers but trying to commercialize smell: work with Procter & Gamble, the StockX project, and funding from the Gates Foundation are concrete signs that the market needs this technology. The main risk is not data but bold promises: if the model fails to handle real-world context, trust in the whole field could suffer.
We will watch whether Osmo can move from fragrance tasks to medical diagnostics, the hardest and potentially most valuable goal. For now, the company is building a “foundation at the foot of the mountains,” as Wiltschko puts it, rather than storming the peak.

Osmo Claims Human-Level Artificial Sense of Smell
Key Takeaways
1
Osmo compares its model to an average person for detecting and distinguishing odors and to a novice perfumer for creating them.
2
The company has amassed over 10 million human annotations and 800 million olfactory tokens.

Avery Gilbert challenges some commercial claims; Michael Schmuker emphasizes the role of context in human smell.
Why it matters
 : 
The technology is already being used to develop perfumes, but its application for medical diagnosis is still being considered.
Osmo claims to have reached human-level performance with its olfactory AI model, which, according to the company, can detect and distinguish odors like an average person and compose scents like a novice perfumer. Its technology has already been used in commercial perfumes, including for Procter & Gamble. Some of the company's claims are contested, while medical applications remain under consideration.
Scientists Discuss the Claimed Capabilities
Olfactory specialist Avery Gilbert, author of 
What the Nose Knows
, contests some of Osmo's commercial promises on Substack, deeming them insufficiently documented. He also argues that patent applications continue to rely on human evaluators and perfumers rather than AI. In March, he criticized this dependence on human judgment in light of the AI capabilities being touted. Osmo had not responded to these criticisms by the time of publication.
Michael Schmuker, a former professor of neural computation at the University of Hertfordshire and a former consultant for Osmo, praises it as a technical achievement. To explain this limitation, Michael Schmuker mentions a molecule found in both Camembert cheese and used diapers: context allows humans to differentiate these odors. He notes that distinct associations can arise from the same molecule. Last month, Osmo claimed that its model had outperformed in-house perfumers at one of the world's largest consumer goods companies.
Custom Perfumes to Repellents
According to Osmo, brands are already using its technology to develop and manufacture their scents. The company has a robot as large as a school bus that assembles ingredients according to precise formulations. It produces samples for internal research as well as perfumes for paying clients. The design can start with a written instruction, akin to a request made to 
ChatGPT
.
At the Web Summit in Lisbon, Alex Wiltschko demonstrated on his computer a perfume request reminiscent of a Portuguese egg tart, aimed at men aged 18 to 23. After a few minutes, the model provided three proposals with creamy, coconut, and fruity profiles, along with the molecules to be used. If the order had been confirmed, the scent would have been available about a week later.

Other projects extend beyond perfumery. The Gates Foundation, which invested $5 million at launch, later awarded Osmo $3.5 million to test repellent molecules against disease-carrying insects. The company identified eight substances that are more repellent than DEET. It has also collaborated with StockX, an online clothing marketplace, to detect counterfeit sneakers based on the chemical differences between their materials.
Human Evaluators Feed the Model
Founded in 2023 by Alex Wiltschko following his research at Google Brain, Google's former AI lab, Osmo relies on building large datasets. According to its founder, previous databases contained fewer than 200,000 human-written odor descriptions. This scarcity is compounded by the high cost of chemical sensors and the confidentiality of research in the fragrance industry. Osmo presents the results of its work as the world's first foundational model dedicated to olfaction.
Near Newark Airport in New Jersey, about 30 experienced evaluators describe odors; their assessments are compared with chemical analyses to train the system. More broadly, around 70 trained annotators, based in the U.S. and elsewhere, use a common lexicon of perfumery. Osmo has gathered over 10 million human annotations and 800 million olfactory tokens, which specify the present molecules and their concentrations.
Spectrometers and other sensors operate continuously to analyze fruits, flowers, raw materials, or organs. A variation in molecular proportions can significantly alter human perception of an odor. Alex Wiltschko states that the tokens associated with a newborn largely overlap with those of fresh Barilla spaghetti.
Medical Uses Would Require Better Sensors
Alex Wiltschko aims to give computers olfactory capabilities and envisions providing this sensory input to global AI models. He describes odor molecules as a mode of communication accessible to most species, unlike sound or vision. He also emphasizes their connections to emotions and memory, with a single exposure potentially leaving a lasting impression.
The founder envisions applications such as threat detection in airports and disease diagnosis. Possible uses include identifying spoiled food on a production line or detecting tumors by smell. Pilot studies have already trained dogs for this latter task.
Michael Schmuker believes that such applications would require significant advancements in sensors; the models would also need to continue improving. Alex Wiltschko reminds that companies have failed by directly targeting very challenging and costly applications. He describes Osmo's approach as building a technical foundation while exploring multiple avenues.

AIの鼻は人間の嗅覚に匹敵するか？30名の嗅覚専門家がモデルを訓練中
2026年11月18日
                            154                        
                            約6分
                            MIT Technology Review                        
嗅觉AI
气味基础模型
Osmo
感官计算
气味数字化
ニュージャージー州ニューアーク空港近くの目立たない実験室で、「気味」をめぐる長期実験がゆっくりと進んでいる。厳しく訓練された約30名の人間の嗅覚鑑定士が、毎日同じことを繰り返している——においを嗅ぎ、描写し、記録する。彼らのメモは一見無造作だ——「ナッツの香り」「桃の香り」「海の香り」——しかしそれぞれの記録は対応する化学分析データと照合され、最終的に巨大なデータベースに集約される。このデータベースこそが、AIスタートアップOsmoが「世界初の嗅覚基盤モデル」と呼ぶものの燃料となっている。
なぜ嗅覚はデジタル化が最も難しい感覚なのか
コンピュータービジョンと音声認識がすでに成熟した今日においても、嗅覚はAIの「盲点」であり続けている。理由は難しくない。画像や音声はピクセルや波形として正確に分解できるのに対し、においと分子構造の関係には未だ完全な理論が存在しない。化学者はある分子がどの原子で構成されているかを説明できても、それがどんなにおいなのかを正確に予測するのは難しい。だからこそ、「においを嗅ぐ」AIを訓練するには、まず大量の「人間による嗅覚＋機器による計測」のペアデータが必要となる。
「私たちは機器にいくつかの固定したにおいを認識させようとしているのではなく、においの世界の座標系を構築しようとしている。」これがOsmoのような企業の核心的な考え方だ。
従来の電子鼻はセンサーアレイによって限られたにおいのカテゴリを識別するもので、汎化能力に限界があった。Osmoが賭けるアプローチは、大規模言語モデルの論理に近い。まず大量かつ多様でアノテーション付きのにおいデータを用いて汎用的な「基盤モデル」を訓練し、その後に特定のシナリオへファインチューニングする。このアプローチの成否は、データの規模とアノテーションの質にかかっているが、これこそが最も難しい課題だ。
人手によるアノテーション：嗅覚AIの「地道な作業」
Osmoのアプローチに近道はない。同社はガスクロマトグラフィー・嗅覚測定法（GC-O）を採用し、まず複雑な混合物の化学成分を一つひとつ分離し、次に人間の嗅覚鑑定士が出口でそれぞれ嗅いで描写する。これにより、各においの分子は人間による「意味的ラベル」を得ると同時に、正確な化学データに裏付けられる。約30名の専門嗅覚鑑定士の仕事は、この「においの辞書」を絶えず拡充することだ。
このプロセスは原始的に聞こえるかもしれないが、現時点では最も信頼できる方法だ。画像アノテーションがクラウドソーシングできるのとは異なり、嗅覚アノテーションは個人の嗅覚感受性と描写能力に強く依存しており、一般人には難しい。Osmoの創業チームは学術界と大手テクノロジー企業出身で、機械学習を使って分子構造とにおいの知覚を対応付けようとする「主要嗅覚マップ（Principal Odor Map）」関連研究に携わっていた。この方向性は嗅覚AI分野における重要な突破口とされ、「アルゴリズムでにおいを予測する」可能性を初めて外部に示した。
注目すべき点として、においデータにはもう一つの本質的な難しさがある。同じ分子でも、濃度が異なれば全く異なるにおいを呈することがあり、異なる分子の組み合わせが予測困難な「嗅覚的創発」効果を生むこともある。これは訓練データが「何か」だけでなく、「どれだけ」「何と一緒に」という情報もカバーする必要があることを意味している。
実験室から産業へ：嗅覚AIは何ができるか
においが真にデジタル化できるなら、その応用シナリオは想像をはるかに超える。食品・飲料業界では、AIが香料の調合や風味の一貫性管理を支援できる。香水業界では、新しい香りの開発を加速し、テキストの説明から処方を生成することさえ可能になる。医療分野では、特定の疾患が人体のにおいの特徴を変化させることがすでに研究で示されており、高感度の「電子鼻」が早期スクリーニングに応用される可能性がある。環境・産業分野では、においセンサーがガス漏れの監視や汚染の早期警告に活用できる。
さらに長期的に見れば、嗅覚基盤モデルはロボットの「第五感」になり得る。視覚と聴覚でロボットが世界を理解するとき、嗅覚によって腐敗、危険、微細な化学変化を感知できるようになる。Osmoが賭けているのは、まさにその日の到来だ——そのとき「においを嗅ぐ」ことは、機器が物理世界を理解するための基本能力の一つとなる。
疑問と課題は残る
ただし、「AIの鼻が人間に匹敵する」という主張はまだ慎重に受け止める必要がある。人間の嗅覚システムは約400種の受容体を持ち、数万種類のにおいを識別できるが、現在のセンサーは感度・安定性・小型化においてなお明らかな差がある。さらに、においの知覚は高度に主観的であり、文化・記憶・感情に左右されるため、「どんなにおいがするか」自体が標準化の難しい問いだ。Osmoの大規模データ戦略がこの溝を真に埋められるかどうかは、時間と産業による検証を待つ必要がある。
しかし少なくとも、ニューアーク空港近くのあの実験室では、30名の嗅覚鑑定士が最もシンプルな方法で、機器に人類最古の感覚の一つを補おうとしている。これはAIが「五感を備える」ための必然的な道のりであり、データ・忍耐・感覚の本質をめぐる長期的な実験でもある。
