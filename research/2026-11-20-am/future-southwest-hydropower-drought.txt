        Drought leads to record low hydropower output in Southwest this summer
KJZZ | 
                    By
Greg Hahne
        Published November 13, 2026 at 2:21 PM MST

White "bathtub rings" show dropping levels on Lake Mead. The reservoir's stores have been dipping steadily, setting records for new all-time lows.
Power output from Glen Canyon and Hoover Dams fell to record lows this summer as the Colorado River Basin has suffered from prolonged drought.
According to the 
Energy Information Administration
, between April and August, hydropower generation in the Southwest was 19% lower than the same time last year, and nearly 30% lower than the last 10-year average.
While there are other dams along the Colorado River, 
Hoover and Glen Canyon generate up to 90% of all hydropower from the waterway. 
The drop in output came as water levels at Lakes Mead and Powell have also hit record lows this year. 
While there have been recent gains in Lake Powell’s elevation, it is still lower than than a year ago.
The federal agency says this year’s ongoing El Niño could bring some drought relief to the region, which could result in a slight increase in hydropower generation. But the report also says it will take more than one year of high precipitation levels to fix ongoing water scarcity issues.
This year is anticipated to have the lowest hydropower generation since at least 2016, with similar levels projected for next year.

Hydropower
A bathtub ring of light minerals shows the high water line of Lake Mead near the Hoover Dam at the Lake Mead National Recreation Area Tuesday, June 30, 2026, near Boulder City, Nev. (AP Photo/John Locher)
Hydropower production hit a record low this summer in the Southwestern U.S. as prolonged drought conditions continue to plague the Colorado River Basin, affecting states like Arizona, New Mexico, and small parts of Nevada and California.
The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) recently 
broke down
 the decline in hydropower generation between April and August 2026, which was 19% lower than during the same period last year, and 28% lower than the 10-year average, according to the EIA’s latest Form EIA-923 survey.
Data source:
 U.S. Energy Information Administration, Form EIA-923, 
Power Plant Operating Data
The water elevation levels at Lake Powell and Lake Mead, which are formed by the Glen Canyon Dam and Hoover Dam, respectively, hit record lows, EIA said. Lake Powell needs a water elevation of about 3,490 feet (known as a a minimum power pool) in order to generate hydropower at the Glen Canyon Dam. At the end of September, that elevation reached 2,518 feet, or less than 1% above minimum power pool. Similarly, the Hoover Dam requires an elevation of at least 950 feet at Lake Mead, which reached 1,038 feet at the end of September, or 9% above the minimum power pool. 
Seven states rely on the Colorado River Basin for hydropower generation: Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming. The U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, which manages most dams along the river, released water from Flaming Gorge, a reservoir located in the Upper Colorado River Basin, to help mitigate the risk of Lake Powell’s water elevation falling below the minimum power pool. 
Additionally, water releases from Lake Powell were reduced in order to maintain an elevation of at least 3,510 feet. However, EIA notes that lower water releases from Lake Powell could also impact water elevation downstream at Lake Mead, which could then affect hydropower generation at the Hoover Dam. The Department of the Interior thus decided to reduce the collective water consumption of California, Arizona, and Nevada by 1.25 million acre-feet each year over the next two years.
Data source:
 U.S. Energy Information Administration, 
Short-Term Energy Outlook
, November 2026
EIA expects that hydropower generation in the Southwest will total 6.13 billion kilowatthours (BkWh) in 2026 in its November 
Short-Term Energy Outlook
 (STEO), 9% lower than last year and 24% lower than the 10-year average. Next year, EIA expects hydropower generation in the Southwest to total 6.1 BkWh, 0.5% lower than this year. Although this year’s El Niño could provide some relief through more precipitation, EIA argues that “one good water year likely won’t eliminate the effects of multiple years of water scarcity.” 
According to the STEO, hydropower generation in the Southwest is expected to account for about 4% of the region’s generation mix in 2026. That share has declined from a peak of 8% in 2011.

Hydropower plants in the Southwest produced record low amounts of electricity this summer, the U.S. Energy Information Administration 
said
 on November 13. Generation from April through August 2026 was 19% lower than in the same months last year and 28% below the 
10-year
 average, according to EIA's survey of power plants. The region, as EIA defines it, covers Arizona, New Mexico and small parts of Nevada and California, and the agency puts the decline down to prolonged drought in the Colorado River Basin.
Lake Powell and Lake Mead, the reservoirs behind Glen Canyon Dam and Hoover Dam, both hit record lows, EIA says. Glen Canyon Dam can generate power only while Lake Powell stays at or above 3,490 feet, a level known as the minimum power pool. At the end of September the lake stood at 3,518 feet, less than 1% above it. Hoover Dam needs Lake Mead at 950 feet or higher, and the lake was at 1,038 feet, 9% above that level.
