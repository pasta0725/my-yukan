Our modern world runs on math, from social media algorithms, package delivery, and weather prediction to medical imaging and machine learning. The rise of computers helped fuel its dominance, enabling us to work with increasingly large numbers that would otherwise be beyond the capacity of our finite human brains. But with the rise of large language models, AI chatbots, and, soon, quantum computing, so-called “big math” is beginning to outstrip its creators entirely. What
that means
, and how we might adapt, is the focus of a new book,
Big Math: The Hidden Codes That Run Our World
, by applied mathematicians Steven Strogatz and Alex Townsend.
Strogatz is perhaps best known in his field for his co-authorship of a
seminal 1998 paper
with Duncan Watts on the collective dynamics of
small-world networks.
He has also studied chaos theory as applied to physics, the geometry of supercoiled DNA, and the biological collective oscillators, such as swarms of synchronously flashing fireflies and crickets that chirp in unison. In 2005, Strogatz extended
his study
of synchronicity to the crowds that caused the wobbling of London’s
Millennium Bridge
.
An award-winning communicator, Strogatz wrote three series of columns on mathematics for The New York Times in 2010, 2012, and 2025 and hosted a podcast for Quanta magazine from 2020–2021. He has written four popular books:
Sync
(2003),
The Calculus of Friendship
(2009),
The Joy of X
(2012), and the New York Times bestseller
Infinite Powers
(2019).
This time around, Strogatz has found a well-matched co-author in Townsend, a younger colleague recently hired at Cornell who studies numerical analysis and deep learning. As senior faculty, Strogatz was assigned to mentor Townsend, and the two men began regularly lunching together in Risley Hall. Strogatz was intrigued by the popular book potential of Townsend’s course on the top 20 algorithms of the 20th century and suggested co-authorship. That concept slowly morphed into a book about big matrix calculations, and then into what became
Big Math
.
“There are so many books about pure math, as if computers didn’t exist,” Strogatz told Ars. “Alex and I are both very applied mathematicians. We thought there should be a book on computers and math, especially the class of really big problems with billions of variables or parameters that you just can’t approach in your head. A lot of the math we’ve had for centuries perfectly scales up to this computer moment. What the abacus did for arithmetic, the
fangcheng
(“rectangular array”) board did for systems of linear equations. It was a matrix you could manipulate with your hand and bamboo sticks. So this externalizing of math outside of our heads into our tools is a 2,000-year-old idea.”
Mathematics now faces its greatest challenge yet in the age of AI, sparking heated debates on the role of human reasoning when the machines are making most of the major breakthroughs. “The mathematician in each of us will always want to know more,” Strogatz and Townsend wrote. “The human being must insist that knowledge serves something larger than itself. That might be the work of our century: letting our power grow without losing our soul. Big Math keeps getting bigger. So must we.”
Ars caught up with Strogatz to learn more.
Ars Technica: Let’s start by defining “big math,” because it’s such a broad umbrella term.
Steven Strogatz
: Operationally, big math is “big” relative to human cognitive capacity. So it can be surprisingly small and yet big. We also wanted to expand it to things that people would think of as computational engineering—in the building of the Burj Khalifa, that’d be civil engineering or design architecture. It’s there in medical imaging. It’s in getting us to the Moon. The lunar module was controlled by a tiny computer that only had to think about 20 or 30 variables at a time to steer the LEM down to the Moon’s surface. But it’s not something a person could do in their head.
Big Math is not simply big in the ordinary sense of big numbers. There’s an undercurrent in the book about the importance of linearity and sparsity. When problems are linear, they can be scaled up to immense size, and they stay tractable. If they’re non-linear, you don’t have any hope of dealing with them with pure math. You can do them with computers. But for the people who want rigorous theorems, we’re in terra incognita when we deal with anything that’s non-linear and big.
Small math can be very hard. Historically, we had small math problems with one or two variables, arithmetic, algebra that you learn in high school. Even to this day, a lot of pure math is relatively small. It doesn’t concern itself with enormous numbers of variables simultaneously because it’s totally intractable. Cryptography can be small, but it’s so complicated with its non-linearities and other algebraic difficulties. So big is one way to be difficult, but there are a lot of other ways.
Nowadays, trillions of parameters are typical in the biggest of the large language models. But infinity is not big from this point. Infinity, which you would think of would be the biggest of big math, is not because calculus fits perfectly nicely in a human head. Infinity turns out to be very tractable in a way that a billion is not.

Ars: One of your real-world examples of big math is particularly relevant at the moment: mathematics as it relates to the gerrymandering issue.
Strogatz
: It’s an interesting question of, what’s a fair map and what’s a reasonable redistricting? The legal system gives us certain criteria, but they are vague. The districts should not be too snakey-looking, not look like salamanders, for instance. The term in the law is ‘compact.’ They should be reasonably compact. But
what does
that
mean mathematically
? The districts are also supposed to be contiguous; you don’t want to have separate islands and call that one district. It’s supposed to respect natural boundaries like rivers or other landscape features. Also, each district is supposed to have roughly the same population.
So how to make maps that respect all of the criteria? It turns out there are a lot of ways to make them. Using past voting information, you can stack the deck. That’s what gerrymandering is all about, trying to redistrict in favor of whatever party is in power.
There has been a big debate about whether certain maps violate people’s rights. The principle should be one person, one vote. If you do things that dilute somebody’s power so that their votes are wasted, so to speak, that’s traditionally thought to be unfair or maybe even illegal.
The Supreme Court has heard all kinds of cases, but the court is very resistant to the use of math
. I quoted [Chief Justice] John Roberts talking about the efficiency gap as one measure of gerrymandering. He referred to it by its Greek letters and said something like, “The average person on the street is just going to think this is gobbledygook, that this is not a criterion that the law should pay any attention to.”
Nevertheless,
Moon Duchin
and her colleagues showed this very down-to-earth way of generating a big ensemble of reasonable maps that satisfy all the legal criteria so that you have a baseline, you could do the control experiment. What the math does is give you a principled way of generating maps at random that respect the criteria that the law requires, but otherwise are just a random sample of reasonable maps. How often would a map produce the result that the gerrymander intended? If you can show that it’s an extreme outlier, that’s a measure of something. Does it prove malicious intent? Not really. It does prove it’s not typical.
There was an Alabama
voting rights case
where Roberts actually voted with the plaintiffs, who said that there was real harm being done to Black voters in Alabama with one of Alabama’s maps. The state was claiming there’s no way we could get a reasonable map to have two districts with Black majority voters. Moon Duchin showed that was wrong. You could generate thousands of maps that would do that. So it just wasn’t true what the state had claimed.
After the book was copyedited and ready to go to press, a
Supreme Court decision
was made that, in fact, you can partisan gerrymander as much as you want. It doesn’t violate the Voting Rights Act. We wrote a more optimistic story.
Ars: How has math changed over time from a philosophical standpoint?
Strogatz
: I think a crisp way to encapsulate it is the role of the computer. For a long time, a computer was a servant to mathematicians and engineers and scientists. It was no more philosophically threatening than a microscope or a telescope. It could extend our senses. It’s a chisel, it’s an anvil, it’s a tool. It helped us do calculations faster; things that would be too hard for people to do at scale you could do on a machine. We had mechanical calculators. They became electronic computers in the 1950s with the invention of the transistor and got much faster to give us today’s supercomputers. We program them; they do exactly what we tell them. So they’re servants.
The first philosophically big change is in the 1950s, when computers start to be used as more than servants; they become scouts. You could send the computer out into unknown, unexplored territory in the mathematical landscape. The clearest example was work by Enrico Fermi and his colleagues (Pasta, Ulam, and Tsingou) that we wrote about in the chapter on simulation. Mary Tsingou was the one who did the calculations in what was traditionally called the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam numerical experiment.
What distinguished it from earlier uses of computers is that Fermi and colleagues had no idea what the answer was going to be. It’s one thing to use a computer to calculate the power released by an atomic bomb blast or to calculate an artillery shell’s trajectory. We didn’t know the exact numerical values, but we knew what was happening. With this experiment, they were trying to simulate a simplified model of how atoms could, in the jargon, thermalize as chemical bonds jiggle. You want to see the decay to entropy. How do systems degenerate to greater and greater disorder if they’re just left to their own devices?
Fermi thought that the non-linear aspect of chemical bonds—meaning if you’d stretch a chemical bond by twice as much, it doesn’t pull back exactly twice as hard, so it’s not strictly proportional—might account for the so-called second law of thermodynamics, the decay to entropy. Pencil and paper math was not up to the job because you couldn’t calculate these non-linear interactions far enough to see the decay into entropy.
So they ran it in the computer, and a shocking thing happened: It didn’t decay. It showed a strange echo. You started all the energy in one of the modes of vibration of this system, and then it started to degenerate. But after a while, 98 percent of the energy came back into the original mode. That was the echo that they called a recurrence phenomenon. It led us to a whole new branch of math that we call soliton theory. It was an early indication that once you walk into the nonlinear jungle, human intuition breaks down. We couldn’t walk there, but we sent our scout, this computer probe, into the wilderness of the mind, and it was the only thing capable of doing the calculations and reporting back to us what it found.
Ars: The rapid rise of AI in mathematics has
alarmed
many in the field, particularly in light of OpenAI’s
release last month
of 722 AI-generated math papers, dubbed the “
mathocalypse
.” Technically, AI is also a new tool, but it seems fundamentally different.
Strogatz
: So from servant to scout was a new thing, and computing was our partner. At first, it was all very thrilling. It was like having a new Ferrari or something. You could race ahead and then discover things. That’s what Fermi and Ulam said at the time. That was truly amazing. They didn’t know what to expect. The computer was now teaching us by showing us things, but we could still keep up. We could develop these new theories like soliton theory, so we could rationalize what it was showing us.
What’s different now is that it’s going from servant to scout to oracle, where it’s showing us things that we don’t understand and it’s not articulate enough to explain itself. It
seems
to be right. A clear example would be something like Google Translate, where it’s fantastic at translating one language into another, but it has not discovered new principles of linguistics, or if it has, it can’t tell us what they are.
Likewise, protein folding with AlphaFold that won the Nobel Prize in chemistry a couple of years ago for the people who built it. We now can solve a thousand times more proteins than we had before, but we have not done it by learning new physics or chemistry. It just recognized patterns, but it can’t tell us how it’s doing it. So that’s where we are, and it’s going to get worse.
Ars: In your book, you ask if insight still matters, citing
Stephen Wolfram
as an example of someone who might answer that question in the negative.
Strogatz
: Wolfram’s position is tricky. He pushes computational irreducibility. I love these two words, perspicuous and perspicacious. Systems can be perspicuous; you can see into them. Science has, for thousands of years, concerned itself with systems that are perspicuous, meaning you can get insight into them because you’re so perspicacious. But the question from Wolfram would be, have we been the famous drunk that’s looking under the lamppost for his keys because it’s dark everywhere else? You wouldn’t have any hope of finding them except under the lamppost.
So is it that we’ve artificially restricted ourself to systems that are transparent enough that we can develop science that our insight illuminates? I think Wolfram would claim that most of nature is computationally irreducible in the sense that there is no simple insightful theory into what’s going on. The best you can do is just run a simulation and watch what happens, similar to just letting nature unfold and watch what happens in reality.
In the jargon people use nowadays, you shouldn’t expect a compressed description, a simplified model that will account for what you’re seeing. We have seen those. Newton could explain the motion of the planets from a few simple principles and that worked, but that’s, I think in Wolfram’s take, artificially simple. Most of science is not going to end up being like that. W
e don’t know if Wolfram is really right about that. Wolfram, I think, is demonstrably right that many computer simulations are computationally irreducible, but nature might not be.
