This week at SC26, NVIDIA is marking two decades of
CUDA
by honoring the researchers, developers and engineers who helped make GPU computing a foundation for modern science and AI.
CUDA’s 20th anniversary presents an occasion to reflect across computing eras, from the people who wrote the very first CUDA programs to the AI developers building on their work today.
At the platform’s core is this developer community whose experiments, tools and persistence expanded what accelerated computing could do.
From SC06 to SC26
NVIDIA
announced CUDA in November 2006
and brought it to the scientific community at SC06, giving scientists and developers a familiar, high-level programming model for using GPUs. It fundamentally extended accelerated computing beyond graphics use cases to accelerate demanding applications for science, simulation and more.
In the two decades since, developers have used CUDA to advance
Nobel Prize-winning AI research
, accelerate
scientific applications on supercomputers
and support
visual effects in Oscar-winning films
.
Today,
more than 11 million developers
build on CUDA across the computing stack.

Honoring the Architects of Progress
Now, at SC26, NVIDIA is looking back at some of the CUDA luminaries who’ve spearheaded breakthroughs in science and AI:
At the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, biophysicist Klaus Schulten’s team helped connect GPU computing with molecular biology through
Nanoscale Molecular Dynamics and Visual Molecular Dynamics
, tools for molecular simulation and visualization. In 2013, researchers at Illinois, the University of Pittsburgh and Vanderbilt University used CUDA-accelerated tools to help resolve the HIV capsid’s atomic structure. They combined experiments with simulations on
Blue Waters
, one of the world’s most powerful supercomputers at the time.
In a critical breakthrough in AI, University of Toronto researchers Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton used CUDA to
train AlexNet
on just two NVIDIA GPUs. Their image-recognition system
won the 2012 ImageNet competition
, helping demonstrate the potential of GPU computing for deep learning.
That connection between computing and scientific discovery is seen with AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for protein structure prediction. Their team’s
publicly released AlphaFold 2 software
uses CUDA for GPU execution
.
These are just a few of many examples that show how one generation’s work creates opportunities for the next. With 1,000+ NVIDIA CUDA-X libraries, developers are now reshaping industries and giving machines the power to see, learn and reason — from discovering new medicines and designing next-generation factories to advancing autonomous vehicles, modeling financial markets and unlocking quantum computing — and more.
