Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, one of mathematics’s most notoriously difficult problems, with the help of Claude, cracking a puzzle that has eluded some of the world’s best mathematicians for decades. If verified, it would mark yet another seismic achievement for AI in mathematics and add to the
upheaval already unleashed by OpenAI’s previous, often inelegant forays
into the field.
Alpöge’s solution has yet to be verified independently. Anthropic has not commented or involved itself in Alpöge’s announcement. In
a document on his website
, Alpöge says he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about the solution “late Sunday night” and suggests he held off publication until after the
company’s IPO on Thursday
.
The Riemann hypothesis is one of seven Millennium Prize problems, which are widely considered some of the most formidable open challenges in mathematics. First proposed in 1859,
the Riemann hypothesis
concerns the
distribution of prime numbers
and how zeroes of a function are involved. It’s regarded as one of the most important unsolved problems in mathematics.
A statement on Alpöge’s website says Claude was integral to developing the proof:
I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof
1
of the Riemann Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions. It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.
The Verge
reached out to Anthropic but didn’t immediately hear back. Anthropic said it was working on
the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude in August
, but noted at the time: “We don’t expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis.”
Alpöge’s solution, presented in Lean, has only been published on his website and has not yet been publicly accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which administers the Millennium Prize and offers a $1 million bounty for each problem solved. Under the institute’s
rules
, a proposed solution must be published and withstand at least two years of scrutiny before it can be considered for the prize, during which time it must have “received general acceptance in the global mathematics community.”

The AI takeover of mathematics has begun
In the quarter-century since the prize was established, only one problem has been officially solved: The
Poincaré conjecture
, a topological problem concerning three-dimensional spheres. In September, OpenAI
claimed
to have cracked another, the Navier-Stokes problem, which concerns the flow of fluids.
OpenAI said it pursued Navier-Stokes after hearing rumors that rival researchers were closing in on a solution.
Alpöge had been working on the problem
with New York University mathematics professor Tristan Buckmaster. Buckmaster claimed OpenAI produced a solution for Navier-Stokes using the route he and Alpöge were working on with OpenAI’s Codex and Claude.
OpenAI spent millions of dollars and deployed 10,000 agents in the attempt. The result is widely considered credible, though it has yet to receive formal recognition. But the achievement has been
marred
by
allegations
of questionable research practices, including claims it used unpublished work from mathematicians who had shared their research with its AI tools. OpenAI has strongly denied wrongdoing, but this has done little to quell the uproar.
Though most of the spotlight has been on OpenAI’s movement in math, Alpöge
said in July that he solved
the Jacobean conjecture using Claude. The deluge of findings has left mathematicians scrambling to digest the material and
figure out where they belong
in what is rapidly becoming a profoundly different mathematical landscape.
