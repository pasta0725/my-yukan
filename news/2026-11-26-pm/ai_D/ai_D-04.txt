TITLE: Anthropic’s Dario Amodei calls accusations of surrendering human control to AI ‘absurd’
SITE: TechCrunch AI
PUBLISHED: 2026-11-26 04:44 (page: 2026-11-25T19:44:24+00:00)

TEXT:
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei is pushing back against claims that Anthropic believes we should “surrender human free will” to AI systems.
In a long X post
on Wednesday, Amodei called the suggestion “absurd.”
“We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security,” he wrote. This argument against autocracy applies word for word against governance by AI, and I feel equally strongly that turning over governance to AI is a bad idea…No one who had carefully read my writing or my interviews could reasonably conclude that I believe anything different.”
The statement comes after
a growing chorus from Anthropic rivals
suggesting that human agency could be at risk if AI is developed irresponsibly. Earlier this month, OpenAI CEO
Sam Altman said
he wanted to ensure people continue to have “agency over the future.”
Meta’s chief AI officer Alexandr Wang also wrote in an
essay on X
that we “should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI.”
“It is hard to imagine a world where humanity can maintain control over fully unconstrained recursive self-improvement,” Wang wrote. “Now that we are at a point where the scientific and technical problems on capability progress are likely solved, we must not only make progress in the extremely urgent areas of safety, alignment, and security, but we have to start thinking through how we progress thoughtfully.”
But in published essays, Amodei claims he has repeatedly advocated for democratic oversight of AI, saying the technology should strengthen democratic institutions and that humans should retain ultimate decision-making power.
Amodei noted that there’s a difference between surrendering human free will broadly and the idea that “as AI systems advance, they become capable of doing
specific
tasks in a more automated way.” As examples, he gave AI driving a car, designing a cancer drug, or writing end-to-end software.
Amodei suggested drawing a line at recursive self-improvement (RSI), or AI that can improve itself without human assistance.
Many safety experts
see RSI as inevitably leading to a loss of human control, a point Amodei implicitly acknowledged, writing, “we may want limits on this process.”
Still, Anthropic is laying the groundwork for RSI by incorporating AI into an increasing share of its own research and development. The company said in
June
that Claude authored more than 80% of the code merged into Anthropic’s codebase. An
independent assessment published
this month by AI safety research nonprofit METR found that Anthropic’s models are improving at about twice their historical rate, primarily driven by AI-assisted research.
In its
own IPO filings,
Anthropic describes how each successive generation of Claude accelerates the development of the next, a process it refers to as “closing the loop.”
“As we said publicly in early November, we don’t expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding,” Amodei wrote.
The problem with “meaningful human supervision or understanding” is that AI is already advancing faster than engineers and safety experts can fully audit it, leaving them increasingly reliant on other AI systems to act as judge and jury. The issue compounds when AI models engage in deceptive behavior, particularly when they recognize they’re being evaluated, making them harder to trust and control.
Still, Anthropic is by no means the only company that is pursuing RSI or building AI systems to automate more and more human tasks. This week, OpenAI claimed to have solved one of the
biggest open problems in computer science
— the exact speed limit of matrix multiplication, which is the mathematical engine behind modern computing, including deep learning and physics simulations.
In his post, Amodei accused competitors of engaging in a “vibes-based commercial and political positioning,” disingenuously throwing around phrases like “human control and agency” or “AI should just be a tool” without approaching the AI race any differently.
“Certainly the products that they offer don’t differ materially in their orientation towards automation of tasks; every company seems to be releasing models more and more capable of task automation,” Amodei wrote. “Every company claims to understand there are risks and downsides of such automation and to offer safeguards.”
