Quantum processor deployments at supercomputing centers have grown nearly sevenfold over the past five years, with nearly 30 supercomputing centers around the world now in the process of deploying over 50 quantum processors.
Many of these centers use
Slurm
to run scientific supercomputing workloads, so expanding its support for quantum processors builds on familiar workflows and tools, enabling computational chemists, nuclear physicists and materials scientists to bring quantum computing into their research domains without friction.
In a new release, Slurm builds on previous work with IBM, Pasqal and the wider quantum computing community to allow QPU builders to create a single interface to their hardware for every supercomputing center, through a simple file. This makes it easier than ever for HPC centers and QPU builders to integrate and serve their hardware.
The expansion is already being adopted by
Alice & Bob
,
IBM
,
IQM
,
Pasqal
,
Quantinuum
and
MQSC
and supports the widely used
QRMI
and
QDMI
interfaces.

Beating the Bottleneck
Until now, integrating a QPU into a supercomputing center meant a new software challenge for every new QPU or supercomputing center. By building on existing quantum-HPC interfaces such as QRMI and QDMI, the Slurm expansion allows QPU builders to reuse their integrations across all their deployments. It also means that supercomputing centers can add new quantum processors to their existing scheduling and resource-management workflows without developing bespoke QPU-specific solutions.
For quantum computing to advance science, it needs to be used by scientists. Now scientists can run quantum-classical applications developed with platforms such as NVIDIA CUDA-Q with Slurm coordinating access to all the processing they need.
Slurm 26.11 will be available later this month.
Orchestrating Resources in an Open Quantum-GPU Platform
The
Slurm expansion
adds a community-developed orchestration layer to NVIDIA’s open platform for quantum-GPU supercomputing, which centers around the CUDA-Q open platform for hybrid quantum-classical computing. At SC26, new releases expand all aspects of the platform:
CUDA-Q QEC
allows researchers developing quantum error correction to rapidly prototype and test new ideas, including with a new
decoder server
. A new TCBP-JOBS decoder is 13,000x faster than the industry standard.
CUDA-Q
gets an expanded layer of fault-tolerant primitives that application builders can use to realistically compile their ideas. Fermilab has already used CUDA-Q to perform studies in three weeks that would normally have taken five months.
CUDA-Q Algorithms
provides reusable components for builders of quantum applications, including new capabilities in helping design the loading data into quantum circuits and tools specifically for chemistry and materials applications
CUDA-Q Pulse
gives quantum hardware builders a layer for
shaping the ‘pulses’
needed to control qubits on a QPU, including tools to GPU accelerate their generation.
The quantum ecosystem around the world is continuing to build with this platform. Quantum Motion integrated its QUICHE chemistry framework with CUDA-Q. Altera and Oxford Quantum Circuits designed an FPGA-based controller connecting qubit control and readout to GPUs through NVQLink.
At supercomputing scale, QunaSys used cuQuantum and 1,024 NVIDIA Hopper GPUs on ABCI-Q to simulate more than 300 million quantum operations, reaching chemical accuracy for a four-hydrogen-atom model.
NVIDIA researchers used
JUPITER
to simulate 104 qubits under realistic noise, testing fault-tolerant designs before they’re built.
