As of November 6, 2026:
 Starship Flight 15 is targeted for no earlier than Friday, November 13, from Pad 2 at Starbase, Texas. SpaceX Vice President of Launch Kiko Dontchev gave that date on stage at the UP.Summit on November 5, Teslarati reported, citing Ars Technica's Eric Berger; an FAA air-traffic planning slide also lists it, NASASpaceflight (NSF) reported. SpaceX has not posted a Flight 15 mission page or a launch time, and NSF reported that SpaceX still needs FAA approval of a license change for the ship catch. The date is a target and may move.
This is SpaceNexus analysis of what the flight would change if it works. For the last flight, see 
What Flight 14 Changes
.
What SpaceX plans
On 
SpaceX's
 October 20 earnings call, Elon Musk said the company had gathered enough data from Flight 14 to attempt a catch of both the Super Heavy first stage and the Starship upper stage on the next flight, Ars Technica reported. Musk said he believes success is "more likely than not."
The vehicles are Booster 22 and Ship 42, according to NSF. Both have completed full-duration static fires: Booster 22 on Pad 2 on October 25, and Ship 42 at SpaceX's Massey's test site on October 26 and 27. Ship 42's next steps include "loading of a batch of Starlink satellites," NSF reported.
In NSF's words: "No Ship has yet been caught, and it would also be the first Block 3 booster catch and the first catch at Pad 2." NSF understands that the working plan is a mission of roughly 24 hours, so the ship's orbit can line up with a re-entry corridor back to Starbase. SpaceX has not published the profile.
What a ship catch is, and why it matters
A catch means flying a stage back to the tower so its arms take hold of it. On Flight 5, on October 13, 2024, the booster "was caught by the chopstick arms of the launch and catch tower at Starbase." SpaceX has caught a Super Heavy three times; the third, on Flight 8 in March 2025, was "the third successful catch of a Super Heavy booster." Our 
catch record
 has every return.
SpaceX's September update set out the two endings for a ship: a deorbit burn, then "reentry and splashdown in a pre-determined location in the ocean or a return to launch site for catch and reuse." SpaceX says Starship "is designed to be fully reusable, which means it is built to survive the rigors of reentry intact." No ship has flown twice yet: SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said on the call the company would attempt to refly a Starship upper stage "early" next year, Ars reported.
Musk set out why this matters to SpaceX: "The rate at which we launch Starship will increase dramatically as we solve rapid and complete reusability." NSF put the near-term stake plainly in October: a successful dual catch "would mark the first full recovery attempt of a Starship vehicle and open the door to subsequent milestones, including the first reflights of a properly recovered Ship." 
(Analysis: if the ship catch works, both halves of Starship come home for the first time, and turnaround could start to become a matter of inspection and refurbishment rather than a new upper stage for every flight.)
What has to go right, and the risks
The license.
 SpaceX needs FAA approval of a license modification, "including permission to bring Ship 42 back to the tower at Starbase, which will involve an overflight of populated areas during the return," NSF reported. Ars noted the ship "must return to the launch site over land, including northern Mexico." Shotwell said: "We have had significant constructive engagement with the FAA, and we expect the license for our Flight 15 to include return to launch site and tower catch for both stages."
A healthy ship in orbit.
 On Flight 14, a Raptor Vacuum engine shut down; NSF reported that Musk described it as a software failure isolated to that engine's computer. For Flight 15, NSF reported, controllers "would keep built-in early-return points if they are not confident the vehicle can safely deorbit at the end of the profile," the same conservative approach that ended Flight 14 after about three hours.
Re-entry and the catch.
(Analysis: the ship must come back from orbit with its heat shield intact and reach the tower precisely enough for the arms to close. The booster has done the last part; the ship has not.)
Honest odds.
 Booster catches have not all gone to plan: on Flight 6, SpaceX said, "automated health checks of critical hardware on the launch and catch tower triggered an abort of the catch attempt." 
(Analysis: "more likely than not" leaves real room for a waved-off attempt or a lost ship, either of which would be a test result, not the end of the program.)
What it means for SpaceX
Cadence.
 Musk said he expects Starship to reach a "weekly" flight cadence in the first half of 2027 and to launch twice weekly in the second half, Ars reported, noting that Musk "often offers hyper-optimistic timeframes." Shotwell said SpaceX would keep launching Starlink V3 satellites next year. Flight 14 put the first 26 V3 satellites in orbit. 
(Analysis: Starlink can absorb a high Starship flight rate, and that rate depends on reuse.)
Propellant transfer.
 In October 2025, SpaceX said "a long-duration flight test and the in-space propellant transfer flight test" were "targeted to take place in 2026." That has slipped: SpaceX will seek to complete the refueling test early in 2027, Ars reported from the earnings call, and NSF said NASA's latest Starship milestone chart places the ship-to-ship demonstration in the first quarter of 2027. A day-long Flight 15 "would deliver critical data on propellant boil-off rates," NSF wrote.
What it means for U.S. launch
The FAA.
 SpaceX is currently authorized to conduct "up to 25 annual launches" from Starbase, according to the FAA's October 14 notice in the Federal Register. The FAA is reviewing a SpaceX proposal for up to 100 launches a year in a first phase and up to 456 in a second, which "would be implemented within several years." None of it is approved yet, NSF noted, and Starbase has not approached 25 launches in any calendar year.
Florida.
 Booster 23 and Ship 43 are being lined up for the first Starship launch from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center, NSF reported, though that debut "now looks more likely early in 2027 than before year-end."
The rest of the market.
 United Launch Alliance announced on October 28 that it plans to return its Vulcan rocket to flight on November 13, the same target date, pending range approval, Ars reported. Blue Origin now plans New Glenn's return to flight in January, Ars reported, citing Bloomberg. Caleb Henry of Quilty Space said the launch crisis "will not go away this decade for sure," Ars reported. 
(Analysis: if catch-and-reflight works, Starship adds heavy-lift capacity during a shortage, though the FAA notice shows how far the paperwork has to move before Texas can host a high flight rate.)
What it means for the Moon
NASA's current plan.
 Artemis III, launching in 2027, is now a crewed mission in low Earth orbit to test rendezvous and docking with test versions of landers from both Blue Origin and SpaceX, NASA says; NASA officials said "summer" 2027 at an October 23 news conference, Ars reported. SpaceX will fly "a Starship Version 3 test article from Florida" with a docking system at its nose; the crew "will not enter Starship," NASA says. NASA is aiming for a crewed landing in 2028 and says that, "based on lander readiness," it will decide which company goes first. Our 
Moon tracker
 follows each step.
How the Starship lander would get there.
 NASA describes "the series of propellant 'tanker' Starships in low Earth orbit" filling a "depot" Starship, which then fuels the lander. The lander and Orion then "rendezvous and dock in Earth orbit" before the lander sends both to low lunar orbit.
How many tankers: contested.
 At NASA's Aerospace Safety Advisory Panel meeting on October 22, member Charlie Precourt said: "I think the last estimate is around 22 flights," Ars Technica reported; he did not name Starship. Musk responded on X: "That's way too high. Actual number will be more like 6 to 8." At NASA's October 23 news conference, officials declined to give a precise number, Ars reported, and Administrator Jared Isaacman said Musk's figure was possible but represented the "ultimate potential end state" of Starship. 
(Analysis: whichever number holds, every tanker is a Starship launch, so the lunar plan depends on ships flying often and cheaply, which is what catching and reflying them is for. Flight 15 does not test refueling, but it tests the return half of that cycle.)
China.
 Chinese officials laid out plans at a conference in Beijing for a crewed lunar landing "before 2030," Ars reported; more in our 
analysis of China's plan
. Ars wrote that depending on the landers, "a US lunar landing could be delayed to 2030 or even later."

What to watch on launch day
The FAA license change,
 and whether it covers the ship's return to the tower.
A SpaceX mission page and launch time,
 neither posted as of November 6.
Booster 22's return:
 the first attempted catch of a Block 3 booster and the first at Pad 2.
Ship 42 in orbit:
 the Starlink deployment, and whether it flies the full day or returns early.
The ship's return to Starbase and the catch attempt,
 the first for a Starship upper stage.
After the flight:
 a ship reflight (early 2027, per Shotwell), the propellant-transfer test and the first Starship launch from Florida, which could be Flight 16.

SpaceX is targeting Friday, Nov 13 for Starship Flight 15, with the mission expected to attempt the first catch of the upper-stage Starship vehicle. The date puts a firmer mark on the 
November dual-catch plan
 SpaceX sketched out after Flight 14 reached orbit for the first time.
SpaceX VP of Launch Kiko Dontchev announced the date at Up.Summit on Thursday, saying the company is aiming for that Friday window, and that the flight is expected to include the first-ever Starship catch.
Kiko Dontchev of SpaceX just said at the UP.Summit that the company is targeting Friday, Nov. 13, for the launch Starship Flight 15.
— Eric Berger (@SciGuySpace) 
November 5, 2026
That goal lines up with what Elon Musk described on the Oct 20 SpaceX earnings call, when he said Flight 15 would try to catch both the Super Heavy booster and the Ship. Musk put the odds above even money, calling a full recovery more likely than not and framing it as a first for an orbital launch vehicle.
President and COO Gwynne Shotwell added that SpaceX expected the Flight 15 licence to cover return-to-launch-site tower catches for both stages.
Flight 14, 
flown in late September,
 put Starship into orbit and deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites, moving the program past the suborbital tests that defined earlier flights. SpaceX has already caught Super Heavy several times, 
starting with Flight 5
 in October 2024, but it has yet to catch the Ship. Bringing that upper stage home from orbital speed for a tower catch remains the harder recovery step.
On Flight 15, both vehicles would steer back toward the launch tower so its mechanical arms can attempt the catches. A Nov 13 target would put that attempt about eight days out, though dates can still slip for weather, hardware readiness, or regulatory timing. If both stages are caught, SpaceX would clear a major reusability milestone on the path to higher flight rates; if not, Musk has said a steady stream of attempts is already lined up through the rest of the year, with launch cadence expected to rise into 2027.
