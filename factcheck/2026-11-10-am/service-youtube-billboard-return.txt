YouTube Streams Will Once Again Count Toward 
Billboard
 Charts
By 
Chris DeVille
1:21 PM EST on November 9, 2026
Add Stereogum to Google
Kirill KUDRYAVTSEV / AFP
Huge news for chart nerds and aspiring chart-topping musicians: 
Billboard
 will soon count YouTube streams again when tabulating its music charts. The change will take place starting in 2027, according to a 
blog post
 from YouTube executive and music industry veteran Lyor Cohen.
Measuring the popularity of recorded music with charts is a tricky endeavor, partially because so many of the vectors are moving targets. 
What
 people are listening to is in a constant state of flux, but so is 
how
 people are listening. There have always been flaws in 
Billboard
's methodology, like in the '90s, when a song could only crack the primary singles chart, the Hot 100, if it had been officially released as a single, even if it was all over the radio and MTV. Speaking of which, music video plays on TV have never counted toward the charts, even when networks like MTV, VH1, BET, and CMT were dictating cultural taste to a widespread extent.
The internet and television are different beasts, and music videos did factor into 
Billboard
's calculations once they were on YouTube. YouTube's streaming data was first factored into 
Billboard
's tabulations in February 2013, which immediately allowed Baauer's meme-friendly hit "
Harlem Shake
" to shake up the charts as listeners flocked to hundreds of user-made videos featuring the track. In 2019, 
Billboard
 incorporated YouTube's data into its main album chart, the Billboard 200.
But YouTube 
withdrew its data
 from 
Billboard
 in January of this year, complaining that 
Billboard
's "outdated" system placed too much weight on subscription-backed streams and unfairly deprioritized free, ad-supported streams. "This doesn't reflect how fans engage with music today and ignores the massive engagement from fans who don’t have a subscription," Cohen wrote at the time.
Now, 
Billboard
 has relented. "We are returning to the 
Billboard
 charts starting in 2027," Cohen writes. "
Billboard
’s new methodology better reflects how fans engage with music today across both subscription and ad-supported services." 
Billboard
 specifies that YouTube stats will inform the rankings beginning with the Jan. 16, 2027 charts, which will measure listening between Jan. 1-7.
The haggling seemed to be over how YouTube streams would be counted on the Billboard 200. Under the current rules, 2,500 ad-supported streams count the same as one full album purchase (physical or digital). The new rules reduce the one album equivalent to 1,500 ad-supported streams. Under both the old and new rules, one album purchase is equal to 1,000 paid/subscription streams. As a result, expect the distinction between the album and singles charts to continue to blur.

Billboard's charts are increasingly weighted towards on-demand streaming, but not enough for YouTube
The video platform will stop sharing data with Billboard after January 16, 2026.
By
Ian Carlos Campbell
Dec. 17, 2025 5:02 pm EST
ASSOCIATED PRESS
Add Engadget on Google:
Preferred Source
Google Discover
YouTube 
announced
 that it will no longer share data with Billboard for the creation of the Billboard Hot 100 and other charts because the video platform doesn't believe they're calculated fairly. The issue lies, per YouTube, with how Billboard weighs on-demand streams in its rankings: The publication weighs streams from paid music streaming services over ad-supported streams, and YouTube just so happens to run an ad-supported streaming service.
Much like getting a book on 
The New York Times
 Bestseller list, landing a spot on the Billboard charts doesn't carry quite the same importance that it once did. When the majority of the media people consume is digital and on-demand, sales numbers aren't a guaranteed indicator of success or popularity. That's part of the reason why Billboard started 
including digital streams
 in its calculations way back in 2007, and why it announced plans to adjust how it weighs on-demand streaming just yesterday.
On Tuesday, 
Billboard announced
 that it would add more weight to streaming to "better reflect an increase in streaming revenue and changing consumer behaviors." Billboard currently counts an "album consumption unit" as an album sale, which either breaks down to "3,750 ad-supported or 1,250 paid/subscription on-demand official audio and video streams." In the new ranking scheme that will go into effect in January 2026, "each album consumption unit will now equal 2,500 ad-supported or 1,000 paid/subscription on-demand official audio and video streams." 
"The change means that it will take 33.3 percent fewer ad-supported on-demand streams of songs from an album, and 20 percent fewer paid/subscription on-demand streams of songs from an album, to equal an album unit," Billboard says. Put another way, things are moving in YouTube's favor, just apparently not as quickly as the company wants.
YouTube characterizes pulling its data from Billboard as fighting for fairness and equality, but it's equally connected to the platform's demonstration of its own power. It already 
funnels billions of dollars
 to artists and labels from the money it makes on ads and subscriptions, but it clearly also wants the power to mint new chart-topping stars. "We are committed to achieving equitable representation across the charts and hopefully can work with Billboard to return to theirs," YouTube says in its announcement. "Until then, if you're curious about what music is making waves on YouTube, you can visit our charts 
here
."
