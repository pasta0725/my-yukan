XBOXが誕生25周年を迎え，CEOのアーシャ・シャルマ氏が次の25年に向けた展望を語る。「数百人が必要なゲームを少人数で開発できる時代が来る」
ライター：奥谷海人
　MicrosoftのXBOX部門でCEOを務める
アーシャ・シャルマ（Asha Sharma）氏
は，2026年11月15日に迎える
XBOX誕生25周年を記念し，世界各地の従業員に向けたメッセージ
を公式サイトのXBOX Wireで公開した（
リンク
，
リンク
）。
　シャルマ氏は，1979年にリリースされたテキストアドベンチャー
「Microsoft Adventure」
から，1982年の
「Microsoft Flight Simulator」
，1990年代のDirectX開発を経て，2001年の初代XBOX発売に至るMicrosoftのゲーム事業の歩みを紹介。XBOX Liveによるオンラインサービスの普及にも触れ，現在では年間10億人以上のプレイヤーにゲームを届ける事業へと成長したことを強調した。
　2001年11月15日に北米で発売された初代XBOXは，PC向けソフトウェアで培われたMicrosoftの技術を家庭用ゲーム機に持ち込み，
「Halo: Combat Evolved」
などの人気作品とともに存在感を高めていった。
　その後もXBOX 360によるオンラインサービスの拡充，XBOX Oneでの苦戦，XBOX Series X|Sへの世代交代など，成功と失敗を繰り返しながら事業を展開し，近年ではActivision Blizzardの買収やGame Passの普及などを通じ，コンシューマ機の枠を超えたゲーム事業への転換を進めている。
　また，XBOXの25周年を記念してシアトルで開催されたFanFestでは，今後の新作タイトルやサービスに関する情報も公開された。主な発表内容は以下のとおりだ。
・XBOX Playback：
プレイヤーが初めて遊んだゲームや総プレイ時間，実績など，過去のプレイ履歴を振り返る新サービスを発表。日本は提供対象外となっている。
・「Halo: Campaign Evolved」：
協力型サバイバルモード「Firefight」を無料アップデートで追加すると発表。
・25周年記念アイテム：
「Forza Horizon 6」「Halo: Campaign Evolved」「Gears of War: E-Day」「Minecraft」「Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024」「Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4」に，記念スキンや特別カラーなどを用意。
XBOXの25周年を記念して，さまざまなゲーム内アイテムが用意されている
・「Fable」：
呪われたクリーチャーとの魔法戦闘やクエストの展開を紹介する新たなゲームプレイ映像を公開。
・「DOOM: The Dark Ages」：
敵やボスが次々と出現する新モード「Slaughter Mode」を11月16日に追加。対象DLCやプレミアム版の所有者は追加料金なしでプレイ可能。
・初代XBOX作品のPC対応：
「Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic」「The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind」など，初代XBOXの25作品がPCでプレイ可能に。一部作品には実績機能も追加された。
・「Candy Crush Saga」：
「Halo」や「Fallout」などのキャラクターや楽曲を取り入れた25周年記念イベントを開催。
・XBOX本体の機能更新：
ホーム画面の背景切り替え機能やゲームライブラリの整理機能などをXBOX Insider向けに発表。
・「Wolfenstein 3」：
MachineGamesがシリーズ最新作を正式に開発中であることを発表。B.J.ブラスコヴィッチとグレース・ウォーカーが再登場する。
・id Softwareの新作：
アーシャ・シャルマ氏は，id Softwareの次回作がすでに開発中であることを明らかにした。
　続いて
「The Next 25」
と題した今後の展望では，ゲームの表現力が飛躍的に向上した反面，開発に必要な費用や人員が増え，リスクも増大し，多くのアイデアがゲームとして実現されないまま失われていると指摘。こうした状況を変えなければならないと訴えている。
　シャルマ氏によれば，これまで創作物の規模を決めていた人員や資本，資源との関係が変わり始めており，将来的には1人の人間が，かつて国家規模の組織でなければ実現できなかった能力を手にするという。
　さらに，現在なら数百人の開発者と数億ドルを必要とするゲーム世界も，小規模なチームで構築できるようになると予測した。
　具体的な技術には言及していないものの，生成AIなどによる開発工程の効率化を念頭に置いた発言とも受け取れる。シャルマ氏は，ゲーム業界の未来は，少人数のチームによる大胆なアイデアから革新が生まれた黎明期に近づいていくとの考えを示した。
　こうした変化は，大手パブリッシャが巨額の開発費を投じるAAAタイトルだけでなく，インディー開発者にも新たな可能性をもたらすことになる。シャルマ氏は，ゲーム制作に必要な資源の制約が緩和されれば，これまで実現が難しかったアイデアにも挑戦できるようになり，ゲームの世界がさらに多様化していくとの期待を寄せている。
　また，XBOXがこれまで4世代のコンシューマ機を展開し，
第5世代の開発も進めていること
を改めて明言。優れたゲームとXBOXならではのハードウェアを提供するとともに，より多くの人々が新しいものを生み出せる環境を整えていくことが，今後の使命になると締めくくっている。

===== https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/11/12/25-years-of-xbox/
(status 200)
This message was just sent to Team XBOX employees globally.
Team, 
Microsoft was founded on the belief that computing could put extraordinary capabilities in the hands of ordinary people. Gaming has been part of that story since the company’s beginning. 
In 1979, we released 
Microsoft Adventure
, a text-based game that looked more like MS-DOS than an early version of 
Minecraft
. Adapted by a single programmer, Gordon Letwin, one of Microsoft’s first 13 employees and later an architect on OS/2, the game let players explore a digital world, collect treasure, solve puzzles, and encounter mythical creatures.
A few years later, Bruce Artwick brought the magic of flight to the 16-bit IBM PC, making it possible to fly a plane from your desk. Over the following decade, Microsoft published dozens of other titles including 
Minesweeper
, 
Age of Empires
, and another four 
Microsoft Flight Simulators
, while Windows helped usher in 3D gaming with partners such as id Software (which would join XBOX some 24 years later).  
In the mid-1990s, a small team working on DirectX, the technology that helped developers make games for Windows, had a big idea: using PC architecture to power the first American video game console in decades. Shortly thereafter, work began on the secret “Project Midway.” 
When the original XBOX launched in 2001, it was impossible to miss. It was 2-3x larger than rival consoles, with a giant X molded into a black enclosure and a bright alien green jewel at the center. It shipped with a hard drive when competitors asked players to buy standalone memory cards and an Ethernet jack when 9 in 10 households still used dial-up. It also boasted more than twice the processing power of the PlayStation and GameCube and pioneered dual analog controllers.  
A year later, Xbox Live revolutionized online play with persistent friend lists and matchmaking, as well as a common service for developers to build connected experiences. And bringing all of this together was 
Halo: Combat Evolved
, a launch title that not only defined XBOX but proved its first advertising campaign: “Games Will Never Be The Same.”

Xbox has reached its 25th anniversary, and CEO Asha Sharma used the milestone to look both backward at Microsoft's long relationship with games and forward at the increasingly difficult economics of modern blockbuster development. The anniversary message celebrated the people, hardware, and games that helped establish Xbox as a console competitor, but its most consequential section was less nostalgic.
In a portion titled 
The Next 25
, Sharma described a business where creative ambition is routinely limited by the growing cost, time, staffing, and financial risk attached to making big games. She did not explicitly use the term artificial intelligence. Even so, her description of what could change -- individual creators gaining capabilities previously associated with entire nations, and small teams making worlds currently requiring hundreds of developers and enormous budgets -- strongly suggests that AI-assisted development is central to the future she has in mind.
That prospect arrives during a difficult period for the AAA game business. Teams are larger, production cycles are longer, and the financial stakes behind major releases have climbed sharply. At the same time, consolidation, studio closures, layoffs, and a more cautious approach to greenlighting new projects have made the industry's appetite for unproven ideas appear narrower. Sharma's message frames new technology as a potential way to break that pattern rather than simply manage it.
Microsoft's gaming roots extend beyond Xbox
Sharma's retrospective begins well before the original Xbox arrived in 2001. Microsoft was involved with games decades earlier, including 
Microsoft Adventure
, a text-based MS-DOS title released in 1979. Created by Gordon Letwin, who was one of just 13 Microsoft employees at the time, the game is a reminder that interactive entertainment existed within the company's history long before a Microsoft-branded console became a reality.

Valheim comic book series

That earlier legacy also includes familiar names such as 
Minesweeper
 and 
Microsoft Flight Simulator
. The latter is especially useful to Sharma's broader argument: Flight Simulator has evolved enormously since its 1982 debut, becoming an example of how far a franchise can progress as hardware, software, data, and creative techniques advance. Microsoft's gaming identity, in this framing, is not only about the Xbox brand. It is about a much longer history of building and supporting interactive software on PC.
Of course, Xbox changed the scale of that commitment. The original console entered a fiercely competitive market and quickly built its identity around powerful hardware, online services, and major exclusives. 
Halo: Combat Evolved
 became an immediate defining game for the platform, demonstrating that Microsoft could make a serious mark in the console space rather than merely extend its PC business into a new category.
Over the years that followed, Xbox's embrace of online multiplayer, platform services, technical features, and major studio acquisitions helped it remain a leading console brand alongside PlayStation. Sharma noted that 111 people across Xbox and its game studios have now worked there for at least 25 years. That detail gives the anniversary reflection a human element: the company's gaming history has been shaped not only by products and franchises, but by employees who have remained through multiple hardware generations and shifting corporate strategies.
The AAA development problem
The celebratory tone changes noticeably when Sharma turns to current game production. Modern games can display extraordinary art, engineering, performance capture, music, writing, design, and technical craft. Yet the resources necessary to make them can be overwhelming. Larger worlds, higher visual expectations, extensive accessibility options, ongoing post-launch support, localization, cross-platform testing, and the demands of live services all add to the scope of a typical major release.
The result is a tradeoff that will sound familiar to many players and developers. Games can be more ambitious than ever, but fewer teams can afford to take large creative swings. Projects may be narrowed, delayed, or cancelled before audiences ever see them. Developers can spend years working toward a vision that is eventually reshaped by deadlines, budget pressures, or a publisher's concern that the market will not support the risk.
"Too many ideas never become games, and too many games fall short of what their creators imagined," Sharma wrote in discussing the growing burden of development.
Her position is that this should not remain an unavoidable consequence of making ambitious games. That is a notable claim coming from the leader of an organization that owns major studios and franchises, and one that has participated directly in the broader industry trend toward massive productions. Microsoft is not an outside observer of AAA development's escalating costs; it is one of the companies trying to operate at that scale.
The challenge is also larger than Xbox. Across the industry, publishers have sought growth through acquisitions, larger releases, recurring revenue, subscriptions, and multiplatform launches. These strategies can broaden a game's potential audience, but they do not automatically solve the fundamental problem of expensive development. A project with a massive budget still needs to sell well, retain players, or otherwise justify its cost. When it does not, the consequences can affect entire studios.
A future implied rather than named
Sharma's proposed future rests on a striking prediction: the connection between creative scale and the people, capital, and resources required to achieve it is beginning to weaken. She suggested that a single person could soon have access to capabilities that once demanded the resources of nations, while smaller teams could make worlds now associated with hundreds of developers and hundreds of millions of dollars.
Artificial intelligence is never directly identified in the anniversary post. But the language closely resembles the current argument made by advocates of generative AI and other AI-assisted tools. Supporters believe such technology can help with programming, prototyping, asset creation, animation, localization, testing, world-building, and other time-consuming tasks. In its most optimistic form, that vision is not necessarily about eliminating developers. It is about giving smaller teams leverage that once belonged only to the largest companies.
For an independent creator, that could mean reaching a prototype stage faster or attempting a more complex idea without securing blockbuster funding. For a mid-sized studio, it could mean spending more time refining a game's distinctive elements rather than repeatedly handling production work that tools could accelerate. For a massive publisher, it could mean pursuing projects that go beyond the technical and content scale of today's biggest releases.
Sharma specifically compared the possibility ahead to the distance between 
Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024
 and the franchise's first 1982 entry. It is an intentionally large comparison, one that presents future technology as a generational leap rather than an incremental efficiency gain.
Still, the promise is far from settled. AI's use in game development raises questions over job security, training data, intellectual property, artistic authorship, quality control, player trust, and whether purported efficiency benefits will be shared with workers or used mainly to cut costs. A game can have more generated content without becoming more memorable, coherent, or fun. Big worlds also need thoughtful design, strong direction, and a clear reason for players to explore them.
Xbox Labs offered an earlier clue
This is not the first time Sharma has pointed toward a transformative shift without using the phrase AI outright. In an October 16 post introducing the Xbox Labs experiment involving vibe-coded projects, she discussed the rapid spread of games being decompiled and brought to web browsers, as well as code being remixed in new ways.
Vibe coding generally refers to using natural-language prompts and AI tools to generate or shape software, allowing people to build projects without approaching every task through traditional programming workflows. The concept has generated excitement because it could lower barriers to experimentation. It has also produced skepticism, particularly around reliability, maintainability, security, and the tendency for quickly generated projects to lack the polish expected of commercial games.
The same divide applies to games. Easier creation tools could produce a wave of unexpected work from people previously excluded by technical barriers. They could also flood digital storefronts with shallow or derivative projects. The outcome will depend on the tools themselves, the standards platforms enforce, and the extent to which developers use automation to support genuine ideas instead of merely producing more content at a faster rate.
Major industry figures have made similar cases for AI's potential to reshape the development landscape, often arguing that small and agile teams will gain influence as older production models are disrupted. Yet the biggest companies are also well positioned to benefit. Firms with vast computing resources, established distribution platforms, huge game libraries, and the money to invest in experimental technology may have an advantage in determining how this transition unfolds.
What players may want from the next era
For Xbox fans, the anniversary message creates an interesting tension. The brand's first 25 years are remembered through hardware generations, standout games, online communities, and franchises that helped define different eras of console gaming. The next 25, at least in Sharma's vision, may be shaped as much by development technology as by any single console feature.
Players are unlikely to be satisfied by a technological promise alone. They will judge the outcome through the games that arrive: whether Xbox studios make distinctive work, whether projects receive the time and care they need, whether new tools result in better experiences, and whether the people making those experiences are treated fairly. A smaller team building a larger world is an intriguing possibility, but scale is not the same as creativity.
Sharma's anniversary reflection makes clear that Xbox sees the current development model as unsustainable in its present form. It also suggests Microsoft believes emerging tools can help change that equation. Whether that produces a more open, inventive, and sustainable game industry -- or simply a faster route to cheaper content -- remains one of the central questions facing the medium's next chapter.
