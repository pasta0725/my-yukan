The US government has expanded the use of an AI tool for advising air traffic controllers to assist in managing air traffic across the entire national airspace system. Airline executives have voiced support for the rollout, but the US labor union representing air traffic controllers has said it was not involved in designing or testing the AI-driven system.
The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) says the $875 million SMART system has helped reroute 180 flights since 
being implemented
 in airspace over Washington, DC, on September 21. The agency claims that this saved an average of 10 minutes per flight and almost 80 gallons of fuel per flight—and it is apparently hoping such results can be replicated nationwide through the expanded deployment.
“After a successful initial rollout to the National Capital Region in September, the FAA will now use SMART in the airspace of all 22 centers across the country to reduce delays, reduce fuel consumption and improve the travel experience for the flying public,” according to the agency 
statement posted
 on November 10.
The 
FAA fact sheet
 describes SMART as a cloud-based system that combines information about “weather patterns, flight paths, traffic flow and controller staffing metrics” to predict air traffic patterns. It is designed to make scheduling recommendations, identify optimal flight routes, and determine the best order of arrivals for aircraft.
However, a human team of FAA specialists based in DC is ultimately responsible for validating the SMART system recommendations before passing them along to air traffic control facilities across the country, according to the agency.
Furthermore, the AI-powered SMART system “cannot be used to replace the critical role air traffic controllers play or take over control of an aircraft,” the FAA clarified in the September 21 announcement about the initial deployment. Local air traffic controllers can decide whether or not to accept the SMART system recommendations.
The previous FAA announcement included supportive statements from Delta Air Lines CEO Ed Bastian and American Airlines CEO Robert Isom.
But the National Air Traffic Controllers Association, which represents more than 15,000 members, also 
told Flying magazine
 that it was not consulted or involved in the “design, testing, or implementation of SMART and therefore cannot speak to its effect on the air traffic control workforce.”
Following the initial deployment in airspace above DC, the FAA expanded the SMART system’s coverage to air route traffic control centers in Atlanta along with Jacksonville and Miami in Florida, according to 
The Dallas Morning News
.
The FAA also shared an example with The Dallas Morning News about how SMART recommended a shorter and faster route for a cargo flight arriving at Dallas-Fort Worth International Airport, although the newspaper noted that the agency did not identify the flight involved and has not provided information about how SMART may have assisted passenger flight traffic.
Ars has previously covered the 
SMART system’s development
 and lingering questions about how it works, as well as the air traffic controller shortage and other challenges facing the FAA.

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) said it is expanding the use of its AI-assisted traffic planning tool to airspace managed by all 22 US air route traffic control centers, even after more than two dozen lawmakers called for a pause in its Washington-area rollout.

The FAA is expanding its artificial intelligence-supported air traffic management system to all 22 air route traffic control centers nationwide. According to a 
Nov. 10 statement
, the Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories (SMART) system will be used to help reduce delays and fuel consumption following its initial deployment in the Washington, D.C., area in September.
SMART combines approximately 200 data streams, including weather conditions, flight paths, traffic volumes and controller staffing information, to identify potential congestion before it develops. FAA specialists in Washington analyze the system’s data and forward scheduling recommendations to the Air Traffic Control System Command Center in Warrenton, Virginia. The recommendations are then sent to individual air traffic control facilities, where local management determines whether to implement them. Controllers retain responsibility for aircraft separation.
FAA Reports Early Results
Since 
launching SMART
 on Sept. 21, the FAA says the system has helped reroute 180 flights, saving an average of 10 minutes and nearly 80 gallons of fuel per flight. The agency initially introduced the technology in airspace surrounding Reagan National, Washington Dulles and Baltimore/Washington International airports. SMART was developed under a broader $875 million, 12-year contract awarded to Air Space Intelligence in June.
Congressional Concerns
The nationwide expansion follows an 
Oct. 9 letter
 from more than two dozen lawmakers calling on the FAA to suspend the system’s initial rollout until questions about safety evaluations, personnel training and oversight were addressed. The lawmakers requested details about the system’s planned 90-day evaluation period and the standards required before nationwide deployment.
“Any upgrades and changes to ATC systems must be made with safety as the first priority,” the lawmakers wrote in their letter to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford. 
The FAA’s Nov. 10 announcement did not address the lawmakers’ questions about testing and training.

WASHINGTON —
 The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is expanding its Strategic Management of Airspace, Routes and Trajectories (SMART) system to airspace managed by all 22 FAA centers, extending a traffic-planning tool first used in the Washington region in September.
The agency’s 
November 10 expansion statement
 says the cloud-based platform combines 200 data streams, including weather, flight paths, traffic flows and controller staffing. The announcement describes national coverage without providing a center-by-center implementation timetable.
Since SMART began on September 21, the FAA says it has helped reroute 180 flights, saving an average of ten minutes and almost 80 US gal of fuel per flight. These are agency-reported results for the flights described, not a measured reduction in delays across the national system.
Recommendations Pass Through People
FAA specialists at the SMART center in Washington analyze and validate the data, then send scheduling recommendations to the Air Traffic Control System Command Center in Warrenton, Virginia. The Command Center passes them to individual facilities for local leadership to consider.
The FAA’s 
September launch announcement
 says facilities can accept or decline those suggestions. It also says SMART cannot take over control of an aircraft or replace controllers.
The 
program fact sheet
 describes a planning layer alongside existing systems. Its artificial intelligence analyzes schedules, airport capacity, weather and other constraints to identify conflicts before departure, updating forecasts as conditions change. Air traffic controllers retain responsibility for keeping aircraft separated.
The FAA selected Air Space Intelligence in June to supply SMART and Flow Management Data and Services (FMDS). The 
contract announcement
 describes FMDS as the Command Center’s future technology backbone, with SMART as an enhancement within it. The November expansion therefore concerns a particular planning capability, rather than completion of the broader modernization program.
What Changes for Airlines
The national scope advances the 
planned Washington-area deployment Airways reported on in September
. For airlines, the intended benefit is earlier agreement with the FAA on routes and departure times when demand, weather and available capacity conflict. Earlier warning gives planners more opportunity to change a flight before it joins a queue or encounters congestion.
Oversight questions accompanied the initial rollout. In an 
October 9 call for a pause
, Senator Chris Van Hollen and House colleagues sought information on safety testing, consultation, training and standards for expansion. Their questions concern the evaluation process; they are not a finding that SMART caused an operational failure.
The November statement does not explain how the savings were calculated, provide a comparison baseline or report how often facilities accepted recommendations. Those details would help distinguish useful individual reroutes from a sustained improvement in airline reliability. Tracking delay minutes, cancellations and recovery during comparable weather events would give airlines and passengers a clearer test of what nationwide use delivers.

FAA Issues Emergency Medical Kit Modernization Proposal
The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) today 
proposed a rule 
to modernize requirements for emergency medical kits (EMK) on commercial airplanes, ensuring crew members have up-to-date resources to handle the most common in-flight emergencies.
Under the proposed rule, the FAA would transition away from a rigid, prescriptive checklist of medical items. Instead, the agency would adopt a flexible, performance-based standard that allows airline operators to tailor kit contents and crew training to evolve with modern medical science.
Members of the public and aviation stakeholders will have 60 days to submit comments on the Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) in the Federal Register.
Based on recommendations from the Aerospace Medical Association, the rule recommends equipping EMKs with sufficient resources to manage nine life-threatening conditions:
Cardiac emergencies
Breathing difficulties
Gastrointestinal emergencies
Opioid overdoses
Childbirth
Seizures
Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reactions)
Major bleeding
Hypoglycemia
To ensure medical equipment remains aligned with healthcare advancements, the FAA would evaluate and revise EMK standards and flight-crew training guidelines every five years. This action would fulfill Section 368 of the Congressional FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024, which directed the agency to update emergency kit requirements.

FAA Statement on 5G Auction
The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is confident that the use of radio signals from the FCC's 5G auction can safely coexist with aviation after years of FAA-led testing and technical analysis. The FCC worked closely with the FAA while developing its auction Order, which contains key safeguards that protect the band of frequencies that aircraft radio altimeters use.  
These safeguards include limiting the power of the auctioned 5G signals to levels the FAA has determined are safe for aviation; protecting the altimeter band with a buffer band between it and the band of frequencies that will be auctioned; and limiting the height of 5G transmission towers. Additionally, the FCC’s Order will ensure the functionality of key altimeter-based alerting systems including Terrain Avoidance Warning Systems.
Later this summer, the FAA will issue its own rule to require aircraft altimeter upgrades to ensure altimeters can safely operate with signals from the 5G wireless signals that will be auctioned.

FAA Issues Proposed Rule to Streamline Transport-Category Airplane Certification
The FAA issued a 
proposed rule
 to make the certification of transport-category airplanes and their propulsion systems faster, simpler, and less expensive while maintaining the highest safety standards.
The proposed rule would fold many commonly used exemptions, special conditions and equivalent-level-of-safety findings into the regulations, which would reduce today’s time-consuming back-and-forth between the FAA and manufacturers over these pathways. Additionally, it would align the FAA’s certification approach more closely with the European Union Aviation Safety Agency’s standards, streamlining each regulator’s certification of the other’s products.
The proposed changes in the rule address cabin interiors, emergency exits and signs, fuel tank flammability assessments, windshield visibility, decompression standards, and protections against unsafe power lever or thrust reverser use, among other things.
The rule also would address recommendations from an Aviation Rulemaking Advisory Committee, the National Transportation Safety Board, and a provision of the 2024 FAA Reauthorization Act.
