      Latest updates: BepiColombo's arrival at Mercury
29 OCT. 2026
publication date
24144
Views
After eight years of travelling through the inner Solar System, 
BepiColombo
 is arriving at Mercury. This page will show the latest updates throughout the mission's arrival. 
BepiColombo is a world-class science mission led by the European Space Agency (
ESA
) in close collaboration with the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (
JAXA
). It will be the first mission to orbit Mercury with two spacecraft, with ESA’s Mercury Planetary Orbiter (
MPO
) and JAXA’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (
Mio
) orbiting the planet at the same time. 
The '
arrival phase
' begins with the separation of the Mercury Transfer Module (
MTM
) from the spacecraft stack on 3 September 2026. The composite spacecraft will enter orbit around Mercury on 21 November 2026, and MPO and Mio will separate from each other on 9–10 December 2026. The mission’s science phase is set to begin in April 2027. 
      29 October 2026
On 29 October, the 
BepiColombo
 Mission Control Team completed its final simulation ahead of the mission’s upcoming arrival milestones at Mercury. The rehearsal focused on the separation of the Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter’s Sunshield and Interface Structure (MOSIF) from ESA’s Mercury Planetary Orbiter (
MPO
), an operation scheduled for 16 December. It brought to a close an 
extensive simulation campaign
 that began in June 2026, preparing the teams step by step through the complex sequence of operations involved in BepiColombo’s arrival at Mercury.
Unlike simulation campaigns carried out before launch, however, these rehearsals had to take place alongside the day-to-day operation of a spacecraft already in flight. This meant carefully coordinating each simulation around ongoing mission activities, adding another layer of complexity to an already demanding campaign. And the teams were not simply rehearsing nominal operations: the simulations deliberately introduced unexpected problems and scenarios, challenging them to diagnose issues, make decisions and adapt when operations did not go according to plan.
Over the past five months, the campaign rehearsed every major step of the arrival sequence:
MTM separation
, Mercury orbit insertion, the separation of MPO and Mio, and finally MOSIF separation. Throughout, it has been a highly collaborative effort, bringing together teams from across ESA, JAXA and industry to rehearse procedures, coordinate across organisations and prepare together for the critical operations ahead.
      6 October 2026
On 15 September, all 
instruments on ESA’s MPO spacecraft
 were switched on together for the first time since the MTM separated from the spacecraft stack on 3 September. The first analyses show the instruments to be in good health.
Among these ‘first light’ tests, the laser altimeter BELA fired its laser for the first time. Over 21.5 hours, it emitted over 700 thousand pulses into open space. These tests show the BELA laser and the entire instrument to be in excellent health. The last full test was back on Earth in 2016. 
Read the University of Bern press release
On the same day, the Mercury Radiometer and Thermal Imaging Spectrometer (MERTIS) observed deep space simultaneously through its planet and calibration viewing ports. Until now – including during the 
last in-space test for this instrument
 during the mission’s fifth flight past Mercury – MTM blocked the planet port. Around 1 gigabit of data was received as a result of the 22-hour science performance test. A first analysis shows little to no loss in sensitivity in the instrument’s spectrometer since the last low-temperature ground calibration in 2013. 
Read DLR’s first-light report on MERTIS (and BELA)
MPO’s science camera SIMBIO-SYS activated its three optical channels, successfully taking its first image in space. These first tests and telemetry data confirm that the instrument is operating correctly. Over the coming weeks, the team will fine-tune some operational conditions to prepare SIMBIO-SYS for the mission’s scientific observations. Learn more on SIMBIO-SYS first light 
from the Italian Space Agency (ASI)
 and 
from the Observatoire de Paris – PSL
      28 September 2026
Since separating from MTM on 3 September, ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) must take charge of its – and the still-attached Japanese Mio science orbiter's – final approach to Mercury. On 24 September, MPO tested its axial thrusters for the first time in this new configuration.
For 8 minutes and 42 seconds, the thrusters fired as the ESA Mission Control Team at 
ESOC
 closely monitored power, temperature, pressure, acceleration and Doppler data received via ESA's 
Cebreros
 deep-space antenna in Spain.
This resulted in a precise push of 11.4 m/s, the first planned trajectory correction since MTM separation. This demonstrates the spacecraft's ability to stay on course for its next major milestone. A number of similar small trajectory correction slots are planned for precise targeting prior to the main orbit insertion sequence in November. 
      21 September 2026
The BepiColombo team held a press briefing on 9 September 2026 at the Europlanet Science Congress (
EPSC2026
) in The Hague, Netherlands. The briefing was published online last week: 
Watch the EPSC2026 press briefing
The briefing gave an update on the status of the spacecraft following MTM separation (3 September), and provided an overview of the next steps during this exciting time in the BepiColombo mission. The speakers were: Santa Martinez Sanmartin, BepiColombo Mission Manager; Ignacio Tanco, Head of ESA's Inner Solar System Missions Unit; Geraint Jones, Lead ESA BepiColombo Project Scientist; and Go Murakami, JAXA BepiColombo Project Scientist.

14:52 BST / 15:52 CEST
Acquisition of signal via two 
Estrack
 deep space antennas, in Cebreros (Spain) and Malargüe (Argentina), have confirmed the successful separation of BepiColombo's Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) from the spacecraft stack. Current telemetry indicates all systems are nominal, and MPO's solar panels are charging the spacecraft's batteries.
Behind today's success are years of preparation by teams across the BepiColombo mission. Executing a critical separation more than 200 million kilometres from Earth demands precise planning, navigation and commanding, with no opportunity for real-time intervention.
After nearly eight years in space and billions of kilometres travelled, BepiColombo's two science orbiters, ESA's MPO and JAXA's Mio, are now on their way towards orbit insertion around Mercury. MPO will power the spacecraft stack until Mio separates on 9–10 December 2026.
13:41 BST / 14:41 CEST 
We have detected the preliminary Doppler signal indicating that MTM has separated from the BepiColombo spacecraft stack. The Doppler signal is a variation in the radio frequency established between Earth and the spacecraft. When the spacecraft changes velocity, the frequency of the signal changes. It is the same principle that makes us hear a change in pitch in the siren sound when an ambulance moves towards us or away from us.
The initial Doppler signal is consistent with the planned MTM separation sequence. For full confirmation of the separation being successful, we're awaiting a signal from the MPO spacecraft at around 14:53 BST / 15:53 CEST.
13:00 BST / 14:00 CEST
BepiColombo's Mercury Transfer Module should have now separated from the spacecraft, a key step in the mission's journey towards orbiting Mercury. Now expecting acquisition of signal confirming separation no earlier than 14:53 BST / 15:53 CEST.

Update for 2:50 p.m. ET on Nov. 23: 
On Sunday afternoon (Nov. 22), the BepiColombo team announced that, while the spacecraft did successfully arrive in orbit around Mercury on Saturday (Nov. 21), the engine burn that got it there 
was cut short
 due to a software glitch. On Monday (Nov. 23), we got 
another update
: BepiColombo "is safe and in a stable orbit around Mercury" and is on track for the next big milestone: The separation of its two orbiters on Dec. 9.
For just the second time ever, Mercury has received a robotic visitor from Earth.
The 
BepiColombo
 mission — a joint effort of the 
European Space Agency
 (ESA) and the Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency (
JAXA
) — arrived in orbit around Mercury on Saturday (Nov. 21), more than eight years after leaving its home planet.
Before BepiColombo, just one spacecraft had ever circled the solar system's innermost planet: NASA's 
MESSENGER
, which did so from March 2011 to April 2015.
BepiColombo consists of two main spacecraft: ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter (MPO) and JAXA's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter (MMO), also known as Mio.
The duo launched — along with a power and propulsion element called the Mercury Transfer Module (MTM) — aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana 
in October 2018
, beginning a long and looping trip through the inner solar system.
Mercury
 is a difficult planet to reach because it's so close to 
the sun
. Spacecraft approaching the planet are accelerated to high velocities by the immense gravitational pull of our star and must therefore shed a great deal of kinetic energy to be captured by Mercury's much weaker tug.
So the BepiColombo team designed a trajectory that featured one flyby of 
Earth
, two of 
Venus
 and six of 
Mercury
 over the course of about seven years. That flight time was 
increased by a year
, however, after the mission suffered an issue with its solar panels, which reduced the power available for the MTM's electric thrusters.
BepiColombo's arrival at Mercury will take a total of about six months. 
(Image credit: European Space Agency)
Saturday's orbital insertion came after a 37-minute-long engine burn, which began at 1:24 p.m. EST (1824 GMT). There was a bit drama involved, however, as confirmation of the maneuver's success didn't come for several hours afterward.
"We've heard back from Bepi — the spacecraft is now in contact with the ground and the teams at ESOC [the European Space Operations Centre] are busy assessing the telemetry. Flight Dynamics already has confirmed it achieved orbital capture around Mercury! Early next week we'll share the next update with the upcoming steps," the mission team 
said via X
 on Saturday evening.
We've heard back from Bepi - the spacecraft is now in contact with the ground and the teams at ESOC are busy assessing the telemetry. Flight Dynamics already has confirmed it achieved orbital capture around Mercury!
Early next week we'll share the next update with the upcoming steps.
November 22, 2026
Orbital insertion is the latest milestone in BepiColombo's Mercury arrival phase, which is expected to take about six months in total.
Arrival began on Sept. 3, when the MTM released MPO and Mio. The two orbiters remain connected today; they won't separate until Dec. 9 or thereabouts.
Shortly thereafter, Mio will settle into its final orbit, a highly elliptical path that will take it over Mercury's poles. MPO is also destined for a polar orbit, though a much less elliptical one than Mio's; the ESA probe will conduct a series of engine burns before finally getting there on March 10, 2027.
BepiColombo — which is named after the late Italian scientist and mathematician Giuseppe "Bepi" Colombo — won't start doing science work until April 6, 2027. The two probes will gather complementary data from their different vantage points, shedding a great deal of light on a mysterious world.
"The mission will study all aspects of Mercury, from the structure and dynamics of its magnetosphere and how it interacts with the 
solar wind
, to its internal structure with its large iron core, and the origin of the planet’s magnetic field," ESA officials wrote in a 
BepiColombo mission description
.
"The data will enable scientists to understand more about the origin and evolution of a planet located close to its parent star, and a better understanding of the overall evolution of our solar system," they added.
