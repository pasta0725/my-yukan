===== https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/hundreds-of-artex-ai-hacking-tools-found-publicly-exposed-to-the-web/
(status 200)
Security researchers have discovered hundreds of copies of a Chinese-made AI hacking tool publicly exposed to the internet for anyone to access, potentially allowing outsiders to launch cyberattacks against unsuspecting targets.
The hacking software, called Artex, is an open source autonomous penetration testing tool, meaning software that automatically probes computer systems for weaknesses, and it’s been linked to several cyberattacks since its public release in July. As open source software, it’s free for anyone to download and run on their own servers, making it a popular tool for those who cannot access Anthropic or OpenAI’s 
most advanced cybersecurity AI models
 for discovering security vulnerabilities. Microsoft has 
previously described
 Artex as a system of AI agents that can conduct cyberattacks “from reconnaissance through data exfiltration with little needed human interaction.”
In a 
blog post
, cybersecurity firm Censys, which scans the internet for exposed or vulnerable systems, said that it identified more than 800 distinct deployments of Artex that were accessible from the web without needing a password to log in.
Silas Cutler, principal security researcher at Censys who wrote the blog post, said that not every instance of Artex is operated maliciously and many are used for authorized testers. But it’s unclear why hundreds of these Artex systems are exposed to the web, as the software does not require them to be accessible from the internet. 
The findings underscore how even powerful hacking tools can be misconfigured to be publicly accessible by anyone, increasing the chance that someone will find and misuse them. The publicly accessible systems also expose attack tools, such as implants (hidden malicious software) used to compromise and maintain persistent access to a victim’s network.
Security companies have linked the use of Artex to several broad-scale cyberattacks, including 
against the Korean finance industry in September
. Cybersecurity giant CrowdStrike attributed the attacks to a Chinese-speaking hacker who used an Artex instance in the attacks, in part because the hacker had left an “open directory” hosting the system. Censys said that its data showed the Artex instance in question had been publicly exposed for months, and appeared to contain more attack data that extended “well beyond South Korean banks,” including payment and gaming companies.
Artex was also used in an attack on IT support giant ConnectWise 
earlier in October
, which allowed Chinese-linked hackers to spy on thousands of organizations whose data and files are hosted in Microsoft’s cloud. Microsoft, which has visibility into the attack, said in a blog post that the hackers used Artex to break into customers’ cloud environments and steal their data at such speed that defenders had “little time to detect and respond.”
Many of the attacks linked to Artex involved exploiting simple vulnerabilities, such as guessing weak or known passwords, and a lack of multi-factor authentication. 
The findings show that simple vulnerabilities, including misconfigurations, can expose both the attacker and their targets.
Artex has since announced it would go closed-source, effectively limiting further releases for paid customers. There are still dozens of copies of Artex’s code available on code-sharing sites that can still be used in active attacks.

===== https://censys.com/blog/artex-agentic-asbestos/
(status 200)
Executive summary
ARTEX
 is an open-source, LLM-driven autonomous penetration testing framework released in July 2026. In October, two threat intelligence companies attributed its use as part of threat actor operations; CrowdStrike linked it to a campaign against South Korean financial institutions, and Microsoft linked it to Storm-0940’s compromise of ConnectWise customer tenants.
As of November 4, 2026, Censys observed 
1,465 hosts
 running an exposed ARTEX console. About 650 sit in two blocks of identically configured hosting, which leaves roughly 
815
 distinct-looking deployments. The ARTEX console does not require public-facing access, and overall global deployment is likely higher.
155 ARTEX hosts (10.6%)
 also run at least one other threat that Censys detects. These include other AI attack frameworks, recon platforms, tunneling tools, and C2: Vshell, Cobalt Strike, and Adaptix C2.
Censys saw open directories on the ARTEX server named in CrowdStrike’s report starting 
August 18, 2026
, seven weeks before that report was published. The directories exposed exploit binaries and a 
.claude
 folder.
That mistake is common: 
32 open directories
 run on other ARTEX hosts today, exposing post-exploitation kits, stagers, and implants.
What is ARTEX?
ARTEX was published on GitHub as 
Autumn-27/ARTEX
 on July 26, 2026, under the AGPL-3.0 license. Its developer, 
Autumn-27
, is also the author of 
ScopeSentry
, a widely used Chinese asset-discovery and vulnerability-scanning platform with more than 2,000 GitHub stars. ARTEX is best understood as an AI agent layer on top of that recon foundation. It syncs assets found by ScopeSentry’s domains, subdomains, IPs, ports, and endpoints straight into its own asset graph.
Figure 1: Web UI of ARTEX
Following public release, the project grew quickly, reaching an estimated 1,500-2,000 stars within ten weeks of release. On October 8, the day after CrowdStrike released a report detailing observed malicious usage, the author (
Autumn-27
) posted a statement on their GitHub profile, claiming they were not involved in the attacks and that ARTEX was meant for testing within authorized asset scopes. 
Autumn-27
 then stated that the project would go closed-source with no further releases. The repository has since been taken down, but archived copies, translations, and rebrands such as ScopeWeaver are still circulating.
ARTEX is written in Go and uses a Next.js web console, backed by PostgreSQL. ARTEX usage starts with users defining 
Tasks
, which outline the scope, test accounts and goal for agents – the example shown by default is “
What to achieve, e.g., gain admin panel access or server privileges
”. Running tasks leverages multiple sub-agents for completing the goal.
A 
Planner
 agent turns the goal into attack “intents”. 
Worker
 agents claim those intents, run tools (e.g., nmap, which ships in the Docker image), and write results back. An event-driven engine repeats that explore-decide-execute loop until the goal is met. ARTEX tracks two graphs: an 
asset graph
 of what the target looks like, and an 
exploration graph
 of how much of it has been tested. It also includes 
mitmproxy
 for traffic recording, and it works with Anthropic, OpenAI, or any OpenAI-compatible model endpoint. 
Microsoft describes
 it as able to run “from reconnaissance through data exfiltration” with minimal human input.

ARTEX in two campaigns
Not every ARTEX instance is operated with malicious intent, and some of the hosts publicly exposed are likely operated by authorized testers. As with all offensive security tools, there exists the potential for malicious usage. Two notable reports were released in October 2026 that provided context into observed malicious usage. 
South Korean finance (CrowdStrike, October 7)
On 7 October 2026, CrowdStrike 
reported
 that an unattributed actor using ARTEX targeted a South Korean finance company from late September to early October. The actor was  assessed with moderate confidence to be a financially motivated Chinese speaker. The actor breached a broker-facing loan inquiry service at one bank and an employee mobile system at another, and exfiltrated data. ARTEX ran on 
38.244.50[.]120
 with DeepSeek v4.1-flash as its main model, which the actor likely bought through an API reseller. The actor also ran Claude Code sessions backed by GLM and Grok. CrowdStrike found the activity because the actor left open directories exposing a 
.claude
 folder, prompts, session histories, and ARTEX configuration files.
CrowdStrike’s report identified an open directory exposed at 
38.244.50[.]120:18899
. Censys scan history for that host shows how long it had been exposed:

The file names show the actor working on targets well beyond South Korean banks, including payment and gaming services. That fits CrowdStrike’s observation of broader activity tied to the same persona.

Storm-0940 and ConnectWise (Microsoft, October 21)
Microsoft 
attributed
 the ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain compromise to Storm-0940, a China state-aligned actor active since 2021. The group used ARTEX to automate scanning, password spraying, and OAuth application abuse. It attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 tenants, peaking at 2,900 attempts per minute, and some victims saw privileged cloud access in under three hours. Microsoft also notes it has seen multiple actors across regions using ARTEX.

LLM access is part of the tech stack.
 43 ARTEX hosts also run self-hosted LLM gateway or reseller software, mainly CLIProxyAPI (19) and New API (18). The actor in CrowdStrike’s report bought DeepSeek access through a reseller. For defenders, unfamiliar LLM proxies and resellers in egress logs are worth investigating.
Hunting ARTEX with Censys
Console:
 the query above, or the ARTEX / ScopeWeaver threat labels in the Censys Platform.
Default port:
 8787/tcp, with 8790 and 443 common.
Pivots:
 look for co-located open directories, Vshell or FRP listeners, and LLM proxy software (CLIProxyAPI, New API) on the same host.
History:
 check scan history for mitmproxy banners and short-lived Python 
SimpleHTTP
 servers on high ports. They often appear before the console does.
