Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims he has proved the Riemann hypothesis, one of mathematics’s most notoriously difficult problems, with the help of Claude, cracking a puzzle that has eluded some of the world’s best mathematicians for decades. If verified, it would mark yet another seismic achievement for AI in mathematics and add to the 
upheaval already unleashed by OpenAI’s previous, often inelegant forays
 into the field.
Alpöge’s solution has yet to be verified independently. Anthropic has not commented or involved itself in Alpöge’s announcement. In 
a document on his website
, Alpöge says he texted Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei about the solution “late Sunday night” and suggests he held off publication until after the 
company’s IPO on Thursday
.
The Riemann hypothesis is one of seven Millennium Prize problems, which are widely considered some of the most formidable open challenges in mathematics. First proposed in 1859, 
the Riemann hypothesis
 concerns the 
distribution of prime numbers
 and how zeroes of a function are involved. It’s regarded as one of the most important unsolved problems in mathematics.
A statement on Alpöge’s website says Claude was integral to developing the proof:
I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof
1
 of the Riemann Hypothesis. In fact, the method easily extends much further, resolving the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, and I expect, with more time, the Extended Riemann Hypothesis too. Claude and I have also formalized the arguments without assumptions. It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.
The Verge
 reached out to Anthropic but didn’t immediately hear back. Anthropic said it was working on 
the Riemann Hypothesis with Claude in August
, but noted at the time: “We don’t expect that the techniques Claude used will lead to proving the Riemann hypothesis.”
Alpöge’s solution, presented in Lean, has only been published on his website and has not yet been publicly accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute, which administers the Millennium Prize and offers a $1 million bounty for each problem solved. Under the institute’s 
rules
, a proposed solution must be published and withstand at least two years of scrutiny before it can be considered for the prize, during which time it must have “received general acceptance in the global mathematics community.”

The AI takeover of mathematics has begun
In the quarter-century since the prize was established, only one problem has been officially solved: The 
Poincaré conjecture
, a topological problem concerning three-dimensional spheres. In September, OpenAI 
claimed
 to have cracked another, the Navier-Stokes problem, which concerns the flow of fluids.
OpenAI said it pursued Navier-Stokes after hearing rumors that rival researchers were closing in on a solution. 
Alpöge had been working on the problem
 with New York University mathematics professor Tristan Buckmaster. Buckmaster claimed OpenAI produced a solution for Navier-Stokes using the route he and Alpöge were working on with OpenAI’s Codex and Claude.
OpenAI spent millions of dollars and deployed 10,000 agents in the attempt. The result is widely considered credible, though it has yet to receive formal recognition. But the achievement has been 
marred
 by 
allegations
 of questionable research practices, including claims it used unpublished work from mathematicians who had shared their research with its AI tools. OpenAI has strongly denied wrongdoing, but this has done little to quell the uproar.
Though most of the spotlight has been on OpenAI’s movement in math, Alpöge 
said in July that he solved
 the Jacobean conjecture using Claude. The deluge of findings has left mathematicians scrambling to digest the material and 
figure out where they belong
 in what is rapidly becoming a profoundly different mathematical landscape.

In the same week that Anthropic was floated on the Nasdaq in 
its initial public offering
, a mathematician employed by the company claims to have discovered proof behind one of the world's most famous math problem, the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis. Levent Alpöge wrote in a 
paper
 this week, "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis."
As you're no doubt already aware, and as Alpöge explains, the Riemann Hypothesis is 
the 
classic problem in number theory and one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics. Proposed in 1859 by Bernhard Riemann, who gave the problem its name, it is the theory that prime numbers are not in fact random but determined. "The Riemann hypothesis asserts that all interesting solutions of the equation ζ(s) = 0 lie on a certain vertical straight line," the Clay Mathematics Institute explains. "This has been checked for the first 10,000,000,000,000 solutions. A proof that it is true for every interesting solution would shed light on many of the mysteries surrounding the distribution of prime numbers."
As 
Scientific American
notes
, the problem is the "North Star for the entire field of number theory, and the most famous open problem in math." Furthermore, the problem was previously thought so insurmountable that experts didn't even bother working on it.

Alpöge not only believes that he has used Claude to discover a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, but also believes the method could easily extend to the so-called Generalized Riemann Hypothesis and the Extended Riemann Hypothesis, too. "An astounding amount of mathematics is present in the argument, far more than I had imagined would be necessary as a student," he writes.
This would not be the first time that AI has made a significant discovery or finding in a major STEM field. Earlier this year, 
OpenAI helped create a breakthrough solution for the Navier-Stokes problem, a finding somewhat overshadowed by a plagiarism controversy
.
Alpöge's statement is not without reservation. Aside from confirming whether Claude has actually helped crack the Riemann Hypothesis, which will take some time, he admits sadness at the finding. "It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater."
"In a time of revolution in mathematics, I hope the argument provides a glimpse towards the miracles that lie ahead," Alpöge adds.

Has the 
Riemann hypothesis
 finally been solved? 
Not officially—at least not yet.
On Friday, November 20, 2026, 
Levent Alpöge
, a mathematician on the technical staff at artificial intelligence company 
Anthropic
, 
posted a statement on his personal website
 claiming that 
he and Anthropic’s AI model, Claude, have found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis
—a bombshell claim first reported by 
Scientific American
.
"I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,"
 Alpöge wrote in a four-page note accompanying the release.

GRH is the 2nd of 6 Millenium prizes solved by AI, after Navier-Stokes. This may be the biggest AI math result of all time.

If independently verified,
 the breakthrough would settle a 167-year-old conjecture widely considered the most famous unsolved problem in pure mathematics
—and mark a watershed moment in the rapid rise of AI-assisted scientific discovery. For now, however, outside mathematicians have not yet confirmed that the proof holds.
What Levent Alpöge and Claude are claiming
Alpöge’s announcement goes even further than Bernhard Riemann’s original 1859 conjecture
. According to his note and the accompanying formal files, the proposed proof covers the 
Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH)
—a massive expansion of the original problem that applies to related mathematical objects known as 
Dirichlet L-functions
.
A massive formal archive: 
Instead of a conventional journal paper, Alpöge linked to 14 archive parts totaling roughly 239 megabytes and 175,187 source files written in Lean, a computer proof assistant that verifies mathematical logic step by step.
The proposed method:
 In his four-page overview, Alpöge said he steered the research while crediting Claude with rapid late-stage progress, pointing to a strategy built around weighted prime sums (von Mangoldt sums) and chains of mathematical constructions called theta lifts.
An emotional reflection:
 Recognizing the weight of the moment for human mathematicians, Alpöge struck an elegiac tone. "It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light," he wrote. "But what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater."
Why mathematicians aren’t calling it solved yet
Releasing a proof in Lean is 
a far stricter standard than publishing an unverified AI-generated essay
, because Lean’s software kernel checks whether every logical step follows from established definitions. Even so, independent confirmation takes time.
Within hours of Friday's release, 
an independent verification project
 confirmed that the downloaded archive matched Alpöge’s mirror and that the top-level theorem statements compiled cleanly against Lean’s standard mathematics library (Mathlib). However, the full automated check replaying the entire proof was still running.
As mathematician and author Kit Yates and other experts noted, reviewers still need to confirm three critical things before calling the problem solved:
Exact target: 
That the theorem proved in the Lean code is genuinely the standard Riemann hypothesis and not a subtly altered version.
Faithful definitions: 
That all mathematical objects inside the code accurately reflect standard definitions.
No hidden assumptions:
 That no unproven shortcuts or unintended axioms were slipped into the argument.
What is the Riemann hypothesis—and why does it matter?
Proposed by German mathematician 
Bernhard Riemann
 in 1859,
 the Riemann hypothesis is a deep conjecture about the hidden order behind prime numbers
 (numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, like 2, 3, 5, 7, and 11).
The Riemann Hypothesis, Explained
While prime numbers appear scattered unpredictably along the number line,
 Riemann tied their distribution to the behavior of a complex function 
now called the 
Riemann zeta function
:
Trivial vs. non-trivial zeros:
 Mathematicians study the inputs where the zeta function outputs zero. "Trivial" zeros occur predictably at negative even integers (-2, -4, -6, and so on), while infinitely many "non-trivial" zeros lie inside a narrow vertical strip between real parts 0 and 1.
The critical line:
 Riemann conjectured that every single non-trivial zero sits directly down the middle of that strip, on a vertical line where the real part is exactly 1/2.
Why numerical checks aren't enough: 
Computers have already verified that the first 10 trillion non-trivial zeros lie squarely on that critical line. Yet a single exception anywhere beyond that range would disprove the hypothesis—making a universal proof essential.
What a proof unlocks:
 Hundreds of major theorems across number theory and cryptography have been written under the assumption that the Riemann hypothesis is true. A verified proof would instantly elevate all of those conditional results into established mathematical facts.
Will Alpöge win the $1 million Millennium Prize?
Not anytime soon. The 
Riemann hypothesis
 is one of the 
Clay Mathematics Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems
, each carrying a $1 million award.
Under Clay’s official rules,
 a personal website post and a code archive do not qualify for the prize
. A proposed solution must
 first be published in a qualifying peer-reviewed outlet, survive at least two years after publication, and achieve general acceptance across the global mathematics community
 before the institute will consider awarding the money. As of Friday, Clay’s official page still listed the problem as unsolved.
Who is Levent Alpöge? Inside the AI math race
Alpöge is a well-credentialed number theorist
 who studied at 
Harvard
 and 
Cambridge
, earned his PhD at Princeton in 2020 under Fields Medalist Manjul Bhargava, and served as a Junior Fellow at Harvard’s Society of Fellows before joining Anthropic.
Levent Alpöge announced on November 20, 2026, that he and Anthropic’s AI model Claude had found a proof of the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis. Photo: @__alpoge__(X)
Friday’s claim—arriving just one day after Anthropic’s stock debuted on public markets—is the latest salvo in an escalating mathematical race between top AI labs:
July 2026:
 Alpöge drew headlines after posting a Claude-generated solution to the famous Jacobian conjecture.
August 2026: 
Anthropic reported an earlier advance showing that at least 67.25% of the zeta function's non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line and are simple (up from roughly 41%).
October 2026: 
Rival lab OpenAI released a massive dump of 722 AI-generated math manuscripts—including a claimed proof of a weaker "quasi-Riemann hypothesis"—drawing both intrigue and skepticism from mathematicians demanding human verification.
Navier–Stokes claim:
 On the same day as the Riemann announcement, Alpöge and mathematician Tristan Buckmaster also posted a separate 79-page Claude-assisted paper claiming a breakthrough on another Millennium Prize Problem—the unforced Navier–Stokes equations—though its supporting numerical certificate has not yet been publicly released.
