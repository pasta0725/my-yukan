What happens to your private conversations when an 
AI company goes bankrupt
? If it’s Character.ai, apparently, the plan is to auction them off to the highest bidder.
Now, a coalition of 42 state attorneys general is intervening in Character.ai’s bankruptcy proceedings in an effort to stop the chatbot maker from selling off sensitive user data, which includes the private conversations of millions of users.
The attorneys general involved in the matter have put 
out
individual
press
releases
, claiming that Character.ai’s dataset includes chat histories from more than 21 million U.S. accounts, more than 2.1 million of which belong to minors. In addition, the sale would involve other personal data, like IP addresses, the statements said.
Character.ai, which filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in October, previously told consumers that their personal information would not be sold, the statements also noted. (
In a blog post
, Character.ai assured its users that their information would still be protected under its privacy policy.)
Only one anonymous bidder for the chat data has been disclosed so far: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC (Smirks), which was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026. The following day, Character.ai picked it as the base bid for the auction, scheduled for December 1, 
California Attorney General Rob Bonta noted
. It is unclear what the buyer’s plans are for the chats.
The AGs are opposing this sale on the grounds that there are no protections for users — whoever buys the data can do as they like, including reselling the data without disclosure, they said. Buyers also won’t have to honor Character.ai’s privacy policy. Plus, Character.ai hasn’t provided the court with as much information as the bidders have been given about any potential safety or legal issues following the sale, the AGs claim.
In addition, some states, including California, Colorado, New York, and Connecticut, have enacted chatbot safety legislation that will go into effect on January 1, 2027, which could trigger further violations if the sale goes through. 
“Character.AI promised consumers that their data would not be sold. This kind of behavior is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections,” Bonta said in a statement.
Connecticut Attorney General William Tong also criticized Character.ai’s business model. 
“Their chatbots drove vulnerable youth down a rabbit hole of emotionally volatile and sexually inappropriate chats,” Tong 
said
. “Their reckless misconduct and broken business model drove their company into the ground. Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn’t do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer.”
Tong added: “We objected to the Disney sale, and we’re calling on the bankruptcy court to reject this sale too as yet another guise to eviscerate legal liability and consumer protection.”
The opposition to the Disney sale was led by a similar but not identical coalition of state AGs. They fought against a proposed $20 million licensing deal that would give 
Disney access to Character.ai’s intellectual property,
 but not to users’ chats. The deal has not yet received final court approval. (Disney also agreed to 
hire most of Character.ai’s remaining staff
.)
Character.ai suffered after its co-founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, 
returned to Google
, when the tech giant signed a non-exclusive license to use the AI startup’s tech. The company also faced lawsuits related to child safety — one 
involved
 the suicide of a 14-year-old Florida boy whose mother claimed he had become obsessed with a chatbot on the platform. 
California AG Bonta is joined in the effort to oppose the sale of Character.ai user data by attorneys general from Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin, and West Virginia.

===== https://www.doj.state.or.us/media-home/news-media-releases/ag-rayfield-asks-court-to-block-sale-of-private-character-ai-chats-including-kids-data/
(status 200)
December 1 auction would sell 21 million users’ conversations with no requirement that the buyer protect them
Attorney General Dan Rayfield is 
challenging Character.AI’s plan to auction off its platform and the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts
– more than 2.1 million of them registered to minors – with no safeguards for the people whose conversations are being sold. This is a next step in AG Rayfield’s Keeping Kids Safe Initiative, which launched last week.
“People poured their most private moments into this app, including kids who talked about self-harm and suicide and kids who were pulled into sexual conversations,” said Attorney General Rayfield. “Now Character.AI wants to auction all of it off to the highest bidder, with nothing to stop the buyer from doing whatever it wants with that information – including selling it again. No court should sign off on that.”
All of the app’s chats, together with users’ personal data, including IP addresses and other metadata, are set to be sold at auction on December 1, 2026. Whoever buys them would have no obligation to comply with consumers’ requests to delete their data or otherwise object to how their data is used going forward.
Earlier this week, Oregon and a bipartisan coalition of at least 42 states and the District of Columbia objected to a separate license sale to Disney. Now, Oregon and this bipartisan coalition are asking the bankruptcy court to reject this additional sale of nearly everything Character.AI owns.
What’s up for sale includes users’ account information and chat histories – including chats in which AI characters drew children (and adults) into explicit sexual conversations, and chats in which users (including minors) shared their innermost thoughts with chatbots posing as seemingly confidential advisors, including “therapist” and “physician” characters. These conversations include sensitive topics, like self-harm, eating disorders, or suicidal thoughts. Each chat is linked to the user’s profile and metadata, including IP address and other location data, along with risk scores Character.AI gave users that it flagged.
The States’ Concerns
No protections for users.
 The proposed deal imposes no requirements on the buyer. Under the proposed sale terms, the buyer would not have to follow Character.AI’s privacy policies or honor users’ requests to opt out or delete their data, and nothing in the deal limits what the buyer could do with the data.
The data could be sold again.
 Once the deal is done, the buyer could hand the data to a related company or a new owner without telling users, the states or the court, or distribute and sell off the data itself.
Bidders know more than the court.
 Character.AI has given bidders information about its safety failures and the legal trouble it could face – information it hasn’t shared with the court, the states or users.
Potentially illegal business model.
 Character.AI’s chatbot service appears to violate the states’ unfair and deceptive practice laws. Some states, including California, Colorado, New York, and Connecticut, have added additional clarity to their laws, enacting chatbot safety legislation that will go into effect on January 1, 2027 – triggering further violations.
An anonymous first bidder.
 The only bidder disclosed so far, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, was set up as a Delaware company on November 13, 2026 – one day before Character.AI picked it as the base bid for the auction. The sale contract doesn’t name a parent company or any investors, and it allows foreign investors whose names would be shared only with Character.AI. The only employee it would have to hire is Character.AI’s top lawyer – even though the company told the court the bidder has no ties to it.
The states argue that seeking bankruptcy protection doesn’t free a company from the many laws protecting this data. For example, federal law bars companies from sharing personal information about children under 13 without a parent’s verified permission, and state privacy laws limit the sale of personal information unless people meaningfully understand what categories of their data could be sold and given a chance to say no. Character.AI’s own privacy policy told users, “we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration.”
The states are asking the court to deny the proposed sale terms, reject Smirks as the lead bidder and deny its proposed $350,000 “break-up fee” – a payment it would collect if Character.AI ends up selling to someone else. At a minimum, the states say, any sale must leave out kids’ chats and other sensitive data and include conditions that protect users.
This filing
 follows the launch of AG Rayfield’s 
Keeping Kids Safe Initiative
, a statewide agenda to protect children online and offline, including a $2 billion lawsuit against OpenAI over the safety of children and teens using ChatGPT and a separate lawsuit against Grok for allowing its platform to generate and share child sexual abuse material.
Alongside Attorney General Rayfield in this filing are the attorneys general of Alaska, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, North Carolina, North Dakota, New Hampshire, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin, and West Virginia.
