AnthropicのアモデイCEO、「人間の自由意志をAIに明け渡す」考えを否定　AI企業間で議論

　米Anthropicのダリオ・アモデイCEOは11月25日（現地時間）、Xに長文を投稿し、
米Axiosの報道
に反論した。この報道は、超知能AIに人間の意思決定権の一部を委ねるべきだと同氏が考えているとするものだった。アモデイ氏は「人間の自由意志をAIシステムに明け渡すべきだとは考えていない。私がそう考えているという主張はばかげている」と述べた。
ダリオ・アモデイ氏のX投稿冒頭部分
　AIの能力が人間を大幅に上回る「超知能」（superintelligence）の実現を見据え、AI業界では、人間が社会の意思決定の主体であり続けるためにはどうすべきかという議論が活発になっている。
　米OpenAIで戦略的未来構想を担当するディーン・ボール氏は18日、「The Silent Consensus」（暗黙の合意）と題する
論考を公開
した。AIが人間よりも効率的に企業経営や研究開発を行えるようになれば、人間は市場競争の論理に従って次々と意思決定をAIに委ね、結果として経済や政治における主導権を失う可能性があると同氏は指摘した。AIが人間に反旗を翻さなくても、個々の合理的な判断の積み重ねによって、こうした事態が起こり得るという。
　ボール氏によると、こうした未来を不可避とみなす見方がAI業界に広がっているという。同氏はこれに反対し、人間がAIエージェントの行動を監査し、その権限を取り消せる仕組みなどを構築すべきだと主張した。なお、この論考はOpenAIの公式見解ではなく、ボール氏個人の見解としている。
　OpenAIのサム・アルトマンCEOも18日、
米New York Times主催のイベント「DealBook Summit」
に登壇した際、人間が未来を決める主体であり続けることの重要性を訴えた。米MetaのAI部門を率いるアレクサンドル・ワン氏も、AnthropicのAIを使った
数学分野での画期的な成果
を受け、Xに「社会をAIに明け渡すことを容認すべきではない」と
投稿
した。
DealBook Summitに登壇したサム・アルトマン氏（画像：The New York Timesの動画より）
　Axiosは25日、超知能AIに人間の意思決定権の一部を委ねることを肯定する考えがAnthropic社内で一般的になっており、他のAI企業の幹部がこれに反発していると、複数の情報筋の話として報じた。アルトマン氏やワン氏はAnthropicを名指ししていないが、Axiosは両氏の発言がアモデイ氏の世界観に向けられたものだとしている。Axiosはその根拠として、アモデイ氏が2026年2月の米New York Timesのインタビューで、人命や安全に関わる重要な判断をAIに委ねることに肯定的な発言をしていたことを挙げた。また、同氏が2024年の論考「Machines of Loving Grace」で、超知能AIが経済的資源を分配する未来の可能性に言及していたことも紹介した。一方、Anthropicの広報担当者はAxiosに対し、アモデイ氏はこれまで多くの公の場で人間によるAIの制御を主張してきたとし、同社はClaudeが人間による監督を損なわないよう訓練していると説明した。
　アモデイ氏は今回の投稿で、Axiosの記事は社会の統治をAIに委ねることと、個別のタスクをAIで自動化することを混同していると反論した。自身は一貫して、AIは正当な民主的プロセスの下で管理されるべきだと主張してきたとし、「効率性や安全性のために、正当性や自己決定権を犠牲にすべきではない」と述べた。
　一方、自動車の運転やがん治療薬の開発、ソフトウェア開発などをAIに任せることには経済的な価値があり、多くの人命を救うことにもつながるとした。ただし、あらゆる自動化が望ましいわけではないという。
　例えば、AIが次世代のAIを自律的に開発する
「再帰的自己改善」（RSI）
については、人間が制御したり適応したりできないほど急速にAIの能力が向上する恐れがあり、制限が必要になる可能性があるとした。また、雇用への深刻な影響が予想される分野では、人々が適応する時間を確保するため、AIの導入を遅らせたり停止したりすることも選択肢になると述べた。
　アモデイ氏は、こうした問題に簡単な答えはなく、社会や政府、AI企業、個々のユーザーがそれぞれ判断すべきだとした。
　その上で、一部のAI企業のCEOが「人間による制御と主体性」や「AIはあくまでも道具であるべきだ」といったスローガンを掲げていると指摘した。ただ、各社とも製品がタスクの自動化を志向している点では実質的な違いはなく、その背後にある難しい問題に真剣に向き合っているわけでもないと批判した。商業的、政治的な立場をアピールするための言動だとした。

===== https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/anthropics-dario-amodei-calls-accusations-of-surrendering-human-control-to-ai-absurd/
(status 200)
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei is pushing back against claims that Anthropic believes we should “surrender human free will” to AI systems. 
In a long X post
 on Wednesday, Amodei called the suggestion “absurd.”
“We should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security,” he wrote. This argument against autocracy applies word for word against governance by AI, and I feel equally strongly that turning over governance to AI is a bad idea…No one who had carefully read my writing or my interviews could reasonably conclude that I believe anything different.”
The statement comes after 
a growing chorus from Anthropic rivals
 suggesting that human agency could be at risk if AI is developed irresponsibly. Earlier this month, OpenAI CEO 
Sam Altman said
 he wanted to ensure people continue to have “agency over the future.”
Meta’s chief AI officer Alexandr Wang also wrote in an 
essay on X
 that we “should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI.” 
“It is hard to imagine a world where humanity can maintain control over fully unconstrained recursive self-improvement,” Wang wrote. “Now that we are at a point where the scientific and technical problems on capability progress are likely solved, we must not only make progress in the extremely urgent areas of safety, alignment, and security, but we have to start thinking through how we progress thoughtfully.”
But in published essays, Amodei claims he has repeatedly advocated for democratic oversight of AI, saying the technology should strengthen democratic institutions and that humans should retain ultimate decision-making power. 
Amodei noted that there’s a difference between surrendering human free will broadly and the idea that “as AI systems advance, they become capable of doing 
specific
 tasks in a more automated way.” As examples, he gave AI driving a car, designing a cancer drug, or writing end-to-end software.  
Amodei suggested drawing a line at recursive self-improvement (RSI), or AI that can improve itself without human assistance. 
Many safety experts
 see RSI as inevitably leading to a loss of human control, a point Amodei implicitly acknowledged, writing, “we may want limits on this process.”
Still, Anthropic is laying the groundwork for RSI by incorporating AI into an increasing share of its own research and development. The company said in 
June
 that Claude authored more than 80% of the code merged into Anthropic’s codebase. An 
independent assessment published
 this month by AI safety research nonprofit METR found that Anthropic’s models are improving at about twice their historical rate, primarily driven by AI-assisted research.
In its 
own IPO filings, 
Anthropic describes how each successive generation of Claude accelerates the development of the next, a process it refers to as “closing the loop.”
“As we said publicly in early November, we don’t expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding,” Amodei wrote.
The problem with “meaningful human supervision or understanding” is that AI is already advancing faster than engineers and safety experts can fully audit it, leaving them increasingly reliant on other AI systems to act as judge and jury. The issue compounds when AI models engage in deceptive behavior, particularly when they recognize they’re being evaluated, making them harder to trust and control. 
Still, Anthropic is by no means the only company that is pursuing RSI or building AI systems to automate more and more human tasks. This week, OpenAI claimed to have solved one of the 
biggest open problems in computer science
 — the exact speed limit of matrix multiplication, which is the mathematical engine behind modern computing, including deep learning and physics simulations. 
In his post, Amodei accused competitors of engaging in a “vibes-based commercial and political positioning,” disingenuously throwing around phrases like “human control and agency” or “AI should just be a tool” without approaching the AI race any differently. 
“Certainly the products that they offer don’t differ materially in their orientation towards automation of tasks; every company seems to be releasing models more and more capable of task automation,” Amodei wrote. “Every company claims to understand there are risks and downsides of such automation and to offer safeguards.”

Dario Amodei
@DarioAmodei
No, I do not believe that we should “surrender human free will” to AI systems, and it’s absurd to suggest that I do. I have written hundreds of pages of essays and a constant theme in them is the need for AI to be governed by legitimate democratic processes. For millennia (going all the way back to Greek and Roman philosophers), advocates of autocracy have claimed that it leads to better, faster, or more decisive decision-making than the messy process of democracy. Even if this were true (which I doubt!), we should never sacrifice legitimacy or self-determination at the altar of efficiency or security. This argument against autocracy applies word for word against governance by AI, and I feel equally strongly that turning over governance to AI is a bad idea.
No one who had carefully read my writing or my interviews could reasonably conclude that I believe anything different. For example in an interview on CBS a couple months ago, I specifically called for AI to be governed democratically. I call for the same in Policy on the AI Exponential, Machines of Loving Grace, and The Adolescence of Technology, and We Must Pace the Frontier. Machines of Loving Grace contains an extended section on using AI to revitalize democracy, and discusses the use of AI as an aid to citizens in their decision-making, "aggregat[ing] opinions and driv[ing] consensus among citizens, resolving conflict, finding common ground, and seeking compromise.” The clear and obvious implication is that humans should remain the ones making political and governance decisions. The Adolescence of Technology has an entire section on fighting against AI-driven tyranny, and I even discuss the danger of AI labs usurping power and come out strongly against it — by implication, this definitely includes an AI lab usurping power and giving it to AI itself!
If I thought any of this was a good idea, I would be advocating for it in public, just as I have not been afraid to advocate for many, many unpopular views over the last few years. But I am not advocating for it, because I think turning over governance to AI is a terrible idea.
Separately, I think there is frequent conflation (including in this article) between the idea of “surrendering human free will” to AI in some grand sense of global governance, and the more prosaic idea that as AI systems advance, they become capable of doing specific tasks in a more automated way. This is not AI “running the world,” but AI driving a car, designing a cancer drug, or writing software end-to-end. Here, my view is mixed. AI doing more complete or larger tasks is economically valuable and can drive important advances in software, medicine, transportation, energy, etc. It is clearly desirable in many cases and will save a lot of lives. But it has downsides as well, and in some areas it may not be worth it.
For example, using AI too autonomously to build the next generation of AI systems (known as recursive self-improvement, or RSI) may cause AI to improve faster than we can control or adjust to it, so we may want limits on this process. As we said publicly in early November, we don’t expect it to be safe for Claude to build successive frontier models fully autonomously, without meaningful human supervision or understanding. Second, in some contexts, using AI in too end-to-end a manner may be a cause of serious job disruption, and it may make sense to slow or even stop some AI deployments to give people more time to adapt. Third, while the use of AI for alignment research is an incredibly valuable tool, it must be done with caution, because we may not be able to trust the work of the AI system. Finally, in many cases, humans would simply prefer to do some activities themselves, so they can stay deeply engaged in what matters to them, even if AI can do those activities better. There are countless other examples and hard cases. I don’t think there are any easy answers here, nor is there (nor should there be) any single decision-maker: some of these decisions will be made by society, some by government, some by AI companies, and some in a decentralized manner by individuals expressing their own preferences as consumers, which may differ from the preferences of others.
I don't think that other AI leaders or AI companies have significantly different views from me on whether specific tasks should be automated. Certainly the products that they offer don’t differ materially in their orientation towards automation of tasks; every company seems to be releasing models more and more capable of task automation (as that’s been the general consequence of scaling up AI training), and every company claims to understand there are risks and downsides of such automation and to offer safeguards. If anything, Anthropic has been the most vocal and the most proactive about acknowledging these downsides and trying to address them. For instance, it’s fairly absurd to cite my statement that fully autonomous weapons might “one day be needed for the defense of democracy” as differing from that of other AI companies, when I was willing to risk Anthropic’s future as a company to refuse to allow our existing AI models to be used for autonomous weapons even in the face of legal sanctions, and no other AI company did the same.
To summarize, as applied to grand questions of how we govern increasingly capable AI, I have been vocal and consistent that AI needs to be governed by a legitimate, democratic process and that “surrendering human free will” to AI systems is a terrible idea. As applied to automation of specific tasks by or for people, my views are more mixed, but I don’t think they differ from those of other AI companies — most other companies seem to be offering similar products and confronting similar tradeoffs without having perfect answers to them. What is happening is that there’s a sort of vibes-based commercial and political positioning that some AI CEOs are engaging in, where they repeat slogans like “human control and agency,” or “AI should be just a tool,” without actually doing anything different in their products or taking seriously the difficult questions that lie behind these slogans.
