The FBI is warning that hackers are relying on autonomous AI hacking tools to launch cyberattacks across the U.S. and globally. The campaigns include a widespread hack of customers of American IT company ConnectWise and across South Korea’s financial industry.
In its 
latest flash cybersecurity advisory
, the FBI says hackers have targeted several industries in recent months, including financial services, government, tech, defense, phone and internet companies, and law firms, using open source tools like Artex to carry out autonomous-driven cyberattacks.
Artex is one of several open source AI-powered penetration testing platforms, a category of software built to probe a network’s defenses. Hackers can connect Artex to hosted frontier large language models (LLMs) like those from Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as to open source models and non-U.S. models. The FBI says Artex intrusions can be “noisy,” meaning they generate reams of traffic and data that may trigger a targeted company’s defensive alerts. But the tools allow hackers to move at speeds that make it far more difficult for human defenders to keep up.
In some cases, Artex-linked attacks can move from targeting a system to active compromise “in under three hours.”
The FBI’s warning comes as AI-powered hacks are escalating, particularly by finding easy-to-find security flaws and breaking into companies with relative ease.
Oftentimes, Artex will rely on rapidly scanning a victim’s network to look for signs of weakness, and then exploit simple security bugs, such as weak protections and exposed or stolen passwords to gain a foothold. From there, Artex allows the hacker to spread across the network for databases, mailboxes, and other sensitive information.
The FBI’s advisory noted that Artex has been used in several cyberattacks since the tool’s first public debut in July. Artex was used to hack a number of South Korean financial institutions, blamed in large part on weak cybersecurity defenses and lacking protections, such as multi-factor authentication. The attacks allowed hackers to 
steal the personal data
 of at least 68,000 people.
The FBI confirmed 
earlier findings from Microsoft
 that hackers also used Artex to break into IT management giant ConnectWise and gain access to the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of 
at least 2,250
 of its customers.
The hackers, who Microsoft says are likely Chinese, used the tool to steal data from affected ConnectWise customers.
A spokesperson for ConnectWise did not respond to a request for comment about the FBI’s claim.
Cybersecurity company Barracuda 
also disclosed
 that it was breached as part of an Artex-linked attack by financially motivated criminals, in which the tool found an exposed credential belonging to a third-party service that Barracuda had not revoked when it ended work with the vendor. That access allowed the hackers to access stores of credentials used by Barracuda to collect data from its customers’ systems. Barracuda said it couldn’t rule out access, as its logs were not detailed enough, and chose to “assume the worst” and changed all of its customer credentials.
“Defenders who rely on spotting malicious activity and then responding to it will often arrive after the attacker has finished,” wrote Barracuda’s chief information security officer, Arve Kjoelen.
A spokesperson for Barracuda told TechCrunch that the company “directly notified and advised a minority of XDR customers to revoke and recreate integration credentials,” but did not specify how many customers.
After Artex was used in cyberattacks, the tool’s developer made the software closed-source to prevent further attacks. But the FBI says the previous open source version continues to circulate and remains “widely accessible” for use in cyberattacks.
It’s not only companies and targeted victims that have been compromised as a result of weakened security exploited by AI tools; the tools themselves have been exposed as well, potentially allowing outsiders to access powerful hacking capabilities. Last week, researchers 
found hundreds of publicly exposed Artex systems online
, which had been placed on the internet without a password, likely by mistake or misconfiguration — including the Artex system that was used to target South Korea.

FBI warns ARTEX AI hacking tool is powering global cyberattacks
                            By 
Lawrence Abrams
November 15, 2026
01:29 PM
0
The FBI is warning that hackers are using an AI-powered penetration testing framework called ARTEX to automate cyberattacks against organizations worldwide, including a recent ConnectWise supply-chain breach that compromised Microsoft 365 customer environments.
In a 
FLASH advisory
 released Friday, the agency said multiple threat actors have weaponized the open-source framework since at least August 2026 to target organizations across the financial, government, technology, defense, healthcare, and other sectors.
"ARTEX significantly reduces the time required to chain attack techniques, with some intrusions progressing from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours," reads the FBI advisory.
"The framework enables threat actors with minimal technical expertise to conduct complex, multi-target operations simultaneously, fundamentally accelerating attack velocity and expanding operational scale."
ARTEX is an autonomous penetration testing framework written in Go that uses artificial intelligence agents to perform reconnaissance, discover vulnerabilities, exploit systems, escalate privileges, and steal data with little human intervention.
Rather than using its own AI model, the framework integrates with external large language models (LLMs), including Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, DeepSeek, GLM, Grok, and Kimi.
Attackers provide their goals and tasks to ARTEX, which converts them into commands executed by specialized AI agents. This allows them to automate intrusion activities and target multiple organizations simultaneously.
The FBI says the framework is particularly effective at identifying vulnerabilities in overlooked systems, including partner portals, employee applications, legacy services, and third-party integrations.
While these automated attacks can generate large volumes of suspicious traffic that trigger security alerts, their speed can leave defenders with little time to respond before attackers breach a system.
ARTEX was originally released on GitHub in July 2026 by a developer known as Autumn-27, who removed the repository on October 8 after reports emerged that threat actors were abusing the framework.
However, the FBI says the framework remains available through community forks, archived copies, localized versions, and a commercial edition called ARTEX PRO.
As of early November, researchers had identified more than 1,600 publicly exposed ARTEX consoles, with the actual number of deployments believed to be significantly higher.
The FBI's 13-page advisory includes indicators of compromise associated with known ARTEX campaigns, including IP addresses, command-and-control infrastructure, and suspicious network activity.
Separately, cybersecurity researcher MalwareMustDie released an 
open-source ARTEX detection signature pack
 on Friday to help defenders identify attacks involving the framework.
The detection pack includes rules and indicators for various security monitoring and threat detection tools, covering suspicious network requests, identity abuse, cloud activity, malicious files, and automated attack patterns.
The researcher also published a detection playbook that categorizes indicators by confidence level, warning that IP addresses and other infrastructure indicators can become outdated or generate false positives.
Organizations are urged to secure internet-facing applications, restrict access to cloud administration interfaces, review application permissions and authentication logs, and implement rate limits on sensitive APIs.
The bureau also recommends monitoring for unusual bursts of automated requests, suspicious cloud activity, unexpected credential or privilege changes, and large data transfers.
The FBI cautions that IP addresses listed in the IOCs may have changed since the attacks occurred.
Organizations that identify suspicious activity associated with ARTEX are encouraged to report their findings to the FBI or its Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3).
Known Artex-linked attacks
The advisory highlights several previously reported cyberattacks involving ARTEX, including the 
October ConnectWise Cloud Backup supply-chain attack
 that allowed hackers to gain unauthorized access to Microsoft 365 customer environments.
The attack compromised application identities used by ConnectWise's cloud backup service, formerly known as SkyKick Cloud Backup, allowing attackers to authenticate to customer tenants using permissions previously granted to the applications.
At the time, cybersecurity firm Huntress reported identifying at least 162 compromised tenants.
In an 
investigation published
 in October, Microsoft attributed the campaign to a China-state-aligned threat actor tracked as Storm-0940, which used ARTEX to accelerate reconnaissance, password spraying, and abuse of cloud application permissions.
After compromising ConnectWise application identities, the attackers used ARTEX to attempt authentication against more than 2,250 downstream Microsoft 365 tenants, with some intrusions progressing to credential modifications and attempts to establish persistent access.
The FBI also referenced attacks against 
South Korean financial institutions
 that exposed personal information belonging to approximately 67,000 people.
In that campaign, a Chinese-speaking threat actor combined ARTEX with Claude Code agents to attack multiple banks, including Shinhan Bank, KB Kookmin Bank, and Hana Bank.
Researchers at CrowdStrike discovered the attacker's exposed infrastructure, which contained ARTEX configuration files, Claude Code session histories, and other records documenting the intrusions.
In a separate investigation, South Korean police 
arrested seven suspects
 linked to attacks against more than 90 organizations, with ARTEX reportedly used in most of the intrusions.
Authorities said the gang stole approximately 8.2 million data records from organizations including hospitals, educational institutions, and businesses.
More recently, CrowdStrike reported that a Chinese cyberespionage group tracked as Vapor Panda 
used ARTEX in automated attacks
 targeting more than 200 organizations across 14 countries.
The campaign compromised at least nine organizations, with AI agents performing reconnaissance, exploiting vulnerabilities, deploying web shells, stealing credentials, and pursuing different attack paths when initial attempts failed.

Attackers are using open source AI penetration testing framework ARTEX to automate their attacks on banks, governments, energy and healthcare providers, and other critical sectors around the globe, according to security alerts from the FBI and Amazon Threat Intelligence.
Between October 3 and November 5, Amazon’s MadPot honeypot threat sensors detected two ARTEX campaigns: one that allowed thieves to steal AI credentials at “industrial scale” to gain free access to models, and a second that generated deepfake videos to trick live selfie checks.
In the first one, miscreants used AI agents to steal API keys, and then feed these keys into the ARTEX framework’s model gateway to power more agents searching for more AI model access keys. 
REG AD
“Agents validated identities through each provider’s token and identity services, enumerated storage, assessed permission scope, and went after managed AI model services, secrets and parameter stores, and application identities,” AWS Chief Information Security Officer CJ Moses 
wrote in a blog post
 on Friday. 
REG AD
Targets included LLM gateways and proxy platforms such as One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API, and LiteLLM, and AI application builders Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat, and Ollama.
This campaign also used cloud credentials to gain stolen access to the models.
In a second campaign, attackers used ARTEX to create real-time deepfake videos to trick identity verification (KYC) checks at two cryptocurrency exchanges, and forged ID photos to pass document checks.
“The agents reverse-engineered one vendor’s liveness protocol in detail, documenting the face-detection event stream it sends, how it tracks face position during the check, and the coordinate system it uses to tell the user where to move,” Moses wrote. 
The agents did manage to complete the full movement sequence with the face positioned within 7 pixels of the target, but this feat was not enough, as the vendor’s server-side verdict rejected the deepfake in every attempt.
In a separate session, agents built a real-time deepfake video pipeline on a single consumer-grade GPU using an open-source face animation model. This video achieved 11.5 frames per second, which Moses said is “close to what a live video check expects.”
Meanwhile, agents also worked on the ID document checks, building synthetic camera metadata whose image dimensions matched the real values inside the JPEG file, and preserving the original camera compression tables that are usually replaced by re-saving an edited image.
While the end goal was stealing cryptocurrency, the campaign illustrates how ARTEX runs several sessions simultaneously, with multiple agents working toward the same result. “One searched for ways to cash out, one worked on clearing account balances, one set up the deepfake video pipeline, and one carried out the verification bypass,” Moses wrote.
REG AD
None of the attack techniques are new, but both of these campaigns show how AI agents can boost the speed and persistence of the attacker. 
“The agents succeeded where basic security hygiene was missing, including default or hardcoded credentials, keys left in code and public artifacts, unauthenticated interfaces and inference endpoints, and unpatched software,” Moses added. “Getting those fundamentals right remains the most effective defense.”
