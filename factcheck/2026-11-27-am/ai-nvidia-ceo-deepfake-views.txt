Kat Tenbarge
is the author of 
Spitfire News, 
a newsletter about internet culture, politics, and media.
Jensen Huang is partying it up in Ibiza. In 
a video
 with nearly 6 million views on Instagram, the former CEO of Nvidia is dancing in a crowded club, champagne glass in hand, still wearing his signature black leather jacket. Suddenly, from behind him, Alice Huang — the Chinese technology executive and suspected spy he 
had an affair with
, sparking congressional investigations and his resignation from the chipmaking company he cofounded — appears. She puts her hand on his shoulder and he wraps his arm around her waist as they begin dancing together. The video’s caption reads “Honey Trap Final Boss. #inthemoodforlove.”
Of course, it’s AI-generated. This Instagram Reel is one of hundreds of AI deepfakes featuring Huang, his wife Lori, Alice, and sometimes Nvidia’s vice president of healthcare, Kimberly Powell (the second of three 
reported affair partners
). In this case, Huang is being swapped into an existing meme called “
Ibiza Final Boss
.” The original video featured a British construction worker with a bowl cut dancing in exactly the same way, minus a woman sidling up to him. Other viral AI hits include Huang 
in a scene
 from 
The Office
, Huang in the 
music video
 for George Michael’s “Careless Whisper,” and Huang as 
the former Astronomer CEO
 who got 
caught in an affair
 with one of his employees by the jumbotron at a Coldplay concert last summer. Even the rapper 50 Cent 
posted
 an AI edit of his “P.I.M.P.” music video, now featuring Huang with some flashy silver accessories.
Unlike deepfake pornography or election disinformation, these AI videos aren’t necessarily intended to harm or mislead people. They’re memes making fun of Huang’s cheating scandal and its global implications, created with AI technology that in many cases relies heavily on Nvidia’s tech stack: video diffusion models running on the Nvidia GPUs that power much of the modern generative AI industry. “It’s ironic that without Nvidia generating millions of these AI memes ... would not be possible,” reads one of the top comments on the Ibiza deepfake. “In some sense, Jensen is making Nvidia richer by his own scandal.” The comment was punctuated by multiple cry-laughing emoji.
As it turns out, Huang may benefit, too. On Monday, the Nvidia board 
announced
 he was reinstated as president, CEO, and board director following “a series of discussions and meetings” and “input from senior levels of the United States government.” Nvidia’s stock has 
since surged
, and new AI Huang memes cast him in a victorious light. 
One variation
 swaps him into a scene from 
The Wolf of Wall Street
, announcing “I’m not leaving” to a crowd of cheering men that has been edited to include AI versions of Donald Trump, Elon Musk, and Mark Zuckerberg.

While the saga continues, many social media users are finding the humor in these Huang deepfakes, and their creators are churning out variations to meet the sudden demand. The Instagram account behind the Ibiza one has made three Huang deepfakes so far, all remakes of the same scene. 
Another version
 features Powell dancing with him, and the 
third one
 shows a sea of young women in bikini tops and shorts surrounding Huang, who lifts his champagne up in celebration. Each has accrued millions of views.
“His story has just come at the best time for ai creators and a terrible time for him,” wrote the account behind the Ibiza videos in a direct message to 
The Verge
. “Everyone was doing him. Most I’ve seen anyone AI-ed.”
This deepfake creator identified himself as a 40-year-old man from Melbourne, Australia, but wouldn’t share his real name. He says he has spent “thousands” on AI video generation, using models like TikTok developer ByteDance’s Seedance 2.5 and Alibaba’s Wan 3. These, along with other AI tools like Google’s Nano Banana, help power the ecosystem of deepfakes.
He said his Instagram page has been going “super viral” in the past two weeks, thanks to the Ibiza meme format. He has nearly 100 million views on various iterations of it featuring different celebrities. The Huang ones are among the most popular, alongside versions featuring 
Elizabeth Holmes
 and 
Amanda Seyfried
 playing her in the hit Nathan Fielder documentary 
You Can See Everything
.
“I wanted to elevate the content and know that putting what is happening in the news ... would get traction,” he wrote. “Always wanted to be a filmmaker but this technology is beyond whatever I imagined was possible.”
As generative AI has taken hold of the internet in recent years, a lot of the resulting viral content has been disparaged as “
AI slop
.” 
Hollywood productions
, 
mainstream artists
, and even 
local businesses
 have been maligned for using (or appearing to use) AI in their advertisements and final cuts. Video trends like putting 
sexy AI fruit characters
 in 
Love Island
 parodies racked up millions of views online but failed to deliver any real cultural momentum. The ubiquity of the Huang deepfakes and the relative lack of blowback from AI critics may indicate that the future of AI-generated content is similar to the rest of meme culture: If the topic’s trending, people will engage with something, even if it’s following the same formula as the slop. That an AI tech titan’s own indiscretions are inspiring this latest evolution in deepfakes is just the sardonic cherry on top.
“It’s not that we’re inundated in deepfakes of Trump being like ‘Nuclear war tomorrow,’ like the way it was framed as the big problem,” says misinformation researcher Abbie Richards. “Instead, it just feels like there is so much slop to push through to have any grounding in reality.”
The AI memes of Huang and other public figures don’t feel as sinister as other forms of AI disinformation and misinformation, mainly because of how goofy they are. It’s hard to imagine anyone falling for a “
Gangnam Style
” parody or any serious implications if they did. But these deepfakes are still eerily realistic, as one comment on the Seyfried Ibiza variation pointed out: “All jokes aside, is anyone else finding it increasingly alarming that AI is capable of this?”
Even the creator behind the Ibiza AI videos thinks “someone will try and clamp down because it’s getting outta control.” But in the meantime, he’s looking for ways to monetize his newfound virality. Despite his significant financial outlay, he says he hasn’t made any money yet, besides getting “gifted $3 on a reel once.”
While Instagram has started to invite some creators to earn direct payouts from views on their Reels, the account behind the AI “Careless Whisper” parody featuring Huang told 
The Verge
 that “the way to earn is through creating content for clients and companies after gaining attention on social media.” This AI creator, who declined to share any identifying information, wrote in a direct message that they receive dozens of requests from new and returning clients for AI commissions every time they go viral, “which is quite often.” In addition to Huang deepfakes, some of their most viral AI edits feature Tobey Maguire’s Spider-Man 
as a rapper
.
The Ibiza AI meme creator has worked with one celebrity brand so far, belonging to the Irish mixed martial artist Conor McGregor. Four months ago, McGregor launched an energy drink called MAC Energy, and the company requested an AI Ibiza meme showing McGregor dancing with a can of it. The creator complied, and 
the post
 exists as a collaboration between the two Instagram pages, but it isn’t labeled as an advertisement and he says he didn’t ask for any money in return.
“I just did it because it was a good opportunity for my page,” he says.
It seems like the entity that stands to reap the biggest reward from viral Huang deepfakes is Instagram itself and the other social media sites where creators are posting them. And these humorous AI edits are technically allowed on Meta platforms, 
as long as
 creators disclose that they’re AI-generated. Meta didn’t respond to a request for comment by publication time.
But there are other considerations besides profitability. Many of these AI memes hinge on sexist portrayals and racist stereotypes as the joke. Asian American women in Silicon Valley have reported an 
uptick in microaggressions
 revolving around “honeypot” and “Chinese spy” allegations. And when it comes to Powell and the other Nvidia employee who Huang reportedly had a relationship with, the workplace power dynamic at play has faded into the background of mockery.
The more troubling dynamics of Huang’s affairs have faded into the background of mockery
“Even though we know that he was wielding his power to have inappropriate relationships with his subordinates and his employees, it’s still like ‘LOL, let’s make an AI deepfake of him at the office,’” says Josh Lora, a writer and creator who has a 
popular TikTok series
 about the role of cheating scandals in American culture. “Why aren’t we having a real conversation about the effects of this on women, the effects of this workplace on women, especially 10 years after #MeToo?”
The apparent lack of interest from major social media companies to stem the flow of this kind of disparaging AI content isn’t surprising to misinformation expert Richards, even as some 
tech CEOs
, like Elon Musk, called for Huang’s reinstatement and cast doubt on the reporting against him. If Huang memes are truly in vogue, that may be worth more to the AI industry than his dignity.
“I think that they are invested in the widespread adoption of AI,” she says. “So anything with AI going viral and appearing to be popular and liked by the public probably, in their mind, benefits them.”
With Huang back as CEO, the message couldn’t be clearer: For him, the consequences of his affairs were temporary, including whatever humiliation stemmed from the AI memes. Ultimately, all any of it meant was more power for Nvidia and more power for Jensen 
Huang.

KEY POINTS
Hundreds of AI-generated meme videos of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang are circulating, and one Instagram Reel has nearly 6 million views.
Creators say they use video models such as Seedance 2.5 and Wan 3, which often run on Nvidia GPUs, but earn little directly from the clips.
Meta allows such edits if they are disclosed as AI-generated, and critics say the memes reduce serious issues to jokes.
AI-generated meme videos of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang have become a popular format on social media, according to a report by The Verge. One Instagram Reel, which places Huang in a dancing meme known as Ibiza Final Boss, has gathered nearly 6 million views. The outlet counts it among hundreds of deepfakes of Huang and people linked to a recent scandal around him.
The Verge described the clips as satire rather than the kind of deepfakes made to deceive or harm. Other viral edits put Huang into a scene from The Office and a George Michael music video, and the rapper 50 Cent posted an AI version of one of his own videos featuring Huang. Much of the tooling depends on Nvidia, since video diffusion models commonly run on its GPUs.
The creator behind the Ibiza videos told The Verge he is a 40-year-old man from Melbourne, Australia, and declined to give his name. He said he has spent thousands on AI video generation, using models such as ByteDance's Seedance 2.5 and Alibaba's Wan 3, along with tools like Google's Nano Banana. By his account, versions of the meme with different celebrities have drawn nearly 100 million views in about two weeks.
Making money from the clips is harder. The creator said he has earned nothing beyond a $3 gift on one Reel. Instagram has begun inviting some creators to receive payouts based on Reel views. The account behind an AI Careless Whisper parody told the outlet that the real income comes from commissions by clients and companies after a clip goes viral.
The Ibiza creator has done one brand collaboration, with an energy drink company owned by mixed martial artist Conor McGregor. The post is not labeled as an advertisement, and he said he asked for no payment. Meta, which owns Instagram, did not respond to The Verge's request for comment. The report says such edits are allowed on its platforms as long as creators disclose that they are AI-generated.
Researchers and commentators quoted by The Verge see mixed implications. Misinformation researcher Abbie Richards said the problem is less a flood of alarming fakes than the sheer volume of low-quality content, saying there is so much slop to push through to have any grounding in reality. Writer Josh Lora argued that the jokes push aside a more serious conversation about workplace power and its effects on women.
The report also notes that some of the memes rely on sexist or racist stereotypes, and that Asian American women in Silicon Valley have described more microaggressions tied to spy allegations. The Verge adds that Nvidia's board announced on Monday that Huang had been reinstated as president, CEO and board director.
The Ibiza creator himself expects limits to come eventually, saying he thinks someone will try to clamp down because things are getting out of control. For now, the clips show how cheap, realistic video generation is feeding a fast-moving meme cycle that rewards whatever is trending.
