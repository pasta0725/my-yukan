Google DeepMind has 
revealed a new generation
 of the AI-powered Gemini Robotics platform. In the 2.5 release, Google says its robotic AI is getting tantalizingly close to completing real-world tasks as well as humans do. In fact, some Gemini robots were able to get actual work done in Mercedes-Benz factories, and the team is preparing for a day when robots operate alongside humans with an open source safety benchmark.
DeepMind’s 
physical AI
 platform has several computational layers between user input and robotic action. There’s the vision-language model piece, which is Gemini Embodied Reasoning. Essentially, it takes your instructions and uses live video to plan how the robot can accomplish the task. The other main component is the vision-language-action (VLA) that actually generates robot actions, like moving an actuator—that’s Gemini Robotics, which has been bumped to version 2.5.
DeepMind says that Gemini Robotics 2.5 is much better at tasks that require a high degree of dexterity. The model was evaluated across more than 200 activities, including serving food with tongs and using a pipette, as you would see in a laboratory. Google also showed that Apptronik’s Apollo 2 humanoid robot with Gemini Robotics 2.5 can now tie a bow with its five-fingered hands, a notable feat, because deformable objects (like ribbon) are notoriously difficult for robots to manipulate.

Overall, Gemini Robotics 2.5 manages a 30 to 100 percent higher success rate compared to 
the last version
. It’s also 20 to 30 percent faster. But that’s based on laboratory testing. Google partnered with Apptronik and Mercedes-Benz to see how capable this AI is when faced with the uncertainty of the world outside robotics labs. The Gemini-powered Apollo 2 robots kitted automotive parts in a few European factories, reaching a 96 percent success rate.
That’s an impressive showing compared to previous robotic AI systems, but it’s still probably not enough for a company like Mercedes-Benz. A human doing that sort of repetitive task will get it right virtually every time, and DeepMind director Kanishka Rao notes that closing that last 4 percent gap will be a “steep road.”
You could manually program a robot to do a handful of tasks in a factory without issue—this type of automation has been common for decades. Gemini Robotics is not trying to just do a few things, though. These are intended to be general robots that can handle almost anything, but there are trade-offs to that approach.
“We can be very good at a particular application at the cost of generalization,” said DeepMind Robotics head Carolina Parada. “Or we can just push on the generalization axis and maybe not get to 99.9 in every application.”
Argon assistance
Training data is essential for training all generative AI models. Most text-based chatbots in existence were fed basically the entirety of the open Internet, but there isn’t as much “physical data” for robotic AI. The best kind of training data for robots comes from teleoperation, but the team says Gemini 4 Argon has helped create a “simulation engine” that greatly accelerated development of Gemini Robotics 2.5.
The team used Argon to generate thousands of variations on practice runs based on real-world camera data. These “digital twins” apparently allowed Gemini Robotics 2.5 to more quickly generalize new tasks and settings. There’s still a role for the real thing, though. DeepMind combined this synthetic data with traditional training demonstrations to ground the model.
Argon was also used for analyzing failures and suggesting ways to improve in both simulations and real robots. However, this isn’t something that works in real time. Frontier models like Gemini take time to churn through their tokens, but robots need to be able to operate without long pauses. The new VLA isn’t lightning quick, either. While Google has experimented with some embedded robotics models, the current 2.5 release is still running on cloud-based servers. So those gains in accuracy and speed still don’t come without latency. The video above is sped up by 2x to make the robot look more human-like.
DeepMind is also working to ensure the failures don’t put any nearby humans at risk. The team notes that Gemini Robotics 2.5 uses inference-time reasoning to avoid hazards. So if something gets in the way, the robot will stop completely or wait until it’s safe to continue. This is essential if you’re going to have a robot operating outside of the lab, for example, in a Mercedes factory. DeepMind says this feature allowed Gemini robots to safely complete tasks 33 percent more often when obstacles were present. The success rate went up 43 percent when people were in the way.
There is no agreed-upon way to test physical AI for safety, but DeepMind hopes to move the industry in that direction with ASIMOV-Jailbreak. This is an open source database that contains 1,500 prompts designed to trick a robotic AI into doing something unsafe. For example, a prompt might ask the robot to place items in a bag and close it with a fastener too fiddly for the robot to operate. The AI should decline to do that, but attempting to proceed and incorrectly closing the container would be the “unsafe” outcome.
DeepMind says that placing Argon in the loop results in a 98.8 percent jailbreak safety level. With just Gemini Robotics ER 2, the system scores 73.5 percent in Google’s safety benchmark.
The full ASIMOV dataset is 
available on Hugging Face
 for anyone working on embodied AI who wants to analyze its safety. Gemini Robotics 2.5 is not generally available, but it’s now in the hands of Google’s robotics partners, including Agile Robots, Apptronik, and Boston Dynamics.

On November 10, 2026, Google DeepMind announced Gemini Robotics 2.5, a new AI model that controls robot movements. In a proof of concept on live Mercedes-Benz production lines, the company says it achieved a 
96%
 success rate on parts sorting and kitting tasks. However, that figure applies to a specific parts-preparation task, not to the success rate across the entire automotive manufacturing process.
The demonstration combined "Gemini Robotics ER," which tracks the progress of the overall task, with "Gemini Robotics 2.5," which controls the movements of the robot's hands and arms. In addition to improved task performance, DeepMind published evaluation results on hazard avoidance during operation and resistance to dangerous instructions. The robot used in the demonstration also has a safety mechanism that detects surrounding objects and stops. These address different kinds of risk, so the AI model's judgment and the hardware's safety measures need to be evaluated separately.
AD
Automating parts preparation on live Mercedes-Benz lines
According to 
Google DeepMind's official announcement
, the proof of concept was carried out on Mercedes-Benz's live production lines in Kecskemét, Hungary, and Sindelfingen, Germany. The robot used was "Apollo 2," a humanoid developed by Apptronik. It was tasked with picking up metal brackets tossed loosely into supply bins and arranging them in a different bin in the order required for each vehicle.
In this task, Gemini Robotics ER tracks how the work is progressing, while Gemini Robotics 2.5 controls hand and arm movements. DeepMind also describes a function that detects missing parts and corrects for them. The aim is to let robots handle this kind of parts preparation so that human workers can focus on more complex assembly steps.
The 
96%
 success rate DeepMind reported is strictly the result of a proof of concept for this parts sorting and kitting task. The relevant section of the official announcement does not provide details such as the number of trials, the evaluation period, or how often humans intervened during the work. Performing a specific task on an operating production line is meaningful, but this number alone cannot be used to judge long-term stable operation or applicability across an entire factory.
Improved dexterity and task speed across roughly 200 tasks
DeepMind evaluated Gemini Robotics 2.5 on roughly 200 tasks. Examples include handling food with tongs and tying a bow with ribbon on a gift box. These tasks require not only accurate tool use but also fine movements for handling materials that easily change shape. The evaluation emphasizes tasks that demand dexterous hand manipulation, going beyond simply moving objects.
According to DeepMind, success rates improved by 30 to 
100%
 over previous baselines, depending on the type of task. Task completion speed also rose by 20 to 
30%
. However, these are relative improvements over earlier models; they do not mean that every task reached a 
100%
 success rate or that success rates rose by up to 100 percentage points. Actual success rates and the size of the improvement vary by task.
The supported robots are not limited to a single machine, either. Apollo 2, used in the factory demonstration, has a SharpaWave hand with five fingers and 22 degrees of freedom, while the Franka Duo uses two-finger parallel grippers. On its 
Gemini Robotics model page
, DeepMind says the model also supports whole-body control that coordinates the entire body, not just the hands. Movements such as walking while adjusting the center of gravity, squatting, and bending over are envisioned, but these are capabilities the company describes, not a guarantee that the model works on every machine or in every environment.
"Gemini 4 Argon" was also used in training the model. DeepMind describes a method that generates thousands of simulated practice scenarios from footage of real environments captured by cameras, building digital twins that replicate real-world settings. By combining these simulations with demonstration data from real robots, the company aims to improve the model's ability to handle diverse situations. The system also helps form hypotheses about the causes of failures from footage of failed attempts and test improvements. However, no specific figures have been given for how much this approach shortens training time.

Three different mechanisms underpin robot safety
The safety measures DeepMind described fall broadly into three categories: the robot's ability to avoid obstacles and people while operating, resistance to instructions that encourage dangerous behavior, and a physical stopping mechanism built into the hardware. Because the hazards addressed and the evaluation methods differ, the individual performance metrics cannot be combined into a single safety indicator.

On hazard avoidance during operation, DeepMind says the ability to complete tasks safely improved by 
33%
 around obstacles and 
43%
 around people. This evaluates the ability to finish tasks while avoiding dangerous movements through inference-time control, and does not mean physical accident rates fell by the same proportion. The announcement does not give absolute success rates before and after, so there is not enough information to judge what level of safety was reached.
For resistance to dangerous instructions, the evaluation used the public benchmark "ASIMOV-Jailbreak." The test measures resistance to hundreds of prompts that disguise dangerous requests across dozens of scenarios. Gemini 4 Argon scored 
99.5%
, ahead of the 
82%
 recorded by Gemini Robotics ER 2, the comparison model. However, this figure is an evaluation result for Gemini 4 Argon and is not a direct measurement of the operational safety of Gemini Robotics 2.5 itself. Moreover, being able to refuse dangerous instructions is a separate matter from a real robot avoiding physical accidents.
On the hardware side, Apollo 2 has an object detection and stopping mechanism. According to Apptronik, Apollo 2 has a zone that triggers an immediate stop when an object is detected, and a surrounding zone in which behavior can be configured when people, equipment, or obstacles approach. This mechanism provides hardware-side safety that is independent of whether the AI model recognizes and refuses a dangerous instruction. However, how each zone was configured in the Mercedes-Benz factory demonstration cannot be confirmed from the published materials.
None of these results guarantees that the robot will not cause an accident. The AI model that chooses safe movements, the mechanism that refuses dangerous instructions, and the hardware stopping mechanism each play a different role. In real workplaces, these must be combined and then verified to confirm that contact with equipment and people can be prevented.
Full factory deployment will require validation of recovery from failures and sustained operation
DeepMind is rolling out Gemini Robotics 2.5 to partners in the robotics field, and the model page also directs interested parties to an early-access waitlist. However, availability to partners is different from being freely available to general developers and companies. Anyone considering adoption needs to check who is actually eligible and what the terms of use are.
On the operational side, the robot's own management systems come into play alongside the AI model. According to Apptronik's product description, "Artemis" coordinates functions such as perception, planning, and control, while "Fleet Connect" handles robot status monitoring, task coordination, and deployment management. However, detailed specifications on exactly how these systems were used in this factory demonstration, or how they connect to Google's model, have not been confirmed.
A 
96%
 success rate on live Mercedes-Benz lines represents progress toward practical use of humanoid robots for parts preparation. But completing a specific task with high probability is not the same as operating stably in a factory over a long period. Going forward, the key questions will be whether the robot can recover on its own when a task fails, how much human intervention is needed, and how performance and work efficiency change with continuous operation. If such operational data becomes available, it will be easier to connect the results of this proof of concept to deployment decisions on actual manufacturing floors.
