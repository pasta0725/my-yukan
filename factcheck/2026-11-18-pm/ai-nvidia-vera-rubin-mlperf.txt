In its 
MLPerf
 benchmarks debut, with results released today, the 
NVIDIA Vera Rubin
 platform delivers leading performance across training, post-training and agentic inference. 
NVIDIA swept every MLPerf Training v6.1 benchmark and, in its first MLPerf Endpoints submission, delivered more than 10x generational gains in throughput per watt for agentic AI — measuring how much agentic work a system delivers with each watt of power. 
These results are just the beginning. Vera Rubin performance will keep climbing through continuous optimization of NVIDIA’s 
CUDA-powered software stack
, so the infrastructure that customers invest in today delivers more value over time.
NVIDIA Sweeps MLPerf Training v6.1
AI agents are now everywhere, writing code, running business workflows, answering customer questions and, as this week’s 
SC26 conference highlights
, advancing scientific research. 
Faster training means smarter agents can reach users sooner, drug discovery models can keep pace with new lab data and fraud-detection models can adapt to new threats faster.
MLPerf Training measures how fast a system can train a range of AI models to a target level of accuracy. 
The NVIDIA platform was the only one with results on every benchmark this round, spanning large language models, image generation, recommender systems and reinforcement learning post-training. In its debut, Vera Rubin NVL72 delivered up to 2x more performance per GPU than the highly optimized NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 system — early results that show the pace of generation-over-generation innovation.

NVIDIA also demonstrated performance at scale. On the DeepSeek-V3 benchmark, Vera Rubin NVL72 scaled from 256 to 1,024 GPUs with about 97% efficiency: quadrupling the GPUs cut time to train from 16 minutes to about four. 
NVIDIA also ran the benchmark on 10,240 GPUs with GB300 systems, representing the largest scale in this round. Microsoft Azure matched that scale with 10,240 NVIDIA GPUs with GB200 systems. 
Results at this scale matter because adding GPUs only speeds up training if they can work together efficiently. NVIDIA NVLink scale-up networking connects the GPUs within each system so they act as one, while NVIDIA Quantum-X800 InfiniBand and Spectrum-X Ethernet scale-out networking link those systems across the data center. Combined with optimized software, this keeps thousands of GPUs working in concert, helping developers build more intelligent models while cutting training times.
NVIDIA Vera Rubin Leads Agentic Inference on MLPerf Endpoints
An AI agent for travel that plans and books a whole trip — or an IT agent that finds and fixes a problem before anyone files a ticket — may reason, call tools and check its work many times to finish a single task. 
Each of those steps generates tokens. Because AI data centers are limited by power, the more tokens a system can generate each second for every watt of power, the more agents it can serve and the more value it creates. 
MLPerf Endpoints tests systems on real agentic coding and enterprise workflows, and checks that the agents still complete their tasks correctly. NVIDIA Vera Rubin leads across all of its agentic benchmarks.
For example, in a provisional result for Kimi K3, a large open model built for agentic workflows, Vera Rubin NVL72 systems deliver more than 10x the throughput per watt of NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 at the same response speed.

===== https://mlcommons.org/2026/11/mlperf-training-v6-1-results/
(status 200)
Today, MLCommons
®
 announced new results for the MLPerf
®
 Training v6.1 benchmark suite. The two new benchmarks added in this round, and the submissions received, demonstrate the impact of recent architectural advances in AI training systems and the increasing diversity of the hardware used to train them.
“We’ve reached an exciting new threshold for the MLPerf Training benchmark suite, with a higher level of participation in this round, the representation of both established and emerging hardware offerings, and the increased diversity of configurations and deployments in the results,” said Pavan Yalamanchili, MLPerf Training working group co-chair. “Having much more of the matrix of possible systems represented in the published results gives us a clearer view of how they stack up against each other in the most important real-world scenarios for AI training.”
MLPerf Training v6.1 adds two new benchmarks, …
The MLPerf Training benchmark suite comprises full system tests that stress models, software, and hardware for a range of machine learning (ML) applications. The open-source and peer-reviewed benchmark suite provides a level playing field for competition, driving innovation, performance, and energy efficiency across the industry. The suite’s benchmark collection is curated by a panel of experts from the AI community.
Version 6.1 adds two new benchmarks: DLRMv4-HSTU, a recommender; and Qwen3.5-397B, a large-scale LLM post-training workload.
DLRMv4-HSTU is a recommendation benchmark, updating and replacing the DLRM-dcnv2 benchmark included in earlier versions of the MLPerf benchmark. It utilizes the HSTU attention-based architecture and trains on the Yambda dataset, one of the largest open-sourced recommendation datasets available. 
The Qwen3.5-397B benchmark is a first-of-its-kind workload for MLPerf Training: large-scale post-training using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). Much of the recent innovations in AI, such as agentic functionality, rely on post-training activities; in response, the MLPerf community designed and built a new benchmark to test the performance of such systems. Through post-training this benchmark learns to reason on its own to solve software engineering tasks by training on specific tasks such as tool-calling.
“We are constantly striving to ensure that the MLPerf Training benchmark suite is tracking innovations as they become standard practice in the field,” said Shriya Rishab, MLPerf Training working group co-chair. “Post-training has added a new level of complexity to the architecture of AI training, and we’re proud of the work we’ve done over the past two years on this brand-new benchmark test that will deliver important insights on the performance of state-of-the-art AI training systems. And the v6.1 results show that the community is quickly embracing it.”
Increasing diversity of submissions highlights wide variety of training hardware, architectures, and scale 
MLPerf Training v6.1 further increased the number of submitters, submitted systems, results, and the scale of typical systems, beyond some of the records set six months ago in v6.0. Participants in this round submitted 153 systems, yielding 250 test results. 57 of those systems, or 37% of the total, were cloud-based, an increase from 29% in v6.0. Twelve systems used 1,000 or more accelerators, with the largest system using 10,240 accelerators. Systems utilized NVIDIA, AMD and Google accelerator hardware, including for the first time NVIDIA Rubin-generation GPUs and the Google TPUv7 (Ironwood).
“As the task domains and accompanying training workloads specialize, we are seeing a shift in the underlying hardware configurations that are being deployed to host that work,” said Yalamanchili. “The MLPerf Training benchmark suite provides essential guidance for those deploying AI training systems, with data on diverse systems across workloads that help organizations identify the ‘sweet spot’ for their own buildout, whether it’s a single-node system or a multi-node cluster with thousands of accelerators.”
Record industry participation points to broad community support for independent, transparent benchmarking standards
The MLPerf Training v6.1 round includes performance results from 24 submitting organizations: AMD, ASUSTeK, Azure, Cisco, CoreWeave, Crusoe, Dell, Firmus, Fujitsu, Giga Computing, Google, HPE, Inventec, KRAI, Lambda, MiTAC, Nebius, NVIDIA, Oracle, Quanta Cloud Technology, Supermicro, tiny corp, TTA, University of Florida.  “We would especially like to welcome first-time MLPerf Training submitter Crusoe,” said David Kanter, Head of MLPerf at MLCommons. “The fact that so many organizations continue to submit to the MLPerf Training benchmark while new submitters are still joining in shows the value to the community of having reliable benchmark data for new workloads and new systems.” 
Robust participation by a broad set of industry stakeholders strengthens the AI ecosystem as a whole and helps to ensure that the benchmark is serving the community’s needs. We invite submitters and other stakeholders to join the 
MLPerf Training working group
 and help us continue to evolve the benchmark.

MLCommons is an open AI engineering consortium whose mission is to standardize AI measurement and enable informed decisions. Since 2018, MLCommons has used collective engineering with industry and academia to build and operate industrial-quality benchmarks and metrics that evaluate AI systems, so that customers can understand the trade-offs and make informed decisions.

As AI agents tackle increasingly complex workflows, reducing time-to-train is critical for rapidly iterating on both models and the agents they power. MLPerf Training v6.1 marks the debut of the NVIDIA Vera Rubin architecture, and these early results demonstrate that its CUDA software stack is already highly mature. In its first appearance, the Vera Rubin NVL72 platform delivered the fastest time to train on six of the seven benchmarks, achieving up to 2x more performance than a highly optimized NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 baseline.
For the seventh benchmark, DeepSeek-V3 671B, NVIDIA captured the absolute fastest time of the round by demonstrating massive scale on a 10,240-GPU GB300 NVL72 cluster. This dual-platform approach deliberately showcases the strengths of both systems. While the GB300 results highlight the massive, reliable cluster scale NVIDIA is deploying today, Vera Rubin demonstrated its own exceptional scaling architecture by maintaining above 97% overall efficiency when scaling from 256 to 1,024 GPUs. Together, NVIDIA platforms hold the fastest time to train on every benchmark in v6.1.
NVIDIA platforms were also the only ones to submit results across all seven benchmarks, with Vera Rubin successfully completing the entire suite in its inaugural run. For developers, this breadth matters as much as any single top-line number. It proves that the performance gains inherited on day one span the entire modeling pipeline, comprehensively supporting everything from large-scale pretraining to the reinforcement learning post-training required for modern AI agents.

The pattern is clear. The largest gain lands on the largest, most communication-heavy model. While the platform is consistently 1.4x to 1.6x faster across the rest of the suite, the generational advantage widens even further for the massive DeepSeek-V3 benchmark. 
The suite covers natural language, image generation, recommendation, and reinforcement learning, so the gains hold across multiple modalities as well as across different model shapes and architectures.
DeepSeek-V3 671B: 2x per GPU on the hardest workload
DeepSeek-V3 671B is a 671-billion-parameter token-dropless Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model and the most demanding workload in the suite. Expert parallelism turns every iteration into a burst of all-to-all communication, so fabric efficiency shows up directly in time to train.
Vera Rubin NVL72 trained the benchmark in 4.14 minutes using 1,024 GPUs across 16 racks. GB300 NVL72 ran the identical 1,024-GPU configuration in 8.03 minutes, close to 2x per GPU, and the largest generational improvement in this round. Halving time to train roughly doubles how many experiments a team can run in a quarter, and experiment throughput is what moves model quality. 
At a larger scale, GB300 NVL72 produced the fastest absolute result of the round: 1.38 minutes across 10,240 GPUs. That is one training job spread across 160 racks, each its own NVLink domain, and it shows the platform’s scale-out fabric holding up long after the job outgrows a single rack.
Post-training arrives, and Vera Rubin leads it
MLPerf Training v6.1 adds its 
first agentic LLM reinforcement learning post-training benchmark
, Qwen 3.5 397B GRPO, reflecting a fundamental shift in AI development. Post-training has emerged as the critical differentiator for agentic accuracy, making the GRPO workload uniquely demanding. The continuous cycle of generating rollouts, scoring them, and updating weights pushes system architectures to their limits by forcing constant, massive weight migrations between inference and training engines.
Vera Rubin NVL72 achieved the fastest time for this new benchmark with a time of 10.44 minutes at 1,024 GPUs (6.1-0117), compared with 13.78 minutes for GB300 NVL72 (6.1-0031) at the same scale. The Vera Rubin platform also proved its flexibility by submitting on two different inference backends: NVIDIA NeMo RL with TensorRT-LLM at 10.44 minutes and with vLLM at 10.94 minutes (6.1-0118). Shipping two viable inference backends on a benchmark this new says as much about software maturity as it does about silicon.
Teaching these models to observe, reason, and act requires running each rollout in a simulated environment and executing tool calls, code, or API validations. Because driving these simulations is intensely CPU-bound, 
the NVIDIA Vera CPU is integral to the architecture
, sustaining more than 22,500 concurrent RL environments across 256 CPUs in a single Vera CPU rack. 
While the GPUs handle massive token generation and rapid weight updates, the Vera CPUs work in lockstep to simulate the environments, ensuring the accelerators are continuously fed with the rich feedback required to perfect agentic workflows.
NVLink, Quantum-X and Spectrum-X: Co-designed networking for high-efficiency AI scaling
Every result above is also a networking result. Modern AI training requires thousands of processors to act as one, a challenge NVIDIA solves by co-designing the internal rack domain and the external cluster fabric.
Inside the rack, Vera Rubin NVL72 connects 72 GPUs and 36 CPUs through a copper NVLink 6 spine. Delivering 3.6 TB/s per GPU and 260 TB/s per rack, this all-to-all domain keeps MoE expert dispatch off the critical path. Beyond the rack, the platform scales out via ConnectX-9 SuperNICs over Quantum-X800 InfiniBand or Spectrum-X Ethernet. To manage bursty MoE traffic, both fabrics use adaptive routing and congestion control to keep all-to-all communications hidden behind the compute layer.
This cohesive design translates directly to efficiency. On the DeepSeek-V3 benchmark, Vera Rubin NVL72 achieved above 97% scaling efficiency as it scaled from 256 to 1,024 GPUs (4 to 16 racks). GB300 NVL72 showcased the platform’s large-scale capabilities , extending to 10,240 GPUs to capture the fastest absolute result of the round.
Because the NVL72 architecture provides a consistent 72-GPU domain, developers see predictable performance jumps every generation without altering their operating model. Each cycle simply delivers more compute and bandwidth across the same consistent fabric.

That is what a mature software stack means for customers. The infrastructure gets faster after it ships, and the improvements continue to arrive, extending the platform’s productive lifetime and revenue generating potential.
Because the CUDA platform is highly programmable, customers can unlock even more performance on their own. For example, 
Thinking Machines Lab
 developed an NVFP4 training recipe and custom kernels for its Inkling 2 Small model on GB300 NVL72, using NVFP4 for forward-pass MoE GEMMs and MXFP8 for gradients. Compared with full MXFP8 training, it sped up pretraining and fine-tuning by 8% and reinforcement learning by 20%, with minimal accuracy impact. 
An ecosystem that shipped on day one
Vera Rubin’s debut in MLPerf Training v6.1 proves the architecture is built for immediate global deployment. With 80 percent of all closed-division submissions running on NVIDIA platforms across 20 partner organizations, these benchmarks represent 
reproducible performance
 across clouds and OEMs rather than isolated laboratory peaks. 
Systems are already generally available across various cloud infrastructure partners, complementing a multitude of AI Factory deployments around the globe. Today, Vera Rubin leads on performance per GPU across all seven workloads, while Blackwell and Blackwell Ultra maintain the scale record. Azure’s 2,560-node, 10,240-GPU GB200 NVL72 submission tied the NVIDIA 10,240-GPU GB300 NVL72 run to claim this round’s largest clusters.  
Partners are also pushing the limits of the architecture at the node level: Firmus used AI-driven kernel generation, with automated performance testing and mathematical validation, to post the fastest GB300 8-GPU result of the round, training GPT-OSS 20B in 52.90 minutes (6.1-0091) and Llama 3.1 8B in 52.40 minutes (6.1-0094). Because these platforms share a continuously optimizing CUDA software stack, developers can expect these inaugural metrics to climb steadily higher before the next MLPerf round even begins.
As the Vera Rubin software stack matures the way the Blackwell stack did, expect these initial performance advantages to continue to unlock even greater value throughout the platform’s lifecycle.
