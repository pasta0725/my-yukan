What’s the scariest thing about space exploration? Probably the 
snakes
. The second most frightening thing? Untrackable debris. 
Atomic-6 built Space Armor to shield satellites from debris too small to track. The startup released test results today exploring whether the same tiles could protect astronauts on spacewalks.
“Building Space Armor, we want to protect assets in space,” Atomic-6 CEO Trevor Smith told Payload. “I can’t think of a higher value asset than a human life.”
Bullseye:
 At Texas A&M University’s Hypervelocity Impact Laboratory, Atomic-6 fired a 3-mm aluminum sphere speeding ~7.2 km/s (~16,100 mph) at a ballistic torso—once behind a Space Armor Lite tile and once behind a 4-mm-thick solid aluminum plate. Atomic-6 said the plate stands in for current spacesuit protection.
“No humans were harmed in the testing of this,” Smith said. 
Behind the Space Armor Lite tile, the torso showed no visible damage or penetration, and the tile’s back didn’t bulge, crack, or shed material.
The aluminum plate, on the other hand, let the sphere punch right through, leaving fragments embedded in the ballistic torso.
The company cautioned that a stationary torso isn’t a spacesuit, so the results don’t show injury outcomes or whether a suit would hold pressure after impact. Next up, the company is planning tests with curved tiles and new impact angles. Atomic-6 is also in early talks with suit makers about where armor can fit without cramping movement.
Suit of armor:
 Space Armor comes in two versions. 
Space Armor Lite—the version used in the test—stops hypervelocity impacts from objects up to 3 mm in diameter, according to Atomic-6.
Space Armor Max targets objects up to 12.5 mm and is built for human-rated spacecraft and habitats.
Unlike metal Whipple shields, which need 4–6 inches of standoff space—too bulky for a suit—Space Armor is under an inch thick, Smith said. The tiles can also double as a radome, letting RF signals through to protect comms gear, while a second version blocks RF to keep signals from escaping, at the same price and weight.
Trial by fire:
 Last month, a Space Armor Lite tile 
hitched a ride
 to orbit on Portal Space Systems’ Starburst-1 spacecraft to see whether it would survive launch (it did). Smith joked to Portal CEO Jeff Thornburg that he hopes the satellite gets hit—just in the right spot.
“There’s really no way to orchestrate an impact in orbit,” Smith said. “You can’t just call up a space debris strike.”
Achilles’ bullet:
 Unfortunately, if you find yourself duelling with a true space cowboy, Atomic-6 armor still may not be enough to save you. Smith once took a tile to his local gun range and shot it with his Glock 9 mm, and the round went right through. The reason? Bullets are too slow.
“The faster the projectiles are, the better for our system,” Smith explained. “It starts working around Mach 10, and my 9 mm Glock will not shoot a bullet at Mach 10.”

Atomic-6’s Space Armor Lite stopped debris, with astronaut protection assessed
Atomic-6 said the Nov. 20, 2026 test at Texas A&M used a ballistic torso and compared the tile against a 4-mm aluminum plate.

Key facts
Atomic-6 announced proof-of-concept test results on Nov. 20, 2026, showing a Space Armor Lite tile stopped a 3-mm aluminum sphere traveling about 7.2 km/s while a 4-mm solid aluminum plate allowed penetration.
Atomic-6 said the 4-mm solid aluminum plate let the sphere punch through, leaving fragments embedded in the simulated torso.
Atomic-6 cautioned that the stationary torso test did not establish injury outcomes, pressure retention, or space readiness because it involved no functioning spacesuit or life-support hardware.
Commander Chris Hadfield, former International Space Station commander and Atomic-6 advisory board member, said early results are encouraging and further testing may show whether the material adds astronaut safety.
Atomic-6 announced proof-of-concept results on Nov. 20, 2026, at Texas A&M University’s Hypervelocity Impact Laboratory, where a Space Armor Lite tile stopped a 3-mm aluminum sphere moving at about 7.2 km/s while a 4-mm solid aluminum plate let the sphere pass through.
Behind the tile, a ballistic torso from Georgia-based Ballistic Dummy Lab showed no visible damage or penetration. The back of the tile showed no bulging, cracking or shedding.
The company picked the 4-mm plate using NASA ballistic-limit equations, keeping impact angle, shield-to-torso spacing and mounting identical to the tile test. Fragments from the plate embedded in the simulated torso, while the tile weighed 14 kilograms per square meter.
The company cautioned that the test could not establish injury outcomes or pressure retention or space readiness because no functioning spacesuit or life-support hardware took part. It said its findings describe visible damage only under the tested conditions. Trevor Smith said the testing harmed no humans.
“Building Space Armor, we want to protect assets in space,” Atomic-6 CEO Trevor Smith told Payload. He said a human life is the highest-value asset in space. Chris Hadfield, who commanded the 
International Space Station
 and sits on Atomic-6’s advisory board, called the early results encouraging and said more testing may show whether the material improves astronaut safety.
Atomic-6 is in early talks with suit makers about fitting the armor without cramping movement. Space Armor Lite stops objects up to 3 mm, while Space Armor Max targets objects up to 12.5 mm for human-rated spacecraft and habitats. Trevor Smith said the tiles run under an inch thick. He contrasted them with traditional metallic Whipple shields, which need 4–6 inches of standoff room and are too bulky to wear.
Smith said, “Things that are three millimeters or less, it’s called untrackable.” He said on Oct. 19, 2026, that these objects constitute over 90 percent of the debris and move at thousands of miles per hour in low Earth orbit, though tracking systems cannot detect them. The company said on Oct. 21, 2026, that Space Armor addresses concerns tied to Kessler Syndrome. It describes the syndrome as a cascading effect of debris collisions that could render parts of Earth’s orbit unusable.
“We’ve proven Space Armor® tiles can withstand debris traveling at extraordinary speeds without generating harmful secondary fragments,” said Trevor Smith, CEO, Atomic-6. The tiles reached orbit Oct. 21, 2026, on Portal Space Systems’ Starburst-1, which flew aboard a 
SpaceX Falcon 9
 rocket in the Bandwagon-5 mission, marking the technology’s first flight demonstration. Smith joked to Portal CEO Jeff Thornburg that he hopes the satellite gets hit in the right spot, while noting orchestrating such an impact is not possible.
95.5 WSB reported on Oct. 19, 2026, that the launch carrying Starburst-1 moved from Monday morning to Tuesday. Atomic-6 operates from Marietta, Georgia, and WSB Radio said workers built the shield in Atlanta. FOX 5 Atlanta reported on Oct. 28, 2026, that Marietta-based Atomic-6 creates ultralight armor for satellites and that the technology recently flew aboard a SpaceX launch.
