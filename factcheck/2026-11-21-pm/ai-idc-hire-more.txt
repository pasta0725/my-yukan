　AIが業務を担うようになれば、企業が必要とする人員は減り、ソフトウェアの利用者数もそれに伴って減る。調査会社のIDCによると、過去1年間、AIとエンタープライズソフトウェアを巡ってはこうした見方が通説になっていた。
　IDCは2026年11月17日（現地時間）、この通説と食い違う調査結果を公式ブログで公表した（注1）。IDCは、AI投資で50％以上のROIを得ていると回答した組織を「リーダー」と呼び、AIに最も精通している企業と位置付けている。同社の調査「Worldwide Future of Work Survey」（2026年5月）によると、リーダーの過半数が、今後18カ月で自組織の人員が増えると見込んでいる。
AIで成果を出す企業が増やす人員とは？
　IDCによると、同社がROIの水準で3つに分類した組織は、いずれも一部の人員を削減している。リーダーも例外ではない。ただし、リーダーは削減と並行して増員も見込んでおり、その割合は他の分類を上回る。AIの影響を受けやすい職種でエントリーレベルの採用が鈍る中、AIに精通している企業はどの人員を増やそうとしているのか。
　IDCの調査でリーダーが増員を見込んだ対象は、特定の階層に限られていなかった。今後18カ月でエントリーレベル（初級職）の人員が増えると見込む割合は、リーダーが68％だった。ROIがまだプラスになっていない組織（IDCの分類で「ラガード」）は40％、両者の中間に当たる組織（同「アチーバー」）は51％だ。
　中間管理職と上級管理職でも傾向は同じだった。AI（エージェンティックAIを含む）が今後18カ月の人員数に与える影響を尋ねた設問で、中間管理職が増えると回答した割合はリーダーが63％、ラガードが39％だった。上級管理職が増えると回答した割合はリーダーが59％、ラガードが36％だ。
　IDCによると、エントリーレベルの人員を削減すると回答した割合は、3つの分類の中でリーダーが最も低い。リーダーのうち人員の減少を見込む割合はどの階層でも12％以下で、中間管理職と上級管理職では3％だった。IDCは、人員の削減と増員は二者択一ではなく、新たに生まれた役割や職責に投資を移す動きの表れだと説明している。
AIが置き換えるのは「人」ではなく「仕事」
　IDCによると、同社のレポート「Economic Impact of AI: 2026 Edition」も別の観点から、AIが主に置き換えるのは労働者ではなく仕事だという同様の結論に至っている。レイオフが実際に起きていること、AIの影響を受けやすい職種でエントリーレベルの採用が減っていることはIDCも認めている。その上で同社は、市場をけん引する企業はAIを、対応できる業務量を増やす手段として扱っていると分析する。こうした企業は、人員の必要性が下がるのではなく、取り組むべき仕事が増えると見ているという。
IDCがIT部門に勧める3つの対応
　IDCはベンダーとIT部門の意思決定者のそれぞれに向けて、今後の対応を挙げている。IT部門向けは次の3点だ。なお、ここでいう労働力とは常勤の従業員、外部人材、AIエージェントの3種類を指す。
仕事の割り当てを調整する「ワークロードオーケストレーション」の責任者を1人決める
：　ツールと予算は人事、調達、ITの各部門に分かれている。責任者を決めなければ、どの仕事をどの労働力に割り当てるかの判断に誰も責任を負わない
労働力を横断して管理する能力でベンダーを評価する
：　3種類の労働力を横断して仕事を割り当てる能力と、コストの透明性、予測可能性を評価する。成果の測定方法（将来は成果に基づく価格設定）について議論する姿勢があるかどうかも評価の対象にする
AIを前提にした働き方を継続して見直す
：　AIエージェントと、それを使う人の双方で、能力が変化し続けるため
　リーダーとラガードの差は、AI活用の体制にも表れている。IDCによると、AI活用を推進する専門組織であるCoE（Center of Excellence）が全面的に稼働していると回答した割合は、リーダーが63％、ラガードが15％だった。ガードレール（AIエージェントの動作範囲を制限する仕組み）を設けた上でAIエージェントを完全に自律稼働させていると回答した割合は、リーダーが33％、ラガードが20％だ。IDCは、ガバナンスが先で、自律性はその後に続くとしている。
　IDCは、今後注視すべきは人員数ではなく仕事の配分だとしている。常勤の従業員、外部人材、AIエージェントのそれぞれに配分される仕事の割合と、仕事の単位当たりのコストと成果を把握するよう促している。

The last year of conventional wisdom on AI and enterprise software fits in one sentence: AI does the work, companies need fewer people, and software seats shrink along with the workforce. That assumption is now colliding with the data. 
In IDC’s Worldwide Future of Work Survey (May 2026), 68% of Leaders (organizations reporting a positive AI ROI of 50% or more) expect the number of entry-level roles in their organizations to increase over the next 18 months. Among Laggards (organizations with no positive ROI yet), that figure drops to just 40%, and Achievers, the middle group, fall in between at 51%.
It’s hard to ignore the trend line, which clearly suggests that organizations extracting the most value from AI are planning to add people, not simply cut them. While companies across all three of these groups are also cutting certain roles, the seat thesis that AI success means fewer people is clearly being disproven. Our data show that most Leaders expect to grow their workforce.
Leaders are hiring and adding new roles
The pattern isn’t just aimed at entry-level positions. It holds at every level. Asked what impact AI (including agentic AI) will have on the number of roles over the next 18 months, 
63% of Leaders expect middle management roles to increase, versus 39% of Laggards.
 And for senior leadership, the split is 59% versus 36%.  The most telling data point: Leaders are the least likely to cut entry-level roles, and no more than 12% expect decreased headcount at any level, with only 3% in middle management or senior leadership. While companies in all three buckets are cutting some roles, most are adding them, as these are not mutually exclusive decisions. Rather, they reflect a shift of investment into newly developed roles and responsibilities.
IDC’s Economic Impact of AI: 2026 Edition reaches the same conclusion from a different direction: AI will primarily replace work, not workers. Layoffs are real, and entry-level hiring in AI-exposed occupations has weakened. But the leaders of this market are treating AI as a capacity multiplier. They see more work to be done, not less need for people to do it.
Headcount is only part of the planning problem
IDC’s 
Two Years In — A Longitudinal View of AI-Enabled Work 2025–2026
survey (Sept 2026) finds that workload is being spread across three labor pools: permanent staff, contingent labor, and AI agents. The planning challenge is not only how many people to employ. It also includes workload orchestration: deciding which work goes to which pool, at what cost, and under what oversight. This is not a new question, but it takes on particular urgency as more organizations begin to use the language of employment when discussing investment in AI agents. While it may seem like a small semantic matter, treating “managing agents” as if they were employees is actually a much more serious concern. Why? Because referencing agents as an equivalent to human headcount discounts workers as a line item in a budget. Agentic AI is a valuable resource, but counting agents as headcount equivalents measures the wrong thing. Agents are better understood as capacity than as colleagues. The real question is how work gets done, and how work gets allocated across the three pools, not who or what performs each task.
One labor pool per application
The software industry has been here before. When the cloud arrived, the model shifted from perpetual licenses to subscriptions, and the seat became the unit of value because the product was something a person logged into. That logic held for more than two decades. It is now breaking down because a growing share of the work is being done by agents that never take a seat.
Meanwhile, the application stack still assumes one labor pool at a time. Workday manages employees. SAP Fieldglass manages contingent workers. Agent platforms such as Salesforce Agentforce manage digital labor. Each is strong inside its own boundary. None was built to answer the question leaders now face: what work needs doing, who or what should do it, what it costs, and how to optimize across all three?
This is precisely why HCM, workforce management, workflow automation, and agent management are converging. The vendors that connect them will not be selling a feature. They will be defining a category. This is an emerging opportunity to double down on cross-functional workflows that pull focus from an individual doing a task to a cohort of interdisciplinary teams achieving a business outcome.
Price the work, not the seat
Pricing follows the same logic. If Leaders add people and deploy agents at the same time, seat count becomes a weaker proxy for the value software delivers. Swapping per-seat fees for per-agent fees does not solve the problem either, because it only changes what is being counted. The durable metric is work completed. Vendors that can measure and price against workload, across humans and agents alike, will hold the advantage. The important thing is to support clients’ focus on long-term value: driving revenue gains via topline growth, not just productivity and short-term efficiency gains.
What needs to happen now?
Vendors:
Build the ability to calibrate and route work across all three labor pools, or partner to get it, so you serve both short- and long-term goals before a competitor defines the category for you.
Stop leading with “reduce headcount” for buyers who are expanding. Most successful AI adopters are adding roles, so lead with growth and capacity.
Normalize the conversation around outcome-based value metrics, pushing clients to think more holistically about redefining how their businesses are run.
Tech Leaders:
Assign a single owner for workload orchestration. Tools and budgets are split across HR, procurement, and IT today, so without a named owner, no one is accountable for deciding which work goes to which labor pool.
Evaluate vendors on their ability to orchestrate work across pools and provide cost transparency and predictability, and on their willingness to discuss outcome measurements (and eventually, outcome-based pricing). This is what ultimately turns a usage dashboard into a decision tool.  
Ensure ongoing refinement of AI-enabled ways of working as capabilities shift for both agentic tools and the people who use them.
The number that matters
Headcount will continue to dominate the headlines, but it is not the most important number to watch. Instead, pay close attention to the distribution of work: what share goes to permanent staff, what share to contingent labor, what share to AI agents, and what each unit of that work costs and delivers. IDC data show that most Leaders expect an increase in roles at every level, and they are redistributing work and instituting governance that enables greater oversight.
Leaders are building the organizational structure around AI, not just deploying it. Among Leaders, 63% have a fully operational AI center of excellence, compared with just 15% of Laggards. They are also investing in new job roles, and they trust agents with more: 33% of Leaders run agents fully autonomously within guardrails, against 20% of Laggards. Governance comes first, and autonomy follows.
That is not a staffing adjustment. It is a structural change in how enterprises operate, and the software that runs them has not caught up. The window to claim the orchestration layer is narrowing. Vendors that keep counting seats will be measuring a number their most AI-successful customers are already moving past.
Count the seats, and you see the past. Count the work, and you see the market.
