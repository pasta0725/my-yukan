New York is gearing up to enforce one of the country’s most significant AI safety laws. The state opened a registration portal Tuesday, with which large frontier AI developers like Anthropic and OpenAI will be required to register by January 1, 2027. 
The RAISE Act
, New York’s safety and transparency bill, kicks in at the start of the year. It mandates that large frontier developers — companies with more than $500 million in annual revenue — have to publish safety frameworks on their websites, report critical safety incidents within 72 hours, and issue public model transparency reports. 
The newly formed Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust (DIGIT) will implement the RAISE Act and oversee large frontier AI labs. Penalties can be as high as $1 million for one violation and $3 million for subsequent violations.
The state also can bring enforcement actions against companies that fail to register with the new portal, though a spokesperson declined to provide specifics. 
“Tech moguls tell us that their vision for AI is inevitable,” Governor Kathy Hochul said Tuesday during a press briefing. “They tell us get on board or get left behind… That any collateral damage is worth it as long as we win the race to the AI cliff.”
On Tuesday, Hochul also launched the New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research based at State University of New York’s Binghamton University. The center will independently evaluate the safety, security, and operation of advanced AI systems and work with DIGIT to review companies’ safety frameworks. Hochul first announced the center in January with $55 million in donations and capital.
“We’re building a national model for smart, sensible AI regulation, a model that puts communities above corporations and people above powerful technology,” Hochul said.  
The RAISE Act isn’t the only safety law New York has passed. Last year, the state began enforcing 
a law that requires AI companion
 operators to regularly let users know they’re talking to a bot, and to make reasonable efforts to detect suicidal ideation and self-harm.
In October, Hochul signed a 
separate chatbot safety law
 for minors, restricting behaviors like deceptive human-like interactions, emotionally manipulative or sycophantic responses, and encouragement of behaviors like suicide or disordered eating. That law takes effect June 1, 2027.
Other states have also passed similar AI safety and transparency bills to the RAISE Act. California’s SB 53, which took effect in January, mandates that frontier AI labs have in place and publish safety frameworks, enact whistleblower protections, and report critical safety incidents within 15 days. Illinois’ SB 315, which will go into effect at the start of 2027, has similar requirements, plus includes independent safety compliance audits.

New York opens RAISE Act registration for large AI developers
Edited and reviewed by AI Ping Editorial Team

New York State opened an online registration portal for large frontier AI developers on November 10. Companies that develop, deploy or operate covered models in the state have until January 1, 2027 to register if they meet the law’s definition of a large developer, the governor’s office said. That is the day New York’s RAISE Act takes effect. DIGIT, a unit of the state Department of Financial Services, handles registration. New York had set out the November start in September. It has now opened the portal and released the filing instructions.
New York uses both computing and revenue thresholds. A frontier model is a foundation model trained with more than 10²⁶ integer or floating-point operations. The count includes the original training run and work the developer subsequently does to 
fine-tune
 the model, train it with reinforcement learning or materially modify it in other ways. Developers that have used that much computing or begun training with plans to do so count as large if they and their affiliates had more than $500 million in combined gross revenue in the previous calendar year. The law covers models developed, deployed or operating wholly or partly in New York.
What goes to the state and what goes online
The portal requires an NY.gov ID. State guidance says the person submitting information must first establish that they can act for the company. Once the account is authorized, the developer files a disclosure with its main business address, any New York offices, applicable beneficial ownership information and three points of contact. Once registration is approved, the same account handles incident reports, quarterly risk summaries and assessment payments.
The filing comes up for renewal every two years, or sooner if the model changes hands or previously reported information materially changes. The law directs DIGIT to publish a list of large developers that have filed. Contact details for the named representatives are withheld from that list.
Large developers must also post an AI safety framework on their own websites. It explains how they identify and reduce risks and respond to serious incidents. They have to review it at least annually and publish any material revision, with their reasons, within 30 days.
Developers of any size covered by the law must post a transparency report before or when they deploy a new or substantially modified frontier model. It covers the release date, supported languages, output types and use restrictions. Large developers also summarize their catastrophic-risk assessments, what they found and the extent of outside evaluators’ involvement. A model card can carry that information if it covers the statutory requirements.
AI-generated conceptual image: a sealed filing and an open report represent documents sent to the regulator and published for readers · AI Ping
A 72-hour deadline for critical incidents
Once the law takes effect, frontier developers have 72 hours to notify DIGIT from the point at which they establish a critical safety incident or have reasonable grounds to believe one occurred. If they discover an imminent risk of death or serious physical injury, they must notify an appropriate authority within 24 hours. The public can report suspected critical safety incidents starting January 1, the state says.
Large developers separately send DIGIT confidential summaries of assessments of catastrophic risk arising from their internal use of frontier models. The statute calls for a summary every three months and allows a different schedule agreed with the agency in writing. The portal guidance puts the default filing deadline at 30 days after the end of the calendar quarter.
A research network based at Binghamton
Governor Kathy Hochul also announced a statewide safety research effort based at the New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research at Binghamton University. The center, first announced in January, is to draw more than 80 scientists from New York institutions to test advanced systems independently. Cornell Tech’s Greg Morrisett and Columbia University’s Jeannette Wing will serve as interim co-directors.
Researchers will remain at their home institutions. The center will use Empire AI’s shared computing infrastructure for universities and research institutions. Binghamton University says the consortium’s initial computing infrastructure is housed at the University at Buffalo. Alongside testing current models, the center plans to develop measurement methods and standards for evaluating future systems.

The governor’s office said the center can provide technical assessments at the state’s request. One task it identified is working with DIGIT to review developers’ published safety frameworks and propose ways to strengthen the requirements.
