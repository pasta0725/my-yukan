Hera spacecraft in ‘rectangular orbit’ around target asteroids

The Didymos binary asteroid system, as seen by Europe's Hera probe. The main asteroid, Didymos, dominates the frame while just a sliver of its "moonlet," Dimorphos, is visible beneath it. ESA released this image on Nov. 5, 2026. 
(Image credit: ESA)
The 
European Space Agency
's (ESA) Hera spacecraft has gotten its first close-up look at the Didymos binary 
asteroid
 system. If that system sounds familiar, it's because it made headlines in September 2022 when NASA's DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test) spacecraft smashed into it.
The aim of the DART mission was to test whether an impact could divert an asteroid away from a collision course with Earth. The purpose of 
Hera
 is to follow up on 
DART
 by making up-close observations of the Didymos system.
"The first thing we said was, 'Wow,'" Hera Principal Investigator Patrick Michel 
said in a statement
 about the image, which ESA released on Thursday (Nov. 5). "This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART’s camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world."

What is it?
The 
Didymos
 system consists of the eponymous 2,560-foot-wide (780 meters) space rock and its "moonlet" Dimorphos, which is about 530 feet (160 m) in diameter.
The 
DART mission
 impacted Dimorphos on Sept. 26, 2022, with the aim of causing a change in the orbital period of the moonlet. And it worked: Before the impact, Dimorphos completed one lap around Didymos in around 11 hours and 55 minutes. The DART collision reduced the length of this period by around 33 minutes.
The change is a result of the fact that Dimporphos is now traveling at around 2.7 millimeters per second more slowly than it did before the collision.
The newly released photo shows Didymos in detail. Just a sliver of Dimorphos is visible, below and slightly to the left of the main asteroid.
Why is it incredible?
The changes caused by DART were small. But in a real Earth-threatening scenario, even such a tiny shift could help divert an asteroid on a collision course — as long as scientists get enough advance warning of its approach.
Hera's observations should help reveal what other changes have occurred on and around Didymos.
Scientists are already seeing that, while Didymos has the same central ridge along its equator as before the impact, many changes have occurred. This includes the creation of multiple boulders, craters, and patterns of grooves or ridges.
"Since we expect a lot of impact-ejected material to have made it across the approximately 1 kilometer [0.62 miles] of space from Dimorphos to Didymos, it may be that some of these characteristics originate from this transport of material, and we have exciting work ahead of us to sort it out," said Michael Kueppers, ESA’s Hera mission scientist. "We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify."

First close-up asteroid image as ESA spacecraft built by OHB approaches historic rendezvous with the Didymos system
Deep-space deployment rehearsals for cube sats completed successfully
			November 6, 2026		
After traveling millions of kilometers through interplanetary space over the past two years, Hera, the spacecraft built by OHB for the European Space Agency (ESA), has returned its first close-up images of the Didymos asteroid system. The newly released views mark the start of the final and most critical phase of Europe’s first planetary defense mission.
Captured from around 60 kilometers away, the image reveals a previously unseen side of Didymos, the larger member of the binary asteroid system. The asteroid’s rugged surface, marked by boulders, craters, and ridge-like features, offers scientists a first glimpse of the environment Hera will soon investigate in unprecedented detail.
“Seeing Hera’s first close-up images from the Didymos system is a special moment for everyone involved in this mission,” said Marco Fuchs, CEO of OHB. “After years of development, testing, and operations, the spacecraft is now delivering exactly what it was designed to do. These first images demonstrate the outstanding performance of Hera and are only the beginning. In the coming weeks, the mission will provide the data needed to understand the outcome of humanity’s first successful asteroid deflection experiment and help strengthen our ability to protect Earth from future threats.”
The first close-up image, published by ESA, represents an important milestone as Hera continues its final approach toward the asteroid pair. The spacecraft will move progressively closer to Didymos and Dimorphos, capturing increasingly detailed observations of both bodies. High-resolution imaging of the DART impact site is expected during the mission’s rendezvous phase.
Hera demonstrates outstanding performance on final approach
All spacecraft systems have been successfully tested and verified. Most recently, Hera completed rehearsals for its complex rendezvous and proximity operations. Following a series of precise trajectory-correction and braking maneuvers, the spacecraft is now on its final approach path.
The mission team has also completed deployment rehearsals for Hera’s two CubeSats, Juventas and Milani. To support these activities, OHB temporarily reconfigured its Avionic Test Bench, a full-scale spacecraft model equipped with functioning electronics and cameras, from ESA’s follow-on mission RAMSES back to the Hera configuration. The deployment rehearsals were completed successfully. Once released, the CubeSats are expected to provide unique insights into the asteroid’s internal structure and composition.
For European spaceflight, Hera’s arrival marks a major milestone. Launched from Cape Canaveral on 7 October 2024, the spacecraft is heading to the Didymos-Dimorphos system to investigate the aftermath of NASA’s DART impact. In 2022, DART demonstrated for the first time that an asteroid’s orbit can be altered through kinetic impact. Hera will now provide the detailed measurements needed to assess the effectiveness of that technique with unprecedented accuracy.
A record-breaking project made in Bremen and powered by European teamwork
Hera is also the result of an exceptional European industrial achievement. Led by OHB, the spacecraft was developed, built, integrated, tested, and launched in just four years, an exceptionally short schedule for a deep-space mission of this complexity. The mission brings together around 100 companies and research institutions from 18 ESA Member States.
The knowledge gained at Didymos and Dimorphos will help shape future international planetary defense strategies and is already supporting preparations for ESA’s follow-on asteroid mission, RAMSES, which is also being built by OHB and its industrial partners.
The successful development of Hera demonstrates Europe’s ability to deliver highly complex planetary-defense missions on ambitious schedules.
Attention is now turning to the asteroid system itself. The first close-up images already offer scientists a new perspective on Didymos and may provide valuable clues about how material was redistributed throughout the system following the DART impact. As Hera continues its approach, the mission will gather the detailed data needed to transform asteroid deflection from a one-time demonstration into a well-understood and potentially repeatable planetary-defense capability.
The next major milestone will follow on November 25, when ESA unveils detailed images of both asteroids, providing an unprecedented look at the target of humanity’s first successful asteroid-deflection test.

Hera’s 1st closeup view of Didymos and Dimorphos
The European Space Agency (ESA) 
said
 this week (November 5, 2026) that its Hera mission has caught a first glimpse of its target asteroid system. The larger asteroid in this binary system is 
Didymos
. The smaller object – considered a moonlet of Didymos — is 
Dimorphos
. NASA’s 
DART
 mission — the world’s first 
planetary defense
 test — struck Dimorphos back in 2022.
Hera is the follow-up mission, meant to gather details on DART’s strike. The spacecraft was about 37 miles (60 km) away from Didymos when it acquired the image above. Little Dimorphos also makes an appearance in this image, from its hiding place in the larger asteroid’s shadow. Hera principal investigator 
Patrick Michel
 said:
The first thing we said was wow! This is the other side of Didymos from the one that DART’s camera showed us shortly before impact, so this feels like looking at a whole new world. This side of Didymos has the same central ridge along its equator that we saw before, but otherwise appears very different, with multiple 
boulders
 and craters and what look like patterns of grooves or ridges.
ESA’s Hera mission scientist 
Michael Kueppers
 added:
Since we expect a lot of impact-ejected material to have made it across the approximately 1 km (.6 mi) of space from Dimorphos to Didymos, it might be that some of these characteristics originate from this transport of material.
And we have an exciting work ahead of us to sort it out. We look forward to seeing much more of both bodies and systemically mapping all the changes we identify.
ESA said that detailed images of both asteroids will be unveiled on November 25.
Artist’s concept comparing the size of the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos to the 
Colosseum
 in Rome, Italy. Dimorphos is about 560 feet (170 meters) in diameter. The larger asteroid in this system, Didymos, is about a half mile (800 meters) wide, about the size of the 
Golden Gate Bridge
. Image via 
ESA
.
DART mission and Hera mission
In 2022, the groundbreaking DART mission struck and moved an asteroid. It was a test of Earth’s ability to 
defend itself
 from an incoming asteroid threat. 
Read more: When DART struck Dimorphos
ESA’s Hera launched two years later, on October 7, 2024. It’s a follow-up mission to DART. After it arrives, it’ll spend six months investigating the aftereffects from the DART impact. Hera’s main mission is to determine how effectively the impact changed the orbit of Dimorphos. It’ll also look at Dimorphos’ and Didymos’ physical structure. In other words, DART demonstrated that we can deliberately alter an asteroid’s motion. Hera is going back to investigate the details.
Why? Because if an asteroid were headed for Earth, we’d want to know as much as possible about how to knock it off course!
So far in 2026, the Hera mission has undergone a deep-space maneuver to correct its course toward its target. And in July 2026, it finished receiving an upgrade to its software. A braking maneuver on October 15 also worked to get the spacecraft to its target.
When Hera reaches the two asteroids, it’ll release two cubesats, 
Milani and Juventas
. Milani is in charge of 
spectral surface observations
 (splitting light bounced off the asteroid into a rainbow array of colors, then analyzing that light) of both Didymos and Dimorphos. And Juventas will take the first radar soundings in the heart of an asteroid.
According to ESA
 — the mission planner — Hera will conduct a “crime scene investigation” at Dimorphos. But Hera will also check out both Didymos and the dusty environs unleashed by the impact.
In this artist’s concept, the 
Hera
 mission scans the impact crater that DART left behind. Image via ESA.
Here are 3 mysteries that Hera will help solve
ESA 
shared
 three mysteries that Hera will help solve:
Hera will measure the density and composition of Dimorphos in detail and help scientists determine whether it is a “rubble pile” loosely held together by gravity, or a solid core covered in boulders and gravel.
Hera will map the crater created by DART’s impact down to 4-inch (10-cm) resolution to help scientists better understand how the surface material responded to the collision. It’s possible that there is no crater at all, but rather the impact reshaped the entire asteroid!
About 15% of known asteroids are actually binary systems, but their origins remain mysterious. Hera will determine whether Dimorphos and Didymos are made from the same material, which would hint that the rapidly spinning Didymos once threw off debris into space that later formed Dimorphos.
