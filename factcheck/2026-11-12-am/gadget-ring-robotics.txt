　自律飛行するAIドローンが、「自宅警備員」に――米Amazon傘下のRingは11月10日（現地時間）、家庭向け防犯ドローン「Ring Drone Cam」と、企業向けの自律走行型警備ロボット「Ring Rover Cam」を発表した。いずれもAIを活用して屋内を巡回し、センサーなどが異常を検知すると、指定場所へ自ら移動して状況を確認する。米国での価格はそれぞれ約4万円（249.99ドル）、約16万円（999.99ドル）。
プレスリリース
　両製品には、Ring独自のAIシステム「AI Pilot」を搭載する。カメラやセンサー、画像認識AIなどを組み合わせて建物内の間取り図を作成し、巡回地点を提案。移動時には、家具などの障害物を検知して回避する。
　Ring Drone Camは、利用者が飛行経路やスケジュールを設定できるほか、任意のタイミングで飛行させることもできる。Ring製の防犯カメラやセンサー、警報器などと連携し、異常が発生した場所を撮影しに行くことも可能だ。
　また、カメラの画角は148度で、本体は360度回転できる。2K画質での撮影に対応し、暗い場所では内蔵する2つのLEDライトが自動点灯する。飛行していない間は専用ドックで待機し、カメラをオフにする仕組みだ。
「Ring Rover Cam」
　一方、Ring Rover Camは、オフィスや店舗などの巡回を想定した警備ロボット。センサーなどが反応した際の現場確認に加え、定期的な巡回にも対応する。指定した場所に到着すると360度スキャンを行い、周囲の状況を確認する。最大4K画質での撮影・配信が可能で、移動中の映像も録画する。
　専用ドックでの待機中も人物の動きを検知できる。人物を検知した際には、利用者へ通知して録画を開始する。高さを調節できるカメラも搭載し、カウンターや棚の上なども確認可能とした。不審者に対しては、サイレンや警告灯で威嚇できるほか、双方向通話機能を使って離れた場所から声をかける。
　プライバシーへの配慮として、録画中は本体画面の表示やLEDランプの発光を通してその旨を示す機能も備える。
「Ring Drone Cam」
　Ring Rover Camは2026年12月16日、Ring Drone Camは27年1月27日から米国で出荷する予定。ただし、Ring Drone Camは米連邦通信委員会（FCC）の認証をまだ取得しておらず、販売には認証取得が必要となる。日本での発売予定は明らかにしていない。

No one's home
 tonight, but the sensor on your home office window upstairs alerts your phone: “motion detected.” Maybe it's just your cat or a flicker of a passing car’s headlights. Instead of shrugging it off, you ask Alexa to send your 
Ring Drone Cam
 to check it out.
The tiny, 165-gram drone lifts out of its charging dock, buzzing loudly, and autonomously flies upstairs, giving you a first-person view on your phone’s screen as it scans the room. Since it's dark in the house, the Drone's spotlights kick in, making it look like someone's physically in the home and moving around—a 
potential deterrent
 to would-be thieves. All clear? It heads back to its base to recharge.
That's the peace of mind Ring wants to offer with its latest home security solution, which it 
announced today
 for $250, alongside the 
Ring Rover Cam
, a $1,000 roving security robot designed to help businesses monitor warehouses and factories. Both fall under the company's new Ring Robotics brand. The Rover Cam launches first on December 16, followed by the Drone Cam on January 27.
The Ring Drone Cam flies out of its charging station to monitor the home.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
This isn't the company's first at-home drone camera. CEO and founder Jamie Siminoff says he had the idea for a flying sentry in 2016 before Amazon bought Ring. The company previously announced an indoor security drone in 2020—
the Ring Always Home Cam
—and nearly launched it 
last year
. But Siminoff says the team kept running into edge cases that forced them to add more sensors. Those additions required more computing resources and made the drone more power- and battery-hungry, which made it harder to fly. The solution? Move the intelligence off the vehicle and into the cloud.
The Drone Cam has enough smarts to land if it loses internet connectivity, but the rest of its intelligence now sits entirely in the cloud; the drone sends video straight to Ring’s servers, where a custom AI Pilot agent tells it where to go. That lets the drone be lightweight and nimble.
It will avoid people and objects, and if you accidentally stick a finger into the propellers, Siminoff says, you will feel it, but “you'll be fine.” If your cat manages to swat it out of the air, however, that's a different story. A Ring spokesperson tells WIRED customers shouldn't “fly it” if it's damaged and should contact support for help (no word on repairability). The Drone Cam runs a motor health check before take-off every time and won't fly if it detects a problem.
Users can't manually control the drone but can also set up custom flight routes and patrol schedules. It can automatically respond to alerts from security sensors you may have set up in the home, like motion, door, or window sensors. It will not leave your home, as it needs a Wi-Fi network to operate and uses the 
Alexa Home Map
 you may have created with 
existing Echo devices
 to help it navigate. It can fly five to eight minutes per charge. (The final production model will be black, not white like in the photos.)
“There are times when you do want to just be able to see everywhere around the home, but you don't necessarily want a [fixed] camera," Siminoff says. “That's why we invented Ring Robotics.”
Because it doesn't work outdoors and since it's well below the 250-gram threshold, the Drone Cam doesn't need to be registered with the Federal Aviation Administration. Drones have also 
struggled in the US
 after the Federal Communications Commission banned DJI from importing foreign-made drones without certification. But Siminoff says the Drone Cam complies with all US regulations and is assembled in Texas.
But what if a bad actor gets access to the Drone Cam? Siminoff points to the company's 
TAKE technology
, or Throw Away the Key Encryption. It encrypts video in transit and rotates keys every few minutes, but Ring temporarily holds the key to run cloud-based AI features before deleting its copy. That brief 
server-side access window
 makes it less foolproof than true end-to-end encryption. (Every Ring account requires 
two-factor authentication
 to log in.)
The Ring Drone Cam’s charging base.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
Hackers have also invaded 
smart home devices
 and peeked through 
robot vacuum cameras
, 
baby monitors
, and other gadgets, so it's not hard to imagine a similar situation with the Drone Cam. To counter the threat of surreptitious surveillance, Siminoff says, the Drone Cam is loud by design—the extremely loud buzz of its propellers means a bad actor couldn't remotely snoop without tipping off people in the home.
Concerns don't just stem from 
third parties trying to access your devices
, though. Amazon's 
Ring
has been accused
 of normalizing 
suburban surveillance
, partly stemming from the company's cozy relationship with law enforcement, and it has even handed Ring videos 
to cops without warrants
.
Thorin Klosowski, a senior security and privacy activist at the Electronic Frontier Foundation, says a device like the Drone Cam is a personal choice and that people should “think through” how it will make others in the home—or visitors—feel.
“I think the companies who make these sorts of 
surveillance products
 are invested in us 
feeling less safe
 at home,” Klosowski says. “If there is a way to sell a product that helps alleviate the problem they are creating, I think they will do it.”
The Rover Cam is designed to patrol warehouses and factories.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
The telescoping camera pops out to scan the area.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
Ring Robotics' second robot is designed for businesses, not consumers. The Ring Rover Cam might look familiar—it shares many of the same hardware guts as 
Amazon's Astro
, the 
short-lived home robot
 that didn't do much. Amazon had even pivoted it as a $2,350 security robot for businesses 
before eventually bricking them
 10 months after launch.
Siminoff says the robot was great, but the software needed polishing, and people largely wanted to use Astro for security—that's why the Rover Cam ditches the cute face seen on the previous bot. It has the same AI Pilot as the Drone Cam, which helps it navigate through spaces and avoid obstacles. A telescoping 4K camera pops up to monitor its surroundings, and the Rover beams a light circle to the ceiling so you can always glance up to see where the short bot is on the factory floor.
It responds to sensor alerts, can automatically check an area, features two-way talk, and has a built-in siren. It covers up to 5,000 square feet and returns to its charging dock after its patrol ends. (It can do about 30 minutes). It's significantly cheaper than Astro was, but buyers still face the lingering risk that Amazon could pull the plug if Ring Robotics doesn't pan out.

The Ring Rover Cam and Ring Drone Cam are expected to reach customers starting at the end of 2026 and in early 2027, respectively, according to an 
Amazon announcement
 posted on November 10. Amazon describes the devices as relying on an AI Pilot system that uses each device’s cameras along with “specialized vision models, on-device sensors and cloud processing” to plan patrol routes while autonomously navigating around obstacles such as furniture.
Both devices also use that AI Pilot system to digitally map the floor plan of the customer’s home or business. This setup process occurs after the customer carries the Ring drone through their indoor spaces or allows the Ring Rover to do some autonomous exploration.
The AI Pilot can supposedly use that guided- or self-tour information to identify specific rooms and suggest patrol points near side doors, windows, or even sensitive locations such as a cash register in the case of a business.
However, flying and roving cameras with AI-enabled features could introduce new privacy risks for homes and businesses, on top of existing concerns involving fixed Ring cameras installed in room corners or as doorbell cameras.
Return of the Ring drone
Amazon’s Ring Drone appears to be a direct successor to the company’s 
Ring Always Home Cam
 that was only temporarily available for purchase by invitation as a “Day 1 Edition” product in September 2021—even having the same starting price of $250.
But the Ring Drone still requires authorization from the Federal Communications Commission before going on sale. Assuming that comes through, Amazon aims to make the drone available on January 27, 2027, with customers able to register for email updates through the 
product page
.
The drone can supposedly follow a set route and schedule for customers who subscribe to the Amazon 
Ring Protect
 plan. It can automatically fly along those set routes when responding to a notification from a Ring camera, sensor, or alarm.
Onboard camera capability includes a 148-degree field of view and 360-degree rotation with a 2K video resolution. Two LED lights also activate in low-light conditions to help illuminate the scene for the drone video camera.
However, the drone is limited to just 8 minutes of flight time when operating on a “new, fully-charged battery under controlled conditions” according to the product description. It comes with a docking station for charging.
Another caveat in the product description is that the Ring Drone Cam may experience degraded performance when navigating in environments with reflective surfaces such as “polished marble or wet tile,” blank matte walls without any features, or transparent surfaces like frameless glass and mirrors.
Still, Amazon touts the drone as providing an “autonomous way to check on your home that has never existed before,” whether the customer is away or at home. The Ring Drone can be controlled directly from the 
Alexa Home Map
 interactive map that shows all of a customer’s Amazon-linked devices.

Roaming robot
Meanwhile, Amazon has pitched the Ring Rover Cam priced at $1,000 as “an autonomous security robot designed for business.” This product is already 
available for preorder
 for those so inclined and scheduled to start shipping to customers on December 16, 2026.
The company described the Ring Rover as being inspired by customer feedback involving another invite-only beta product, the 
Amazon Astro
 robot designed for home monitoring.
The Ring Rover can livestream and record in up to 4K video resolution when patrolling or while doing a 360-degree scan at a designated location. Videos triggered by doorbell rings, motion events and “Live View” events are stored in the cloud for sharing and replaying for up to 180 days.
Like the Ring Drone, the Ring Rover can automatically respond to alerts from connected Ring devices by following its preprogrammed patrol points. But the wheeled robot is only designed to operate within a single-floor, indoor environment because it lacks stair-climbing capability.
Amazon’s product description also warns that the Rover is not compatible with “curved edges, sunken areas, flooring transitions greater than 1.375 inches (3.5 centimeters), and black glossy floors.”
To help compensate for its ground-based view, the Ring Rover comes with an automatically adjustable mast to peer over counters, shelving and other obstacles. It can supposedly cover up to 5,000 square feet (465 square meters), according to Amazon’s product description.
The Ring Rover also comes with additional security-related features such as a siren and light tower to loudly announce its presence, along with a microphone for two-way audio communication if the customer wants to address someone near the robot.

Privacy concerns rising
Amazon touts the new Ring Robotics products as offering “privacy and security without compromise,” with features such as LED indicators and screen notifications informing customers when video and audio recordings are active. But Ring, as an Amazon subsidiary, has been previously implicated in multiple privacy and security scandals.
Ring was previously charged in a 2023 
Federal Trade Commission investigation
 with allowing employees and contractors to watch 
Ring video recordings of customers’ private spaces
. The FTC also described Ring as having lackluster safeguards that 
allowed hackers
 to access 55,000 US customers’ accounts.
Videos taken by the new Ring Drone and Ring Rover will be protected with Ring’s 
Throw Away the Key Encryption
 that was introduced in September 2026, according to Amazon’s announcement. The digital rights nonprofit 
Electronic Frontier Foundation
 (EFF) has described this as an improvement over Ring’s former default settings, but still well short of end-to-end encryption.
The EFF has also called out what it describes as Ring founder Jamie Siminoff’s 
“surveillance-first-privacy-last” approach
 to Ring devices, including Ring reintroducing features that allow law enforcement to request footage or live-stream access from Ring users’ home security devices.
Amazon’s previous attempt to launch its Ring Always Home Cam drone also led to expanded privacy concerns. 
Ryan Calo
, a law and technology researcher at the University of Washington, 
previously described
 such a flying security camera as providing new ways to surveil others indoors, along with giving companies much greater access to potentially sensitive or identifying details of homes and businesses.
There is currently no mention of the new Ring Robotics products implementing facial recognition or similar AI-powered technologies for identifying people. But starting in December 2025, Ring has allowed owners of fixed Ring doorbell cameras to turn on a “Familiar Faces” feature that uses facial recognition technology on visitors—and that is already the 
subject of a class action lawsuit
.
