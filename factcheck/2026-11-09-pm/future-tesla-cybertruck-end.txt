===== https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/tesla-will-stop-making-the-cybertruck-in-2027
(status 200)
Tesla will stop making the Cybertruck next year, according to CEO Elon Musk, who 
said
 on X that the company will use the factory space to build more two-seater Cybercab robotaxis. 
The decision to wind down the futuristic, steel-clad EV comes after just a few short years of underwhelming sales. When Musk handed over the first few Cybertrucks in late 2023, he predicted Tesla would sell around 250,000 per year. It was supposed to be such a cornerstone of Tesla’s future that, before construction began, the company talked about its Texas factory as the “Cybertruck factory.” But Tesla has instead sold just tens of thousands — total — over the last three years.
Tesla is killing off the Cybertruck at a moment when it is trying to become an AI- and robotics-focused company. A big part of that is its robotaxi ambitions. 
The company has spent the last two months putting Cybercabs on the streets in its hometown of Austin, Texas. With no major incidents to date, the company has said it plans to expand the Cybercabs operations to other cities, starting with Dallas later this month. (Tesla also operates robotaxi versions of its Model Y SUVs in Austin, Houston, and Dallas, as well as three cities in Florida.)
This is the third product Tesla has discontinued in order to fuel those greater ambitions. Earlier this year, the company decided to end production of the Model S sedan and Model X SUV and instead use the factory space dedicated to those vehicles to make Optimus humanoid robots.
First announced in 2019, the Cybertruck was a product that Musk had long obsessed over.
“Probably my personal favorite for the next product is the pickup truck, and we are going to just do an amazing pickup truck,” he said on one of Tesla’s earnings calls that year.
Musk promised the Cybertruck would be radical, inspired by the designs of cyberpunk sci-fi stories like “Blade Runner.” While that certainly bore out in the design, the company overpromised on the basics of the Cybertruck from the jump. During the now-infamous reveal event in 2019, when head designer Franz von Holzhausen cracked the Cybertruck’s windows with steel balls, Tesla said the EV was supposed to start at under $40,000 and have up to 500 miles of range. It achieved neither of those figures.
Instead, the truck was delayed for two years, in part because of the COVID-19 pandemic. When it finally did hit the market in late 2023, Tesla handed over the first few Cybertrucks to customers in a 
short, somewhat odd for Tesla event
 (by the company’s own standards, at least), which saw a poorly-lit Musk standing in a truck bed promising the truck was “the finest in apocalyptic equipment” fit for “late civilization vibes.” The base price had inflated to around $60,000, and the top-end range had shrunk by over 100 miles.
The truck has struggled on the market ever since. The polarizing design never seemed to sit well with buyers, especially at a higher-than-expected price. Then, during its first full year of sales in 2024, Musk became a major financial backer of Donald Trump, which turned the instantly recognizable Cybertruck into a target for protesters. That continued into 2025 as Musk led the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, which fired thousands of federal workers.

===== https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/22/tesla-is-prepping-a-cybercab-onslaught/
(status 200)
Tesla says it is preparing to launch its shiny gold Cybercab in new markets outside its home of Austin, Texas, in a bid to “literally take over every part of terrestrial transportation.” 
“We’re starting with getting people from one place to the other, but we want to basically own transportation of any kind on Earth,” Chief Financial Officer Vaibhav Taneja said on the company’s third-quarter earnings call Wednesday.
Tesla has previously shown a concept version of a multi-passenger vehicle it calls the “Robovan,” but it’s unclear if Taneja’s bombastic comments mean the company is working on other types of transportation.
What was clearer on the call is that Tesla’s Cybercab expansion will begin in Dallas next month and spread to other cities by the end of this year. Tesla already operates robotaxi versions of its Model Y SUV in small fleets in Dallas, Houston, and the Florida cities of Miami, Orlando, and Tampa. The company also said on its earnings call that it will start selling Cybercabs to fleet operators 
by the end of this year
.
Broader expansion of the Cybercab could set up a real fight with Waymo, which is currently the market leader for robotaxis in the United States, with more than 4,000 of them deployed across more than a dozen cities. Tesla has argued that its Cybercab is cheaper and easier to build, which should allow it to undercut Waymo and other robotaxi operators on price. But the company has to reach real scale outside Austin in order to prove that point.
Tesla said it’s ready thanks to a dramatic increase in the number of miles its self-driving cars have traveled in recent weeks. While that may be true, Tesla was mum on Wednesday about how much money the overall robotaxi effort is bringing in. Tesla CEO Elon Musk said the new ride-hailing business won’t be noticeable in the company’s overall financial figures until the second quarter of 2027, “and probably a big deal by Q3 or Q4 of next year.”
Tesla also made a change to its 
letter
 to shareholders that further obscures what little visibility there was into its robotaxi business.
In Tesla’s second-quarter letter, the company included a chart for “Cumulative Paid Robotaxi Miles.” A 
close read of that chart
 showed a 36% decline from the first quarter of the year. In the new letter released on Wednesday, Tesla changed that chart to show “Cumulative Unsupervised Robotaxi Miles” — an indicator of how far the fleet cars are driving, whether or not they have paying riders on board.
There wasn’t much more clarity on the call, either. When asked about paid robotaxi miles traveled, Taneja said the company is “seeing utilization improving,” but didn’t offer specifics.
Instead, Taneja kept most of his comments generally focused on Tesla’s plans to scale the Cybercab. That included praise for Tesla’s engineering team for creating a vehicle that, according to the manufacturing team, is very easy to build — and build quickly.
“They think this is one of the easiest products for them to scale in terms of manufacturing,” he said. “So scaling would not be that much of a challenge.” 
What might be a challenge, Taneja said, is that Tesla needs to make sure it has “all the regulatory approvals” so it can “start deploying the Cybercabs as fast as we can produce them.” That echoes many previous comments from Musk, who has long claimed that “regulatory approval” was the biggest barrier to Tesla’s robotaxi plans, not technical progress.
Musk has always been a bit hazy about what approvals, specifically, he felt were needed. The company has run into a regulatory hurdle this year, though. In September, just hours after Tesla formally launched its Cybercab in Austin, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) 
opened an investigation
 into whether the vehicle complies with current federal motor vehicle safety standards.
All automakers self-certify that their vehicles conform to these standards, and Tesla has claimed that the Cybercab is compliant. But it is missing features that the current standards require, like a brake pedal and side mirrors. The NHTSA 
has proposed
 removing some of these requirements, but that rule change is likely still at least a few months away.
Not every Tesla executive agreed on this during Wednesday’s call.
Asked about the remaining bottlenecks for a wider robotaxi rollout, Ashok Elluswamy, Tesla’s vice president overseeing autonomy, said they are “not purely regulatory.” 
“It’s technology-driven,” he said. “We had initially had the robotaxi service from 6 a.m. to 9 p.m., and then expanded to 10 p.m. to 11 p.m, now it’s going to be pretty soon, [to] 2 a.m. So we’re slowly expanding the availability of robotaxi, and that is driven by the capability of the AI software.”
That could be why Tesla’s service still doesn’t operate on highways, and in certain conditions, although it has been spotted operating in heavy rain many times in Austin — something Waymo’s robotaxis have largely avoided. Elluswamy said Wednesday that Tesla plans to tackle more of these challenging driving scenarios by the end of this year, before scaling further. 
“We just want to make sure that there’s not a single safety incident. That is our operating principle. And even if there’s the slightest possible risk at all, we don’t like to take on that risk,” he said.

Tesla

Cybertruck

cracked glass

I have witnessed a number of stunning, odd, and fantastical moments at Tesla events. But the 
Cybertruck reveal
 was the most interesting — and not because of the postapocalyptic design. It was the crowd.
When the truck rolled out onto the stage, I watched attendees gasp, some in revulsion. There was mumbling (“This certainly can’t be the
 Cybertruck
?” I heard Tesla superfans whisper). And yes, there were cheers. The momentary shock of Musk’s biggest fans was quickly replaced by support, acceptance, and finally, endorsement.
It moved like a wave over the crowd. By the time attendees were waiting in line for Cybertruck rides, the mood had shifted. Everyone was a fan.
That fandom carried over to Cybertruck reservations, which skyrocketed to 1 million, 
Musk
 said during a third-quarter earnings call in 2023. But it never made the leap to actual sales. Industry experts estimate about 71,000 Cybertrucks have been sold since the first deliveries began in November 2023.
And now the Cybertruck is headed to the scrap pile. Tesla will stop making the Cybertruck next year, Musk said Friday.
Was the vehicle too edgy? Maybe, but this was always going to be a niche market. I believe more practical reasons dampened sales. Musk promised the Cybertruck would have a 500-mile range and a price tag under $40,000. It achieved neither, senior reporter Sean O’Kane 
noted in his report
.

I’ve joked here and on the Equity and Autonocast podcasts that the autonomous vehicle industry feels a lot like 2016, at least in terms of the buzziness surrounding the sector.
But a recent conversation with Trucks VC partner 
Puneeth Meruva
 has me rethinking that comparison. There’s evidence that 2026 is even hotter — at least by certain measures — than those frothy dealmaking days of 2016 and 2017. 
Trucks VC, which specializes in early-stage investing, has fielded pitches from 81 new autonomous vehicle startups so far in 2026 — two-thirds more than last year and enough to surpass the boom years of a decade ago.  The VC firm argues in a new 
blog post
 that we’re in the midst of a second, even bigger AV wave fueled by technological breakthroughs. Yes, I’m talking about AI. 
Image Credits:
Trucks VC
“For a long time AV companies were trying to go from what was AV 1.0 to AV 2.0, which is kind of this holy grail end-to-end architecture for AVs,” Meruva told me. “And I think the foundation models now provide a technical architecture that makes those end-to-end systems possible.”
These foundational models are particularly good at generalizing across different environments and scenarios, potentially making AVs cheaper and faster to deploy. The arrival of powerful open source models like Nvidia’s Alpamayo and Qwen’s general-purpose vision-language-action models has made it even easier for startups to get off the ground.
The barrier to entry is lower than ever before. Meruva said Trucks is consistently seeing startups deploy within a few months and with less than $1 million raised. (And while Meruva wouldn’t name names, the firm has invested in some of them.)
Of these 81 startups that pitched Trucks VC, I mistakenly assumed most would be applying their AV tech to construction, warehouse, agriculture, and mining. And yes, those were in the mix. But what surprised me was the growing number of startups pitching pure-play robotaxi businesses. And many are positioning themselves as direct competitors to Waymo. 
As wild and galaxy brain-thinking as that may sound, Meruva thinks there is room for smaller players. 
“In this world, smaller, more niche autonomy companies can compete — maybe they’re not competing with Waymo on a global scale, but maybe they’re competing in specific regions or specific types of routes,” he said.  
And Meruva doesn’t see the pace flattening. He expects even more AV startups to pitch the firm next year.
