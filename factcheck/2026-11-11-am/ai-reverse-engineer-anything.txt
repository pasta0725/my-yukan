“何でもリバースエンジニアリング”生成AIで広がる　ソフトウェアもスマート家電も手軽に改造……ブームに潜む危険

Googleで優先するソースとして追加
　AIを使ってスマート家電のOSに手を加え、AIに生成させたコードに入れ替えて、広告を消したり自分好みの機能を付け加えたりする行為がちょっとしたブームになっている。ソフトウェアの改造が手軽にできるとうたった“何でもリバースエンジニアリング”（REA）というツールもGitHubで注目を集めているが、セキュリティ上のリスクも懸念されている。
ネットで広がる“何でもリバースエンジニアリング”
　AnthropicのAIコーディングアシスタント「Claude Code」を使ってスマートテレビのコードを書き換えたというユーザーは、
Xにこう書き込んだ
。
　「両親のTVのコードをAIに生成させた。Netflixに行かないうちからスポンサー広告が表示されていた。Claude Codeにモデル番号を教えたら、Wi-Fiでテレビを特定し、25のXiaomiパッケージを削除して、新しいランチャーを書き込んでくれた。まるでApple TVみたいになった（笑）」
　別のユーザーは、カナダLululemon製のワークアウト用ミラー「Lululemon Mirror」を復活させて、スマートミラーに改造したと
報告している
。
　このユーザーは、既に販売が終了しているlululemonのミラーを中古で購入し、GitHubで公開されている方法を参照しながらソフトウェアを改造。さらに小型コンピュータの「Raspberry Pi」を内蔵してミラーを制御できるようにし、自分の好きな音楽や動画を再生できる機能を追加したという。表面に時刻や天気予報などの情報が表示されたミラーの写真も投稿している。
AIエージェントで改変容易に
　こうしたコードの改変は従来、スキルがなければ不可能で、かなりの時間も労力も必要だった。それがAIの登場で一変した。
　GitHubで公開されているツール「
REA：Reverse Engineer Anything（何でもリバースエンジニアリング）
」もその一例だ。AIエージェントに登録して指示を出すだけで、既成のソフトウェアの機能を解析できるという。
　プロジェクトページの解説によれば「REAはソースコードがなくてもアプリを解析し、その機能がどんな仕組みになっているかを説明して証拠を見せ、自分のプロジェクト向けのバージョンを構築してくれる」。プロジェクトを評価する指標の一つである「スター」の数は11月5日の時点で42万を超えている。
　REAは「Claude Code」「Codex」「Cursor」「Gemini CLI」「Grok Build」などのAIエージェントで利用できる。解析できる対象にはWebサイトやJavaScript、Android、Windowsアプリなどが含まれる。
　公式サイトでは具体例として、Chromeのゲームに手を加えてスピードを調整できるようにする事例や、Windowsの計算機アプリのボタンの機能を変更する
事例を紹介
している。
AIコーディングに潜む危険
　こうした風潮に対し、質の低いコードの改変が横行すれば、重大なセキュリティ問題につながりかねないと危惧する声もある。
　「AIで複製したソフトウェアには、そのAIを使った人が理解していないバグや、修正方法を知らないバグが含まれることも多い」──米メディア404 Mediaのジェイソン・コーブラー記者はそう
指摘
する。
　そうしたソフトウェアが氾濫すれば「脆弱なソフトウェアが重要なハードウェアにインストールされて、デバイスやネットワークのハッキングにつながる」「ハッカーたちはこのプロセスに侵入し、コードにマルウェアを仕込もうとするだろう」
　コーブラー記者はそう予測し「たとえAIを使っていなかったり、AIがいいとは思っていなかったとしても、その影響は免れない」と警鐘を鳴らしている。

===== https://www.404media.co/reverse-engineer-anything-ai-companies-crack-down-on-the-coolest-thing-people-were-doing-with-ai/
(status 200)
It was a fun few weeks while it lasted. People using both Anthropic’s Claude and OpenAI’s Codex to do AI-powered “reverse-engineering” or decompiling are now hitting guardrails or having their accounts limited while trying to do things like unbrick their internet-of-things devices or remove surveillance ads from their smart TVs. 
Several people have reported triggering “safety checks” in Claude Code that prevented them from doing AI-powered reverse engineering; a 
highly upvoted Reddit post in r/ClaudeCode
 reads “The Dream is dead. Reverse Engineer Anything (REA) is not likely to work with Claude.” A thread in the Reverse Engineer Anything Discord from last week reads, “Claude is refusing to decompile anything,” with multiple users responding saying they have hit similar walls. And X  user log4j, who used OpenAI’s Codex to reverse engineer Samsung TVs, 
reported having their OpenAI
 account 
banned
 for 
violating
 its rules on “cyber abuse.” Other users said they asked Codex to reverse engineer hardware or their own code now say they have either been warned by the company or had their accounts 
restricted
. A project 
called Decomp.wtf
 that made it easier to reverse engineer software and games has also shut down in recent days.
As we reported last month, 
Reverse Engineer-Anything
 has made it easy for people to use AI tools to hack electronics that they own and replace a manufacturer’s software or operating system with an open source or vibecoded one. People have installed new operating systems on their smart TVs, have turned baby monitors into AI assistants, have unbricked internet-of-things devices that were abandoned by their manufacturers, and have installed new software on very old hardware. At the time, I wasn’t sure how I felt about the trend, but as I’ve seen people take ownership and control back over the devices that manufacturers have purposefully bricked, turned into spyware, or have placed artificial limitations on, I have come to see AI-powered reverse engineering of electronic devices as one of the few LLM uses genuinely worth getting excited about. And so big companies have immediately taken steps to restrict this behavior. 
The policy tweaks show that big AI corporations will protect other big corporations when their users find ways to use LLMs to circumvent the artificial constraints that hardware manufacturers put on their devices. AI-powered copyright circumvention is OK with big tech when it’s a massive company scraping the entire internet to build their LLMs, but is not OK when people then use those tools to liberate their internet-of-things devices. It’s not the end of AI-powered reverse engineering, but people suddenly getting locked out of their accounts for asking AI to reverse engineer their gadgets suggests that the future of this practice lies in open weight, local, and uncensored models, making it less accessible to the masses and the people who could probably benefit from it most. 
Tarun Gogineni, an OpenAI engineer, 
tweeted that people
 are not supposed to have their OpenAI accounts restricted for reverse engineering: “these blocks are mostly accidental and a result of difficult decision boundaries; there is no prohibition against reverse engineering with codex. it’s getting fixed,” he tweeted. 
But people who are doing AI-powered reverse-engineering suggest that they are moving away from big companies’ models to alternatives where they aren’t going to have their accounts disabled or have to figure out how to work around AI company guardrails. 
“My general experience about reverse-engineering getting blocked has been more or less 100% correlated with the ‘cyber’ category blocks I have received. My take is that the lab[s] are probably under pressure to not have their models take part in some kind of criminal hacking activities and their cyber guardrails are erring on the side of being too strict,” Matt Brown, principal consultant at Brown Fine Security and someone who has been using AI to reverse-engineer devices, told 404 Media. “I am investing in hardware and trying to stay up to speed because the frontier labs guardrails will randomly change without warning on the same model. For my security research and pentesting work I really don’t want to invest tooling and energy into an ecosystem that will eventually lock me out. These guardrails on the frontier side have come at the same time as open weight models from (mostly) China are getting better and better.” 
Brown posted a YouTube video last week
 explaining how he found hidden Bluetooth commands in an internet-of-things gas monitor attached to his propane tank with the open weight model GLM 5.3. He has explained in 
various posts
 that the guardrails big companies are putting on reverse engineering are why local AI and 
open weight models
 are important.
AI-powered reverse-engineering isn’t going anywhere, but it does look like it’s going to become much harder to use the most popular frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic for it. It’s unclear how much longer it will be possible for you to open an OpenAI Codex window, ask it to find a smart TV hooked up to your network, and tell it to install a new operating system on it. Moving forward, it looks like this type of work will be best done with open source, open weight, uncensored, and local models. That’s a bummer, because one of the things that was so exciting about the Reverse Engineer-Anything trend was how easily accessible it had become.
Last month, researchers and law professors at Cornell Tech, Columbia University, and New York University 
published a paper
 about how cheap, AI-assisted reverse-engineering is challenging big manufacturers’ dominance over the devices their make, and is challenging existing intellectual property law. 
“AI systems can identify hardware components, extract firmware, decompile object code, and clone entire apps. The resulting struggle between technology vendors and reverse engineers will challenge a wide range of laws, including trade secret, copyright, anti-circumvention, computer misuse, and contract,” they write. “The law of reverse engineering was built for a world where opening a device took time, tools, and rare expertise. That friction is vanishing as agents whittle endless, precise picks for the many little locks that fill our digital lives.”
In recent days, people have started modifying older digital cameras with new, vibe-coded operating systems. In a video about hacking old cameras, the 
YouTuber Breno Loves Cameras explains
 why it’s such a big deal and why camera companies don’t like it; that same logic can be applied to any device manufacturer.
“Camera companies have this practice of keeping features to release on newer cameras, and having distinctions [in features] in camera lines,” the YouTuber Breno said in one video. “This is breaking that from those big companies, which are going to lose a lot of money if this becomes a big thing.”
 Through that lens, it’s easy to see why big companies are protecting other big companies. People finally found a thing to do with AI tools that gives consumers a bit more control over the devices they bought; it must be immediately stamped out.
“This vibe-code hacking is only called ‘reverse’ when somebody who lacks a trillion dollars is doing it,” the science fiction author Bruce Sterling tweeted. “When you ‘reverse engineer’ the entire Internet Common Crawl, you just built a nice AI model.”

Discord · Q&A · Show and Tell
See a feature in an app that you want in your own product? Ask your agent to investigate it with REA. It can inspect the app without its source code, explain how the feature works, show the evidence, and build a version for your project.
REA connects your agent to tools for inspecting native binaries, JavaScript and Electron apps, .NET assemblies, and websites. You can also use the same tools from your terminal. Analysis runs locally, and results include the evidence and limitations behind each conclusion.
Setup registers REA with your agent and installs matching workflow instructions. Native analysis can use an existing Hopper, Ghidra, IDA or Kuna installation; setup can optionally install Hopper with approval. Static JavaScript analysis needs no native analysis engine.
Visit the REA website

With Node.js and npm installed, run:

Build RL environments
Give an AI agent a local environment to practice tasks such as finding an issue or creating a folder, without running the original app. Use it for reinforcement learning (RL) or to test how well an agent completes tasks.
REA turns recorded API requests and responses into tools the agent can call. The environment uses the recorded data and supports changes only when the recording confirms their effects.
With REA set up in your agent, replace the example URL and folder tasks below with your own:
Use REA to build an RL environment from my test web app at
http://127.0.0.1:3000.
Cover listing, creating, renaming, and deleting folders. You may create and
remove test folders. Leave existing data unchanged.
Save it in a folder named my-env. Check which operations work and tell me
how to run it.
Use the command line instead
Use these commands if you already have a file containing the app’s recorded browser requests and responses. Running the result also requires AgentEnv and Docker.
rea environment-build ./session.har ./my-env --name my-env --json
agent-env run ./my-env
REA exports a runnable environment with a basic test to check that it starts and responds. Use it with your RL framework to define task goals, add reward checks, and run training.

Desktop app environments
REA can also build an environment of a Linux desktop app, which an agent uses through screenshots, the mouse and the keyboard. From a one-line request, such as "Build me a Gnote environment where the agent adds olive oil to the shopping list note", your agent installs the app on a local build computer, writes the task, exports the environment and checks the task in a fresh computer. The machine that builds it needs Linux on amd64 or arm64, Docker and the agentenv framework version the guide pins.

FAQ
Which agents can use REA?
Any agent that supports local MCP servers. Setup configures the
supported agents
; other clients can use
manual MCP registration
.
Do I need a native analysis engine?
Deep native analysis uses Hopper, Ghidra, IDA or Kuna, depending on the target
and operation. Static JavaScript and .NET inspection work without a native
analysis engine. Setup can install Hopper after approval; Ghidra, IDA and Kuna
use your existing installations. See 
provider setup
for the options and their supported targets.
Do I need to start Hopper first?
REA starts Hopper when an operation needs it. On macOS, a first-run dialog may
ask you to choose demo mode or activate your license. Complete that prompt in the
same user session before your first CLI/MCP operation or unattended job. Open
Hopper manually if needed. See
Hopper startup and troubleshooting
.
What does installing the skill from skills.sh do?
The skill supplies investigation instructions for your agent. Use 
npx rea-agents setup
to register REA's MCP server and install the matching instructions, then restart
your agent. See 
skill-only installation
.
What code does REA return?
Native analysis returns pseudocode and assembly. JavaScript/Electron analysis
recovers modules and their relationships. Your agent uses these findings to
write and test an implementation; the 
showcases
 give worked examples.
Does REA upload my app?
REA analyzes targets locally. Your agent receives the tool results, and its
model provider has its own data policy.
What should I do if I hit a bug?
Update first; a recent release may already fix it.
For a CLI installed with 
npm install --global
 or 
bun add --global
:
rea update
For agent setup through 
npx
:
npx rea-agents@latest setup
If you're using an agent, complete the 
setup refresh
 and restart
it. Retry the same task. If the problem persists, 
open an issue
with your REA version, target type, steps to reproduce and error output.
