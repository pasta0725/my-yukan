　米Anthropicは11月9日（現地時間）、「Claude」のモデルの選び方を公式ブログで解説した。最新モデル「Fable 5.2」に加え、「Haiku 5.5」「Sonnet 5.5」「Opus 5.5」を「Claude Code」や「Claude Platform」上でどのように使い分けるべきか紹介している。
　ブログでは、まず多くのタスクでOpus 5.5を、出力にかけるトークン数を調整する「エフォート」が「中」の状態で試すことを推奨した。用途別では、バグ修正などスコープが明確な作業にはSonnet 5.5、分類や情報の抽出といった単純処理を大量に実行する場合はHaiku 5.5、コーディングや金融、科学分野の高度な課題にはFable 5.2が適しているという。
　モデルを変更する前にエフォートの設定を変えることも有効とした。ただし「高」を超える設定では、上位モデルを使用するのと同等のコストがかかる場合もあるため、上位モデルのエフォートを下げた状態でコストを比較するなど、タスクに応じて検証する必要があるとしている。
　他にも、タスク全体の中で特定の作業を担う「サブエージェント」に下位モデルを割り当てたり、タスク中の難しい課題について上位モデルに助言を求める「アドバイザーツール」を利用したりする手法などを紹介した。
　Fable 5.2は、Anthropicが同日に発表した最上位モデル。前モデルよりもコーディングや視覚情報の処理性能などを改善したほか、出力速度も最大30％向上したという。Claudeの有料プランやAPIなどで同日から提供している。

Which Claude model should you build with? Fable, Opus, Sonnet, or Haiku
Which Claude model to pick, what they cost, and how to use them together.
AUTHOR
Addy Osmani
CATEGORY
Playbooks
PUBLISHED
Nov 09, 2026
READING TIME
15
 min
The current Claude lineup has four generally available models: Claude Haiku 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.2. Claude Mythos 5.2 is our most capable model for cybersecurity and life sciences research, available through our verification programs.
If you build on the Claude Platform or work in Claude Code, you may be wondering which model to use for your common tasks. This article will help answer that question and how to think about the trade-offs.
FIG A
One photo of an alley, rendered in code by Claude Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2. Each image is a Python program the model wrote, calling a drawing engine it also wrote, shown from first layer to finished. Demo by Lance Martin.
TL;DR
The quick answer is that you should start most work on Opus 5.5 at its default effort level, 
medium
.
There's a reason it's the default model in Claude Code. It costs half as much per input and output token as Fable 5.2, and it's the model we recommend starting with for most coding and knowledge work.
From there:
Move down to Sonnet 5.5 for well-scoped everyday work, like a bug fix or a feature built against a spec. Just because you have a clear spec doesn't mean you should use Sonnet, though. A migration or a performance optimization can have clear specs and still be difficult enough to warrant Opus 5.5, or Fable 5.2 for a major one.
Move further down to Haiku 5.5 for the simplest high-volume work, such as classification, routing, and extraction. Haiku 5.5 is also good to use with subagents that work on a larger model's side tasks.
Move up to Claude Fable 5.2 for the hardest technical projects in coding, finance and the sciences. Also move up when Opus 5.5 at 
high
 still falls short, or for long unattended runs.
Before you move models, try adjusting your effort level. It's a finer dial for both cost and capability. Past 
high
, one more effort level can cost as much as a bigger model, so compare both on your own work. If you're past 
high
, test the next model up at a lower effort instead. Conversely, one level down can get you the result you need at a lower rate.
Higher effort helps when the model needs to think longer, call more tools, or check its own work more. Just keep in mind that it also adds latency. Additionally, moving to a higher effort level won't give a model knowledge or skill it doesn't have. For that, you still need to move up a model.

THE TRADE-OFFS
When you choose between models, you're making a trade-off. When you pick a larger model, for example, you are choosing capability over latency. These are the main trade-offs to think through.
Capability and cost per finished task
Generally, bigger models cost more per token and are more capable, but the cost vs capability trade-off isn't universally true at the task level.
A higher price per token doesn't always mean a higher cost per task, or a better result. As we showed in 
Spending your effort
, on Anthropic's runs of Terminal-Bench 3.0, the previous Fable, 5.1, at 
max
 scored 58.0%, while Opus 5.5 at 
high
 scored 58.9% with half the tokens.
On the hardest work, the price gap can close. On FrontierCode 2.0, Fable 5.2 at 
high
 scored about level with Opus 5.5 at 
max
 (45.3% vs. 45.5%) for roughly half the cost per task. So before you raise Opus 5.5 past 
high
 on a hard task, test Fable 5.2. For everyday work, Opus 5.5 at 
medium
 is still the place to start. For benchmark results, see the 
launch post
.
However, the Claude Platform's 
cost and intelligence measurements
 also show the limits of a small model. These generally do well on high-volume work with checkable outputs and are less suited to long agentic loops.
When you compare two models, price the hardest tasks in your set as well as the typical ones, because the hardest tasks carry most of the spend. On one 20-problem search benchmark, two problems carried 43% of the spend.
FIG C
Fable 5.2 shows how a game engine builds a world. It draws one 3D scene in sixteen looks, from wireframe and pixel art to ray tracing, and labels each technique. Demo by Dan Fein.
Latency against accuracy
A faster setup finishes sooner and can miss more. Think through how long a person can wait before you pick your model and effort level. For replies a person waits on, start Sonnet 5.5 at 
low
 or 
medium
.
Often next generation models are faster than their predecessors. Fable 5.2 generates output up to 30% faster than Fable 5.1. Sonnet 5.5 runs 30% faster than Sonnet 5.
Effort level and latency are roughly correlated. Determine if the accuracy you gain is worth the extra latency. For example, on SWE-bench Pro, Opus 5.5 at 
medium
 scored about 2.5 points lower for about 70% of the cost as the 
high
 effort setting. At 
low
 it scored about 8 points lower for about a third of the cost. At 
xhigh
 it scored about 1.4 points higher for 2.5 times the cost of 
high
.
Often providing a means of self-verification is more cost effective and adds less latency than changing effort levels. When a model isn't good enough for a task, take these steps in order:
Check that the model can verify its own work, with a test, a build or a script that calls the endpoint.
Raise effort one level.
Move to the next model up, starting at a lower effort level than you used on the smaller one.
If you can check outcomes automatically, run everything at a low level and re-run only the failures at a high one. On the same SWE-bench Pro subset, Opus 5.5 at 
low
 with failures re-run at 
high
 passed about 97% of tasks for about $0.17 each, against 95.3% for $0.29 with every task at 
high
.
If you need a faster reply from the same model, you can pay for speed instead of giving up capability. On the Claude API, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 both offer 
fast mode
, with higher output speed at premium pricing.
Context against cost
Every model in the current generation has a 1M-token context window. Large context can help the model complete the task but it costs you on every turn, because each turn resends it. That being said, 
on long runs most of your input bills as cache reads
.
Opus 5.5 charges $0.20 per million tokens for a cache read, and Sonnet 5.5 charges $0.10. Fable 5.2 charges the same $0.20, so on a long, cache-heavy run the gap between Fable and Opus comes down to uncached input and output, where Fable costs twice as much per token.
In 
What a task costs on Opus 5.5
, a turn's cache read costs about $0.03 at 150K tokens of context. At 20K it costs about $0.004.
One takeaway from this is that a lean main context matters more the bigger the main model is. Keep the expensive model's context for the decisions, and let cheaper subagents do the verbose reading.
Haiku 5.5 is the only model where the token price varies by prompt length. A prompt of up to 100K tokens bills at the lower tier, and a prompt over 100K bills at five times that rate. The long-prompt tier still costs a quarter of Sonnet 5.5's per-token price. So Haiku 5.5 pays off most on short prompts, where it costs a twentieth of Sonnet 5.5's input price.
When every call needs a long prompt, compare Haiku 5.5 with Sonnet 5.5 on cost per finished task, not list price.
MIXING MODELS
FIG D
One project, four models, each on one kind of work. Claude Fable 5.2 moves an online shop from JavaScript to TypeScript, and Opus 5.5 builds a monthly subscription. Sonnet 5.5 writes the product pages, and Haiku 5.5 sorts customer messages. Opus 5.5 starts its feature once Fable 5.2's new code is in. Demo by Dan Fein.
Switch at a natural break
The prompt cache belongs to the model that wrote it. Your first turn on a new model pays the cache-write price on the whole conversation. To make that turn smaller, run 
/compact
 first in Claude Code, or start a fresh session with a short written plan. On the API, open the new model's conversation with a summary of the work so far instead of the full history.
Each thinking block records the model that produced it, and each model reads only some other models' blocks. On the Claude API and Claude Models on Google Cloud (Google managed), Opus 5.5 reads Sonnet 5.5's and Haiku 5.5's thinking blocks. Sonnet 5.5 reads Haiku 5.5's. Fable 5.2 reads Opus 5.5's, Sonnet 5.5's and Haiku 5.5's. On the Claude API, Fable 5.1 reads Opus 5.5's.
A move down a tier loses the earlier reasoning, because a model never reads the blocks of a model above it. Other moves can lose it too. When the new model can't read the earlier model's thinking blocks, the API drops them and the request still succeeds. The new model then works without that reasoning.
Preserved thinking
 lists every case. A move from Fable 5.2 to any other generally available model drops its reasoning.
Put subagents on smaller models
A subagent runs in its own context window and hands back a summary. You pay for its tokens at the rates of the model it runs on. In Claude Code, set 
model: haiku
 or 
model: sonnet
 in a subagent's definition. The 
CLAUDE_CODE_SUBAGENT_MODEL
 environment variable sets the default for subagents that don't specify a model.
Save this as 
.claude/agents/log-reader.md

Keep small models on work where a mistake is cheap to spot, like finding files, running tests and reading logs. Keep code edits and judgment calls on the main model. When a small model misreads a search result, the main model opens the wrong file and spends tokens on it.

Explore

Claude Code also has the 
opusplan

opusplan
 on your own tasks before you make it your default.
Escalate hard decisions to an advisor
The 
advisor tool
 (beta) pairs a faster, cheaper executor model with a stronger advisor model. The executor runs the task and can ask the advisor for guidance partway through. Most of the tokens bill at the executor's rate. The advisor helps only when it adds capability the executor lacks and the executor asks for advice, which it does less often at low effort.
Where it might pay off: agentic work where most turns are routine. The platform's cost guide is cautious: of the four pairings in its advisor table, none clearly beat its executor alone, and an advisor can help only when the executor consults it. Measure your executor's consult rate first. Check which advisors your executor accepts. A Sonnet 5.5 executor accepts Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Opus 5 and Sonnet 5.5, for example, and a Fable 5.2 executor accepts only Fable 5.2 or Mythos 5.2.
Where it adds little: one of those pairings was an Opus 5.5 executor at 
high
 with a Fable 5.1 advisor on an internal coding benchmark. It scored 90.1% at $2.92 per attempt. That's 1.7 points over Opus 5.5 alone at 
high
 for about 2.1 times the money. A gain that size is at the edge of run-to-run noise, and about what raising effort would have bought.
Delegate bulk work to cheaper workers
Subagents, above, handle side tasks while one main model keeps the thread. This pattern is for work bigger than one context window. A top-tier model leads, splits the task, hands the pieces to many cheaper workers in parallel, and merges what comes back. On an internal defect-search benchmark over about 22 million tokens of source code, an Opus 5.5 lead over Sonnet 5.5 workers, up to 25 at a time, cost about a third less than Opus 5.5 alone at its default effort. It scored about the same.
On the Claude Platform, 
multiagent orchestration
 in Claude Managed Agents gives you a coordinator agent and a roster of workers. Skip it for one chain of dependent steps or for work that fits in one context window. There, the planning and merging cost more than they save. 
When to fold subagents into your main agent loop
 covers when one agent loop beats a team of subagents.
CHECK COMPATIBILITY BEFORE MIGRATING
Model choice is mostly a question of capability and cost, but you can't always change the model name and nothing else. Every current model supports tool use and image input, but there are differences to test before you move a request from one model to another.
Fable 5.2 extends preserved thinking to every API account.
 The API rejects a request with a 400 error when anything before a Fable 5.2 thinking block has changed, including the system prompt, the tools or an earlier message, unless you opt in to dropping the affected thinking. If you build requests through Claude Code, the Claude Agent SDK or Claude Managed Agents, you don't need to change anything. If your own code edits history, keep it append-only, and test before you switch.
Thinking.
 Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2 always think, and a request that turns thinking off returns an error. Sonnet 5.5 thinks by default, and 
between_tools
 limits thinking to the gaps between tool calls. Haiku 5.5 thinks by default, and you can turn thinking off at 
high
 effort or below. On all of them, read content blocks by 
type
, because a response can begin with a thinking block.
Forced tool use.
 A 
tool_choice
 of 
any
 or 
tool
 returns a 400 error on Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.2. Send 
auto
, mark the tool 
strict: true
, and say in the prompt when to use it.
Computer use.
 Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5, Haiku 5.5 and Fable 5.2 support computer use only through the 
computer_toolset_20260801
 toolset on the Claude API and Claude Models on Google Cloud (Google managed).
Haiku 4.5 to Haiku 5.5.
 Replace 
budget_tokens
 with an effort level, and drop non-default 
temperature
, 
top_p
 and 
top_k
 values, which return a 400 error. End 
messages
 with a user turn, because a final assistant turn for the model to continue also returns a 400 error.
Safety fallbacks.
 Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.2 run safety classifiers that most often flag cybersecurity and biology content. In Claude Code, a flagged request can re-run on an older model, and a notice in the transcript tells you which. Fable 5.2 keeps the fallback models that Fable 5.1 uses. Flagged cybersecurity requests fall back to Opus 4.8, and flagged biology requests to Opus 5.
If one system runs several models, build every request for the strictest of them: keep history append-only, send 
tool_choice: auto
 with strict tools, read content blocks by 
type
, and write prompts and tool descriptions precisely enough for the most literal model. Then a step can move up or down a tier without a code change. When the routing rule is simple, such as one model for each step type, let code pick the model rather than asking a model to.
MEASURE ON YOUR OWN WORK
Published results, like the SWE-bench Pro and Terminal-Bench numbers above, get you a starting model. Then test the candidates on your own tasks before you commit.
Pull a sample of real tasks. Write an outcome check for each one, such as a passing test, a closed ticket or a correct row count.
Run each candidate model at two or three effort levels, each in its own session so that the runs don't share a cache. Record the cost of each completed task from the 
usage
 field of each response.
Plot score against spend. Keep a multi-model setup only if it beats every single-model point on that char

Anthropic says the new models are a step above Opus 5.5 in almost all the capabilities it evaluated, and up to 30% faster than the versions they replace. Fable 5.2 costs $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10 and $50. On a test built from real freelance projects, it did at least as well as a human professional 41.3% of the time, about double Opus 5.5's 21.3%. It didn't win everywhere: Opus 5.5 rated higher on two knowledge-work measures, and Google's Gemini 4 Carbon beat it on a test of business workflows.
In testing, Mythos found a new way to predict the structure of capsids, the protein shells that deliver gene therapies. Anthropic also says that "over 90% of the changes merged into Anthropic's core codebase are now written by Claude (with human guidance)." A new study tackles a subtle feedback loop in hospital AI: when readmission-risk algorithms influence discharge timing, the data used to retrain those algorithms gets skewed, entrenching bias over time.
