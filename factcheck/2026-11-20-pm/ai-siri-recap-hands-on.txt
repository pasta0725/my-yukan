Victoria Song
is a senior reporter and author of the 
Optimizer
 newsletter. She has more than 13 years of experience reporting on wearables, health tech, and more. Before coming to The Verge, she worked for Gizmodo and PC Magazine.
Was it worth it?
I’ve been asking myself this quite a bit over the last month or so while testing both the Amazon Bee Frontier and Siri Recap on the Apple Watch Ultra 4. Both enable a wearable to ambiently listen to your conversations and, using AI, summarize them for you. Why? So you can remember better and, hopefully, stay more present.
The main difference is that Amazon’s Bee takes a 
maximalist
(and frankly reality-bending)
 approach, whereas Apple is going for a more minimalist, privacy-first take. Both, however, require explicit disclosures to the people around you about recording what they say, and potentially follow-up explanations about how exactly this all works. This past week, I’ve been testing the Siri Recap beta to see if Apple’s is a less dystopian experience.

Apple presents Siri Recap as a sort of backup notetaker. As a college student, perhaps you’re less concerned about writing down what a professor says and can just take in the lecture. Say you attend a parent-teacher conference solo. You can focus on the discussion and then whip out your phone later to reference what was said when explaining it to your spouse.
These are fairly innocuous scenarios. But given the discourse around privacy and surveillance gadgets this year, it’s easy to imagine how always-listening devices could be misused. Apple has taken great care to create a version of this that at least considers that bystanders may feel uncomfortable knowing someone else’s Apple Watch may be taking notes on what they say.
There are several customization options for when Siri Recap is turned on or off.
The feature isn’t turned on by default — you have to opt in. Users can then program when the feature turns on. For example, only at the office between the hours of 1 to 2PM on weekdays. Unless saved, all recaps are also set to self-destruct after your choice of one, three, or seven days. Within the summaries, there’s no viewable transcript detailing who said what and when. That makes these recaps more difficult to use as “receipts” for arguments, court cases, or blackmail. There’s also no raw audio file that’s ever saved. Not to get too into the weeds, but the audio gets processed and then immediately deleted through a secure exclave within the 2026 Apple Watch’s S11 chip that’s inaccessible to Apple and other third parties. The vast majority of the processing is also done locally. Whatever does get sent to the cloud is done through Apple’s Private Cloud Compute. Because the feature requires this hardware, it’s impossible to use on older Apple Watches.
It’s not perfect. Unlike Live Rewind, another audio intelligence feature, there’s no outward indicator or audio chime to signal recording intent. So, if you 
do
 decide to ethically disclose your usage, you have to be prepared to explain everything I just described if someone asks questions.
Maybe that’s worth it — if Siri can be trusted to be truly helpful.
This was a TikTok. My spouse would NEVER.
The reality is that better privacy often means a less useful AI product. The more AI knows about you, the better the summaries from these devices tend to be. To preserve privacy, Apple prevents Siri from learning everything about you and the people in your life. For example, Siri Recap doesn’t train on your voice. Excellent for privacy! But that also means Siri has a much harder time discerning when you’re actually having a conversation. That leads to some funny but ultimately unhelpful recaps.
For instance, I was watching a TikTok in which a chronically ill creator was updating their followers about being sent to the emergency room because of sepsis. Siri recapped that video as if I, or potentially someone I knew, had been sent to the ER. Another TikTok weighing in on an AITA Reddit thread got summarized as if I had discussed a shady homewrecker named Dana trying to seduce her bestie’s husband by wearing a glittery blue bikini top at a concert. Because attribution was stripped, the summary could read as if 
I
 were the guilty spouse returning between 2 and 3AM, covered in Dana’s glitter. In both instances, the lack of attribution also made the resulting summaries hilarious and confusing.
Also, neither video was something I wanted or needed to remember. I suspect Siri summarized them because I was playing the audio out loud on my phone, which sits pretty close to my Apple Watch. There’s an easy workaround here. Siri Recap automatically turns off if you use headphones, meaning I could just eliminate recaps from podcasts, audiobooks, TV shows, etc., by always wearing my AirPods. But that’s me adjusting my behavior to accommodate Siri’s inability to discern when 
I’m
 talking.
Another quirk: Siri doesn’t know what 
I
 want to remember or find memorable from a given conversation.
I turned on Siri Recap during my briefing and demos for the new smart glasses from Google, Samsung, Gentle Monster, and Warby Parker. When I sat down to write, the resulting recap helped me remember the main topics discussed. I also found it useful that a handful of interview recaps were linked to a corresponding Voice Memos file. The thing is, Siri Recap heavily prioritizes restating the facts. That means bullet points tend to emphasize statistics, factoids, and dates. That’s useful in straightforward planning meetings, but less useful for what 
you
 might personally find to be a salient takeaway. For example, in an interview with a source, what I found most interesting was a personal anecdote and some strong opinions about RFK Jr. My Siri Recap from that interview omitted it in favor of more concrete details.
I am not having ethical dilemmas over funding my independent documentary about a wealthy former athlete. This is the plot of an episode of The Rise and Fall of Reggie Dinkins.
Photo: Amelia Holowaty Krales / The Verge
Meanwhile, personal conversations are rarely as straightforward. Work meetings often have agendas. Chats with friends and family can jump across a zillion topics in the span of 10 minutes. Plus, most of my conversations 
should
 be deleted from my brain to make room for the really important things. I didn’t need to see a detailed summary of what my spouse and I thought of the last episode of Nathan Fielder’s 
The Curse
. (It was bonkers.) I’d love to never revisit a 20-minute argument with my cousin over hardwired versus battery-powered smoke detectors. (Too bad, the recap outlined the main points.) A recap of my spouse and me debating movie recommendations 
might
 be a useful recap, but it just says “seeking less anxiety-inducing media” and “refusing to ever watch 
Star Wars
.”
My recaps were also a lesson in why conversational context matters. Sometimes, Siri correctly assumes that gossip is irrelevant to the broader conversation at hand and omits it entirely. Other times, its blunt, matter-of-fact recaps of jokes or my friends venting read like an unhinged Regina George from 
Mean Girls
 building an AI-generated Burn Book.
That inconsistency is why relying on AI to make judgment calls on your behalf feels unsettling.
I keep getting stuck on what problem these features are meant to solve. Tech companies usually say ambient listening is meant to help jog our memories or to help us stay more present. Which brings me back to my initial question: Was it worth it? Did Apple’s efforts to protect privacy prove we can have our AI note-taking cake and eat it too?
Outside a few work-related scenarios, I haven’t found Siri Recaps to be useful. Even writing this, I spent a lot of time scrolling to find a small handful of examples that I would want to discuss. On the plus side, I’m not terribly worried about people snooping through my recaps. Of the more than 50 recaps generated, I’m confident 90 percent would be gibberish for anyone 
but
 me.
Conversely, I found more of what Amazon’s Bee generated to be “useful.” But after seeing Bee’s raw transcripts and summaries of every spoken moment in my life, I believe we’re not meant to live that way. I’ve since nuked all my Bee data.
This is a mix of a Last Week Tonight episode and a conversation between my spouse and I. 
Photo: Amelia Holowaty Krales / The Verge
But even if 
I
 prefer Apple’s approach for privacy reasons, my gut says this tech isn’t the answer for jogging memories or staying present. Whenever I’ve forgotten things, the negative consequences have rarely — if ever — led to irrevocably changing my life for the worse. Usually, it’s a bitter lesson that’s forced me to grow and adapt. And anytime I’m not present in the moment, it’s usually because I’m on my phone.
Ambient listening wearables can be helpful when used in low-stakes, straightforward situations where everyone consents. But for me, it’s often in little ways that have many less-invasive alternatives. I mean having to explain to the less tech-savvy people in my life that my Apple Watch now has this feature that recaps our conversations. Having to watch how their faces mull it over. Having to muster the will to answer their questions about how Apple handles privacy with this feature versus other devices I’ve tested. Having to smile when my spouse jokes about censoring their humor because of my job. Having to write in my work Slack that when my coworkers see me in the office, I am testing AI devices that may be recording any conversation, and that I am happy to turn them off if it makes them uncomfortable. Having to weigh whether I have enough bandwidth or time before every conversation with strangers to be thoughtful and ethical.
Right now, there are no established cultural norms telling me how to easily pay this cost. All I know is that if I turn the feature off, I don’t have to think about it at 
all.

My wife and
 I were driving back from a date night. She was talking about a friend's very expensive utility bill, but I had missed the beginning of what she said (I was driving, after all).
Then I remembered: 
Wait, I can ask Siri to remind me!
 At a traffic light, I double-tapped the digital crown on the 
Apple Watch
, and Siri's new Live Rewind feature spun back the clock 15 seconds and showed me a transcript of what my wife said.
I sheepishly interrupted her to show her the transcript. “Look!” Her reply? “Why wouldn't you just ask me to repeat myself?” She shrugged at this new Apple Watch capability and continued talking.
A few days later, I was chatting with my brother on my stoop. Once again, I lost focus for a few seconds and remembered to ask Siri to help me recall what my brother said moments ago. But this time, before I excitedly showed my brother the Apple Watch screen, I preemptively said, “Yeah, I know I could just ask you to repeat yourself.” I got a perplexed look back.
Live Rewind, one of Apple's new 
Audio Intelligence features
 on its latest Apple Watches, is designed to give you a transcript of the last 15 seconds of a conversation. It's in public beta in watchOS 27.3, but is expected to launch officially before the year's end.
Coming alongside it is another new capability called Siri Recap: Give the assistant permission to listen to your environment—you can set schedules or have it trigger when you reach the office—and you'll see a “Recap” in the Siri app at the end of the day. It’ll show you summaries of conversations and list out important details that people said. It's very similar to 
AI notetakers
, many of which run on dedicated hardware devices.
The power here is that it all happens on your Apple Watch, the same device you wear everywhere to track your fitness, monitor health, and check notifications. That ubiquity is exactly what makes privacy advocates uneasy—they say features like these normalize 
ambient surveillance
, much like 
smart glasses
, undermining the privacy of 
bystanders who don't consent
 to being recorded.
“One of the concerns that we have with things like the Apple Watch is we don't know what the watch is picking up on,” Reem Suleiman, senior campaign director at the nonprofit digital rights advocacy group Fight for the Future, tells WIRED. “Just because the device wearer is the one opting in to use the feature, you're not getting consent from everybody who you walk around.”
Apple has spent a great 
deal of effort explaining
 how the system works and how the company plans to 
maintain the privacy
 of its users and the people around them. First, these features are opt-in, and they are limited to the latest 
Apple Watch Series 12
 or 
Apple Watch Ultra 4
. They also require an iPhone 16 or newer with 
iOS 27
 and 
Apple Intelligence
. These features are not available for users under 18, and they currently work only in English.
For Live Rewind, the audio is processed on-device and cannot be accessed. When you activate it, a small chime plays from the Apple Watch that serves as a warning for folks nearby. Text disappears unless you choose to save it to Siri. For Recaps, Siri keeps them factual, doesn't identify speakers, and also omits content the system deems sensitive. If you don't save a Recap, it'll self-delete in seven days. Apple uses its 
Private Cloud Compute
 processing system for the data, which is never stored or logged on its servers. Data stored in the Siri app is 
end-to-end encrypted
 when syncing with iCloud.
Siri Recaps are stored in the Siri app and will self-delete in seven days if you do not save them.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
Even if you're happy with Apple's privacy focus, I haven't found these capabilities tremendously useful over the past week. Live Rewind works well in moderately quiet environments, but the transcript can quickly turn into inaccurate gibberish when there's a lot of background noise.
Activating it in front of the person you're chatting with feels awkward. It comes across like pulling out your smartphone when someone's trying to talk to you—a little rude and distracting. Instead of keeping screens out of sight—a 
big digital detox trend
—you're introducing one in the middle of a human conversation. I brought the topic up with my wife again, and she echoed some of those feelings, saying it felt like a feature that would stunt a person's social skills. Instead of asking someone to repeat themself, they're bringing an Apple Watch into the mix to fill in the gaps.
I am not saying it can't be helpful. When it works, it feels like a superpower. Maybe there are certain situations where you don't want to interrupt a group conversation and ask someone to repeat themself. Right now, though, there's the problem of remembering that this feature exists and remembering to use it. I have also found the 15-second rule a little too short, and it resulted in me missing parts of the conversation.
Recap has been a little more helpful. For testing purposes, I've set Siri to capture recaps all day, every day, but I like that you can create a custom schedule or have it trigger when you arrive at a specific location, like the office. You can manually turn Siri Recap on or off in the Apple Watch's Control Center. Most impressive is that, despite having Recap enabled constantly, I haven't seen a meaningful dip in battery life. I've continued charging the Apple Watch Series 12 after a day and a half.
Siri Recap logs summaries of ambient conversations.
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
One of the more perplexing things was opening the Siri app to look at my recaps and finding a bunch of conversations I had no recollection of having. Turns out, Siri was recording media I was watching, like on YouTube or Instagram. This is a problem I encountered with AI notetakers, like 
the Amazon-owned
Bee AI wearable
, which claimed to be working on “liveness detection” to solve it. Its latest hardware shipped with a feature where it 
doesn't record a conversation
 if it doesn't detect the wearer's voice within a set period. Hopefully, Apple can figure out a solution. For now, you can edit or delete these recaps.
One day, I forgot to record an interview (though I took notes), and I knew the person said something that I hadn't written down. I headed over to Siri and found exactly what I was looking for—paraphrased, but there. That said, the Recaps aren't designed to be detailed summaries and action plans from your meetings or conversations, unlike dedicated AI notetakers. They're meant to jog your memory. At times, I wish Siri were more detailed, but if that's what you're after, you'll have to invest in a third-party device.
Recap can be helpful for work meetings, but I haven't personally found it all that compelling in my personal life. Looking back at what it has picked up over conversations with friends and family, the only thing I feel is: 
Yep, those conversations happened
. A friend was talking about their woodworking hobby—they can't use oil-based polyurethane as a finish in the winter months as it stays tacky. Siri logged this in such a matter-of-fact way, but when I revisited the conversation in the recap, I didn't find it helpful, useful, or interesting. (No offense, friend!) It's just nuggets of life.
Apple has pulled off impressive feats with these features, capturing these moments in a way that feels a little more privacy-preserving than competitors, without tanking the already limited battery life of its smartwatches. But socially, we're still figuring out what it means to walk around with always-on passive recorders. For now, I'm happy to stick with my wife's advice and ask: “Come again?”

Audio Intelligence features are private by design, combining hardware, software, and services to protect user privacy in a way that only Apple can. These features do not create or store audio recordings, and the raw audio used for processing is completely inaccessible to the operating systems, apps, the user, or Apple. This is because the S11 chip on Apple Watch Series 12 and Apple Watch Ultra 4 includes Secure Exclave, a dedicated hardware-isolated compartment that processes audio in complete isolation from the rest of the system, then immediately deletes it.
Users are in control and can choose whether to opt in to each Audio Intelligence feature. They can decide exactly where and when Siri Recap takes notes, and can turn Siri Recap on or off at any time, right from Control Center.
By design, Audio Intelligence features do not identify and attribute speakers, to protect the privacy of both the user and those around them. Siri Recap produces a brief, high-level summary of conversations — not a transcript — and is designed to exclude sensitive information like financial information or government-assigned identifiers. Live Rewind plays an audible chime when it is being used, even if Apple Watch is on silent, and shows a full-display animation and microphone indicator so people nearby can hear and see that the feature has been activated.
Live Rewind text snippets and Siri Recap summaries are end-to-end encrypted in the Siri app with iCloud syncing, and are not accessible even by Apple.
