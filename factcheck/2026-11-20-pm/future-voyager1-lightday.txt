PASADENA, Calif. – NASA's Voyager mission team cheered and wiped away tears here on Thursday (Nov. 19), celebrating a big milestone — 
Voyager 1 just reached one light-day from Earth
, something no other spacecraft has ever done. The excitement persisted despite a communication anomaly that cropped up on the probe, which the team remains confident is temporary.
Voyager team members gathered for a press conference about the mission at the Von Karman auditorium here at NASA's 
Jet Propulsion Laboratory
 (JPL) — the first such Voyager event since 1989, when Voyager 1's twin, 
Voyager 2
, flew past Neptune. The gathering served to celebrate 
Voyager 1
's distance milestone: At 5:16 a.m. EST (1016 GMT) on Wednesday (Nov. 18), the probe reached one light-day from Earth — the distance a photon travels in one day, which is about 16 billion miles (26 billion kilometers). That means it now takes a full day for a signal to travel from Earth to Voyager 1, the farthest human-made object out in space.
"I remember in 1977 I was standing at the Cape watching Voyager launch, and I never thought I would be here today, 49 years later, to celebrate Voyager's one light-day. What an incredible milestone," Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker said during Thursday's event.
An unexpected, but manageable, anomaly
While this day and this week have been chock-full of excitement over the distance milestone, the Voyager mission team has been hard at work, as usual, keeping the two nearly 50-year-old spacecraft alive and functioning.
Unfortunately, as JPL spokesperson Raquel Villanueva shared at this celebration, on Saturday (Nov. 14), Voyager 1 stopped transmitting science and engineering data. The team has remained in contact with Voyager 1 while investigating the issue, and they are confident that they'll be able to solve it soon.
NASA and Voyager team members celebrate Voyager 1's light-day milestone at NASA JPL on Nov. 19, 2026. From left to right: Nicky Fox, associate administrator, Science Mission Directorate; Jonathan Lunine, chief scientist, NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory; Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager; Kareem Badaruddin, Voyager mission manager; Jamie Rankin, Voyager deputy project scientist. 
(Image credit: Chelsea Gohd)
"We listen to each spacecraft every day," Voyager project manager Suzanne Dodd told Space.com at the event. Regarding Voyager 1, "we have an anomaly that is not something unseen before," Dodd said. "We do have a very strong carrier signal. We currently don't have a frame sync, so we're not getting the science or engineering data currently."
"But if you recall," Dodd added, "one light-day is very far from us. [It takes] 24 hours to get the signal to the spacecraft, 24 hours for the signal to come down, so it takes two days to diagnose anything on the spacecraft … So we can't do anything fast, and the data rates are very low."
According to Dodd, this anomaly isn't a major cause for long-term concern. Rather, it's likely just part of the natural aging process for spacecraft.
"These spacecraft are old," she said. "All of us old people have our aches and pains, and you know, sometimes it takes longer to stretch out your muscles and remember things, and so we are in a realm where we're going to see more anomalies with Voyager just due to the distance and the age."
"But we feel Voyager 1, although it's not sending us the telemetry data, it's pointed at the 
Earth
. It's sending us a strong carrier signal, and I'm confident that it won't be too long, and it will send us data back."
And, for anyone curious how Voyager 1's slower twin Voyager 2 is faring, Dodd shared that it is doing very well on its own journey through 
interstellar space
. "Voyager 2 is very healthy. It's continuing its mission. It has three scientific instruments on it," she told Space.com.
"Each spacecraft is basically setting a record every day for longevity or distance," Dodd added about the twin probes.
A Voyager 1 photo of Saturn, which the probe flew by in November 1980. 
(Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech)
A heartfelt celebration 
Despite the communications anomaly — and even though most of the team had been up all night to confirm the one-light-day signal from Voyager 1 — the room was alive with excitement. The Voyagers have been flying for nearly 50 years on a mission expected to last just five years, so the team was beyond ready to celebrate.
Amidst the more technical updates about the mission and its current status out in interstellar space, the event included footage of Voyager light-day celebrations from around the world. From children cheering on Voyager to iconic landmarks lighting up gold in honor of the mission's 
Golden Record,
 the Voyager love echoed around the world in photos and videos. The footage of this international celebration was enough to move Nicky Fox, NASA's associate administrator for the Science Mission Directorate, nearly to tears.
"I actually thought I was going to start sobbing," Fox joked to Space.com after the media briefing. "I think just hearing children's voices and people saying 'We love you, Voyager,' 'Go Voyager' — it just was amazing."
And Fox and the mission team members weren't the only ones sharing their love for Voyager. NASA astronaut Jessica Watkins beamed down her own video message to the Voyager 1 spacecraft from the 
International Space Station
, sharing in the celebration.
"Dear Voyager 1," Watkins said. "As an orbiting outpost just 250 miles [400 kilometers] above Earth, we often feel distant yet deeply connected to our home planet. The International Space Station has had crew continuously living off of the Earth for 26 years, seen hundreds of visitors from around the world, and enabled thousands of experiments to advance space exploration.
"While you have been traveling into the unknown for nearly half a century, helping pave our path, together, our two journeys tell a single story of exploration," she continued. "One remains close to home, and the other reaches ever outward. Voyager 1, you have been a beacon of light, a symbol of exploration, a seeker of truth, a carrier of hope, a brave explorer, traveling further than any human hand has ever reached. You represent the very best of what humanity has to offer.
"As you reach a distance of one light-day from Earth, the entire crew of Expedition 75 and people across the planet send you our admiration and our wishes for a safe and continued journey as you begin your post-one-light-day era. There are so many amazing milestones and discoveries to come. You have a 
universe
 ahead of you, and you carry with you our collective hopes and dreams. When we look up from Earth, we will always think of you as you continue forward into the great unknown. May you continue shining bright for all of us. Go Voyager 1!" Watkins said, ending her message with a weightless backflip from aboard the station.
In concluding the media briefing, the mission team invited those in the room who, over the past 50 years (or more, with one person sharing their excitement having worked on Voyager before it was even called Voyager) have worked on the mission in one way or another, to stand up to a round of applause. With mission team members today ranging from those in their 20s to their 90s, the people behind this enduring mission span many generations.
A golden moment
Interestingly, Fox actually began her light-day celebrations earlier in the week. She, alongside writer/producer Ann Druyan — who was the wife of the late astronomer 
Carl Sagan
, whose work helped create the mission's Golden Record and who took the now-famous "
pale blue dot
" Voyager image of Earth to the public — together lit up the Empire State Building gold.
Nicky Fox's Voyager-inspired golden nails. 
(Image credit: Chelsea Gohd)
"I was actually very privileged to flick the switch and turn on the Empire State Building lights on Tuesday evening [Nov. 17]," Fox, wearing golden Voyager-inspired nail polish crafted by her daughter, told Space.com. "That was just spectacular … I was there with Ann … we were both so giddy with the excitement of doing it … It was amazing. And then going up and standing underneath and seeing the tower was just really, really special."
Looking to the future
While the room lit up with excitement for Voyager's light-day milestone on Thursday, the team was also brimming with anticipation for what lies ahead. As Voyager hurtles toward another major milestone, turning 50 in 2027, it remains an active science mission, and the team remains hard at work to push the boundaries of exploration with these aging probes.
"Voyager right now — the two Voyagers — they're in an area of space that we've never been into. So every single bit of data that comes back is precious because it's unique," Fox said.
"Now we're in a region where the sun's influence is a lot different, and we're in this region where we can start to understand: What is it like out past 
our sun
? What is it like in the space between the 
stars
?" Voyager deputy project scientist Jamie Rankin told Space.com. "We're at a very exciting time of starting to connect what we know and what we've seen, living with this star [our sun] to what is out there."
"What's next? Voyager is just going to keep on going. You know, people sometimes think, 'When's the mission going to end?' It's never going to end," Fox added to Space.com. "Even when we can no longer talk to Voyager, they're both going to keep going … So there's no end for the Voyager mission."

NASA’s Voyager 1 Makes History Reaching One Light-Day From Earth
Jessica Taveau
Nov 19, 2026
RELEASE
26-098	
NASA Headquarters
Voyager 1, depicted in this artist’s concept, reached one light-day from Earth on Nov. 18, 2026.  
Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech
NASA’s Voyager 1 probe, humanity’s farthest-flying spacecraft, has reached one light-day from Earth, after more than 49 years of constant travel. A 
light-day
 is the distance light travels in 24 hours, equaling about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers). The spacecraft achieved the milestone at 2:16 a.m. PST (5:16 a.m. EST) Nov. 18.
Voyager team members at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, managed by Caltech, heard from the probe 24 hours later, the time it took for the spacecraft’s radio signal traveling at the speed of light to reach Earth, early Thursday.
“Traveling a light-day is a singular feat for a spacecraft, but Voyager is so much more than a distant traveler. The Voyager mission would be remarkable just for what it contributed to planetary science with the Grand Tour of the outer planets,” said Joe Westlake, director, Heliophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington. “But it went on to become central to NASA’s fleet of heliophysics missions offering insights into the star that we all revolve around, the Sun.”
Scientists use light to measure the vast distances of space. Light travels at about 186,000 miles (299,000 kilometers) a second in a vacuum. A ray of sunlight passing Earth takes about 1.3 seconds to reach the Moon, about four hours to reach Neptune, and another 20 hours to reach Voyager 1. For comparison, a 
light-year
 is 5.88 trillion miles (9.46 trillion kilometers).    
Even though the team 
has not received
 science and engineering data from Voyager 1 since Nov. 14, the spacecraft is sending what is called a carrier signal, which typically conveys that data to Earth. Mission engineers used the carrier signal and trajectory calculations to confirm the light-day milestone. They continue to analyze the issue impacting the science and engineering data.
Humanity’s only probes in interstellar space, Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, 
launched
 in 1977 to take advantage of a rare planetary alignment. Managed by JPL, they were only the third and fourth spacecraft to visit Jupiter and Saturn, and the first to return high-resolution imagery, transforming our understanding of these worlds and their moons. Voyager 2 went on to study the ethereal ice giants, Neptune and Uranus. Both spacecraft continue to measure changes in the Sun’s magnetic field across vast distances.
“Voyager 1 crossing the light-day threshold is yet another historic feat on an extraordinary voyage of discovery,” said Dave Gallagher, JPL director. “Above all, this milestone stands as a testament to the exceptional teams who built, programmed, and fly these spacecraft today, and who have overcome countless challenges to keep them connected to Earth across the vastness of space. As NASA pushes toward new frontiers in human and robotic exploration, Voyager and the people behind its success continue to inspire us to reach farther and dream bigger.”
In addition to their suite of 
scientific instruments
, the Voyager probes carry a 
Golden Record
, a gold-plated copper phonograph compiling images and sounds from Earth, as a message to any advanced civilization that might one day find it.
“Nearly half a century after leaving Earth, the Voyager mission continues to push the boundaries of human exploration. Its epic journey reflects the vision of Ed Stone, the Caltech physicist and JPL director who guided the mission for decades, and the curiosity and tenacity of generations of scientists and engineers who have carried that vision forward,” said Ray Jayawardhana, president, Caltech. “Beyond its immense contributions to science, Voyager also captures something profound about the human spirit. We may never know where its journey will ultimately lead, but it will venture on, an enduring affirmation that humanity was here and we yearned to explore.”
Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, transformed our understanding of the solar system through their exploration of the outer planets. In 2012, Voyager 1 became the first human-made spacecraft to enter interstellar space. On Nov. 18, 2026, Voyager 1 reached one light-day from Earth.
Milestones, challenges
Traveling about 38,000 mph (61,155 kph), Voyager 1 in 2012 became the first spacecraft to leave the 
heliosphere
, the bubble of particles and magnetic fields created by the Sun that deflects cosmic radiation, and enter interstellar space. Voyager 2, taking a different trajectory, crossed the boundary in 2018.
“The Voyagers are revolutionizing our understanding of interstellar space, a previously unexplored region outside the heliosphere,” said Linda Spilker, Voyager project scientist at JPL. “One big surprise is the extent to which our Sun continues to affect interstellar space, all the way out to Voyager 1. Where does the Sun’s influence end and pristine interstellar space begin? Perhaps Voyager may endure long enough to answer that question.”
Creative engineering solutions have enabled the probes to conserve energy and overcome challenges related to aging hardware, including clogged thrusters and a 
malfunctioning computer
 on the spacecraft. Earlier this year, engineers successfully turned off three devices on Voyager 1 and substituted them with three lower-power alternatives — a move not without risk — to help the spacecraft 
make the most
 of its 50-year-old 
radioisotope thermal generator
. Essentially a nuclear battery, the generator converts heat from decaying plutonium into electricity.
With the plutonium supply continually depleting, both Voyager probes lose about 4 watts of power each year. The latest energy-saving effort is expected to provide enough power for both spacecraft to keep their current suite of science instruments operating until at least their 50th launch anniversaries next summer.
As with all NASA spacecraft that venture to and beyond the Moon, communication between Earth and the Voyager probes is managed by the agency’s 
Deep Space Network
, large antennas located at three facilities around the world.
“We listen to Voyager 1 every day and send commands to the spacecraft weekly. It just takes a little longer to communicate than it used to,” said Suzanne Dodd, Voyager project manager at JPL. “Even traveling at the speed of light, signals now take 24 hours to reach the spacecraft and 24 hours to reach us. One day in the future, when it can no longer communicate with Earth, Voyager 1 will become humanity’s silent ambassador to the stars.”
