Key Takeaways
SpaceX plans to catch Ship, Starship’s upper stage, for the first time on Flight 15.
Ship faces a harder return than Super Heavy, surviving reentry, belly-flop descent, and a vertical flip.
Recovering both stages intact would mark the first full reuse of an orbital launch vehicle.
Catching a rocket booster out of the sky is one thing. Catching the upper stage after it survives orbital flight, atmospheric reentry, and a 
high-speed flip maneuver
 is an entirely different engineering problem, and 
SpaceX
 plans to 
attempt it on Flight 15
.
The goal is to recover both Super Heavy and Starship’s upper stage, called Ship, using the launch tower’s mechanical “chopstick” arms at the same launch site. A successful outcome would mark the first time both stages of an orbital launch vehicle were recovered intact, though that milestone depends entirely on both catches succeeding on the same flight.
The Maneuver, Explained
SpaceX released an approximately 80-second simulation showing Ship’s full return sequence, from atmospheric descent to tower capture.
The 
simulation is labele
d 
“Up Next: Ship Catch.”
 It depicts Ship descending in a horizontal belly-flop configuration, with forward and aft flaps controlling its trajectory through the atmosphere.
Near the tower, the vehicle rotates to a vertical orientation and descends between the extended chopstick arms. The arms then meet hardware on Ship’s sides and support the vehicle after capture.
Ground testing has already begun. 
Ship 42
, the vehicle assigned to Flight 15, was lifted by the Pad 2 chopsticks into a simulated catch position, according to Teslarati reporting. That test allowed crews to cycle the arms and connect the ship’s quick-disconnect arm, though this specific claim has not been independently verified by a primary SpaceX source.

A Much Longer Road Home
Ship faces a far more demanding return profile than the booster does, with multiple sequential phases that must each succeed before the vehicle reaches the tower.
Super Heavy’s planned recovery involves a shorter return profile than Ship’s. The booster returns to the Gulf of Mexico within minutes of launch, per Reuters reporting on Flight 14.
Ship must survive orbital or near-orbital flight, atmospheric reentry, and aerodynamic control in a belly-flop configuration before a powered descent even becomes possible. On 
Flight 14
, a Raptor Vacuum engine 
shut down prematurely shortening
 what was originally expected to be a roughly 
10-hour mission
. Ship splashed down in the Pacific Ocean approximately three hours after liftoff, according to Reuters.
A catch attempt on Flight 15 would require every phase to succeed in sequence. That means reentry, belly-flop descent, the vertical flip, and a final powered approach that places Ship within the arms’ narrow capture envelope.
The simulation shows the plan. It is not evidence the complete maneuver has been demonstrated in flight.
Why It Matters
Recovering Ship at the launch site would remove the need for ocean retrieval and could reduce the time between launches, which is central to SpaceX’s reusability case for Starship.
Recovering Ship
 at the launch site eliminates ocean retrieval and could significantly reduce turnaround time between flights. That speed of reuse is the core of SpaceX’s operational argument for Starship.
Speaking on the company’s earnings call, Musk said a successful Flight 15 would be the first time an orbital launch vehicle had been fully recovered. That claim is contingent on both stage catches succeeding, and Musk cautioned that the outcome is not guaranteed on the first attempt.
One successful catch would not establish a reliable cadence on its own. Repeated recoveries would be required before 
SpaceX
 could treat rapid Starship reuse as an operational reality rather than a stated target.
Flight 15 is a live test of a concept that exists, so far, in simulation and on the ground. What happens next determines whether Starship’s reusability promise moves from a compelling animation into something you can actually count on.

Super Heavy is Starship's giant booster, and Starship (or Ship for short) is its upper-stage spacecraft. As SpaceX's description noted, both elements are designed to be fully and rapidly reusable.

SpaceX has caught Super Heavy

SpaceX founder and CEO

 already told us that Flight 15 would likely feature a

STARBASE, Texas—Starship lifted off right on schedule Friday from South Texas, making its 15th test flight in more than three years.
The largely successful launch took place in the early evening; as the massive rocket rose into the atmosphere, the Sun sank toward the western horizon. Conditions were ideal for the launch, with the atmospheric winds so calm the rocket’s white plume did not dissipate for many minutes. The weather in South Texas is not always comfortable; summers are long and hot. Friday evening was perfect at Starbase.
The first stage ascended toward space flawlessly, releasing the upper stage above the Gulf of Mexico. From there, the Starship upper stage ignited its Raptor engines and powered its way into a nominal orbit. This was the second time SpaceX has flown Starship into low-Earth orbit.
When it came time for the first stage to return to the ground, it dropped like a rock. However, due to an unspecified issue, the vehicle was commanded to divert into the Gulf rather than return to the Starbase facility to be caught by the launch tower. It created a fantastically powerful explosion, even for observers more than 10 miles away.
Primary mission, a success
The primary aim of Friday’s launch was the deployment of 26 Starlink V3 satellites from a narrow door at the base of the spacecraft. This occurred about 40 minutes into Starship’s flight, and all 26 satellites—which have solar panels that stretch 60 meters end to end when fully deployed—safely made it into orbit, where they will now use onboard propulsion to raise their altitude.
This means that Starship is now an operational, orbital rocket. It is the largest and most powerful machine humans have ever launched into space. The challenge for the company now is to make Starship reusable and dramatically increase its flight rate.
In that sense, the first part of Friday’s mission was not a success. SpaceX had hoped to return the first-stage booster, refurbish it, and fly it again. The company has previously landed and reflown Starship boosters but has yet to do so with the latest iteration of the vehicle, version 3.
However, SpaceX still has a chance to make history on Saturday with the potential return of the Starship upper stage. SpaceX has never attempted to catch the vehicle’s upper stage.
Doing so will require Starship to pass a series of tests in orbit and subsequently during its return to Earth to ensure it is fully healthy. This is critical because Starship will be passing over parts of Mexico and the United States before returning to Starbase, and SpaceX does not want to endanger people on the ground. Additionally, it does not want to risk damage to its launch towers.
If Starship passes these health checks, it would land at about 3:15 pm local time in Texas (21:15 UTC). If it does not, the company has earlier options to de-orbit the vehicle into an ocean.
Testing the heat shield is critical
Successfully returning Starship’s upper stage would be historic, as well as significant for SpaceX’s efforts to reuse the massive rocket. It would also give the company valuable information about the vehicle’s performance during reentry.
One of the biggest hurdles left in Starship’s development is the vehicle’s heat shield, which must protect the upper stage during its return through Earth’s atmosphere. More than that, SpaceX aims to make Starship rapidly reusable, and to do this, Starship’s tiles must be light and resilient.
SpaceX has been experimenting with tiles since the beginning of the Starship program, trying to find the right combination of low mass, heat resistance, and durability so the tiles require minimal refurbishment between launches. For this flight, SpaceX said it is applying learnings from the previous test flight in September, including additional retention mechanisms on tiles in areas deemed to be at the highest risk of falling off during ascent and addressing areas with a flow path for plasma to get behind the tiles.
Another problem on recent flights has been heat incursion into the small gaps between the edges of the tiles. According to SpaceX, this Starship has a large portion of the heat shield equipped with a curved tile design that has shown the ability to reduce heating in the gaps between tiles.
