Firefly Aerospace Alpha rocket on the launch pad before the March 2026 mission. Photo: Firefly Aerospace         
Japanese synthetic aperture radar (SAR) provider 
Synspective
 ordered 10 launches on 
Firefly Aerospace
’s Alpha rocket, planned through 2030. 
This is a new customer for Firefly, which plans to launch the missions both Pad-0A at Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia and Space Launch Complex 2 at Vandenberg Space Force Base in California. Each mission will launch two satellites for Synspective’s StriX constellation. 
Synspective is also a major customer of Rocket Lab and signed the largest commercial contract for Electron last month for 20 launches, in addition to two previous 
10-launch deals
. 
Synspective founder and CEO Dr. Motoyuki Arai said in a statement that scaling the constellation requires a diverse set of launch providers. 
“Firefly has shown real commitment, working through the hard realities of developing a new launch vehicle and steadily building a track record with Alpha. Alpha’s payload class is well suited to our upcoming satellites, and its dual-coast access gives us valuable flexibility across orbital inclinations. Adding Firefly to our launch portfolio strengthens our ability to accelerate deployment and deliver resilient service to our customers,” Arai said. 
Firefly hasn’t set a specific date for the next launch of its Alpha rocket, which will feature the full Block II configuration for the first time. CEO Jason Kim told investors in the company’s Q2 results that flight is targeted for the fourth quarter of this year. 
It’s been a big week for Firefly Aerospace after the company also announced 
a deal to sell rocket engines and license its engine technology
 to AST SpaceMobile, which will build its own rocket based on Firefly’s engines.

Firefly To Provide 10 Alpha Launches For Japan’s Synspective
Share
Vivienne Machi
November 10, 2026
Firefly Aerospace’s Alpha rocket launches March 11 from Vandenberg SFB, California. 
Credit: Firefly Aerospace
Firefly Aerospace will provide Japanese remote sensing provider Synspective with up to 10 launches on its Alpha small-lift rocket through 2030, the launch company announced Nov. 10.
The company’s Alpha Block II vehicle will deliver two Synspective satellites to low Earth orbit (LEO) per mission to build out the synthetic aperture radar provider’s StriX constellation, per a company release. Firefly will operate those missions from two launch pads: Pad-0A at Wallops Flight Facility, Virginia, and Space Launch Complex 2 at Vandenberg SFB, California. 
Synspective founder and CEO Motoyuki Arai lauded the dual-coast access as giving the company “valuable flexibility across orbital inclinations.” 
The Japanese provider has used Rocket Lab’s Electron small-lift rocket to deploy much of its constellation to date. In September, the two companies signed a deal for Electron to launch 20 satellites to sun-synchronous orbit between 2028-31. Synspective has purchased a cumulative 47 missions on Electron, per Rocket Lab.

Synspective Adds Firefly to Its StriX Launch Program
Synspective has signed a 
multi-launch agreement with Firefly Aerospace
 covering up to 10 dedicated Alpha missions through 2030. Each mission is planned to carry two StriX radar satellites, providing transportation for up to 20 spacecraft. The Japanese Earth-observation company announced the agreement on November 10, 2026.
The missions will use Pad-0A at Wallops Flight Facility in Virginia and Space Launch Complex 2 at 
Vandenberg Space Force Base
 in California. Synspective describes the two launch locations as a way to access different orbital inclinations and complement its existing launch arrangements.
Two Satellites on Each Dedicated Mission
The paired deployment changes the number of spacecraft delivered on an individual flight. Compared with a single-satellite mission, each planned Alpha launch would place twice as many StriX spacecraft in orbit. The agreement adds Firefly to the suppliers supporting the constellation’s expansion.
Orbital inclination describes the angle between an orbit and Earth’s equator. It determines the highest latitudes that the orbit reaches. A constellation operator selects orbital arrangements in relation to the regions it observes and the timing of successive passes. Launch-site access forms part of delivering satellites into those arrangements.
Rocket Lab
’s 
Viva La StriX mission
 provides a documented example of the earlier single-spacecraft deployment pattern. An Electron launched one StriX satellite on May 22, 2026, from Launch Complex 1. The mission page records a 572-kilometer orbit with a 44.8-degree inclination and identifies it as the ninth Synspective launch aboard Electron.
That flight supported imaging services used for urban development, construction, infrastructure monitoring, and disaster response. The orbit and payload were specified for the customer’s observation mission. Firefly’s newly announced agreement adds a different vehicle and a two-satellite deployment configuration to that launch program.
How StriX Produces Radar Images
StriX uses synthetic aperture radar, which sends microwave signals toward Earth and records the returning reflections. Synspective’s 
satellite data specifications
 identify a 9.65-gigahertz signal in the X band. The reflected signal, called backscatter, contains information about terrain and structures.
The satellite combines observations collected as it moves along its orbit. Processing those measurements produces the effect of a much larger antenna, which is the basis of the term synthetic aperture. Because the instrument supplies its own radio illumination, it can acquire observations in darkness and through clouds.
Synspective offers Stripmap, Sliding Spotlight, and Staring Spotlight modes. Stripmap collects a long strip of terrain as the satellite moves. The spotlight modes steer the beam to spend more observation time on a selected area, supporting finer detail over a smaller scene. The choice of mode connects the area to be covered with the resolution of the resulting product.
New Space Economy’s 
guide to radar satellite operators
 places these services within the commercial Earth-observation market. Radar measurements support analysis of surface changes as well as image interpretation. Repeated acquisitions allow users to compare the same location at different times, including when cloud cover interrupts optical observations.
A Launch Program With Multiple Providers
Synspective also announced a 
SpaceX rideshare contract
 on March 24, 2025. That arrangement covers two StriX satellites on a Falcon 9 rideshare mission targeted for 2027. Rideshare places a customer’s spacecraft on a launch shared with other payloads, giving the company another route to orbit alongside dedicated missions.
The StriX technology originated through Japan’s ImPACT research program and was developed with the University of Tokyo, the Institute of Science Tokyo, and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency. Synspective operates the satellites and turns their observations into data and analytical services. Its launch purchases provide the delivery stage for that operating system.
The Firefly agreement extends this deployment program through 2030. Its defined configuration combines two StriX spacecraft per mission with access to launch facilities on both U.S. coasts.
