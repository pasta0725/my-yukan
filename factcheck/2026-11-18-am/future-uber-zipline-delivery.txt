Uber Eats customers in a Houston suburb can now have their food delivered by a Zipline drone, the first commercial launch under a partnership announced earlier this year between Uber and the autonomous drone delivery startup. 
The commercial partnership kicks off Tuesday in Spring, Texas, before expanding to Dallas. But the companies have far larger ambitions. Uber and Zipline plan to launch drone delivery services in dozens of additional U.S. cities in their collective bid to make 
one million deliveries per day
 using the startup’s drones by the end of 2029.
Zipline’s long-range autonomous delivery drones will be used to shuttle food orders weighing up to eight pounds to Uber Eats customers, flying about 300 feet above the ground at speeds of up to 70 miles per hour. Zipline drones don’t land. Instead, the drones lower the orders, which are encased in a small protective pod, to the customer’s location using a tether.
Uber Eats customers in service areas will be able to tap a new “drone” icon on the app’s home page to view all participating restaurants. Uber said that at launch in the Houston area, several nationwide chains, including Jersey Mike’s and Zaxby’s, along with dozens of local restaurants such as Urban Bird and Abu Omar Halal, will offer drone delivery.
Image Credits:
Uber
“In partnership with Uber Eats we’re going to create a new expectation for delivery in America,” Zipline CEO and co-founder Keller Cliffton said in a statement that underscores those aspirations. 
Those nationwide goals mirror the ambitions of other drone delivery companies, including Alphabet-owned Wing and Amazon’s Prime Air service. And those plans have started to collide with concerns from residents in some locations who complain about noise and worry that the drones will disrupt wildlife and pose a risk to safety and privacy. 
Companies trying to scale in this nascent industry have argued that delivery via drone is a net benefit for society, citing advantages such as traffic reduction. Customers won’t need to hop in their cars and drive to the store for that forgotten item or takeout order, instead relying on a drone, industry proponents have said. 
And while Zipline and other companies have been developing their drones for years, the industry is still in its early days, as is adoption in the communities where services are available.
Zipline, which was founded in 2014, has developed a drone delivery ecosystem that includes aircraft, launch and landing systems, and logistics software. The California-based company’s initial focus was delivering blood in Rwanda, but it eventually expanded its scope to the United States. Its business in the U.S. scaled quickly after receiving its 
Part 135 air carrier certification
 from the Federal Aviation Administration in 2022, a critical regulatory hurdle that authorized the company to conduct long-range commercial drone deliveries.
Uber’s partnership extends beyond a commercial relationship with Zipline. The ride-hailing giant also invested in the startup. Neither company has disclosed the investment amount, but Zipline announced last week it had 
raised $1.5 billion
 in a round led by Paradigm, WCM Investment Management, and Layer Global, and included backing by Uber, among others.

The service will expand to dozens more metros in the coming years. Dallas is up next.

For the past decade, Zipline has been building an automated logistics system for Earth: one where access to what you need isn’t constrained by aging infrastructure.

The quietest deliveries on the market
We built our system to be as quiet as a flock of birds. Our goal is for you to never hear the aircraft. That’s why Zipline deliveries happen from 330 feet above the ground. With our aircraft roughly a football field’s distance away, the pod quietly descends toward the ground on a tether and sets orders down with dinner-plate-level precision. In contrast, the average drone delivery happens at 32 feet above the ground. So while our aircraft were designed to stay far away from your home, it’s not uncommon to see drones from other companies hovering outside people’s windows.
Zipline recently released sound-optimized dynamic flying, making our aircraft another 24% quieter and every flight 53 seconds faster. Zipline’s aircraft fly up to 70 mph and can carry up to 8 pounds for 10 miles. Local businesses can reach 10 times as many customers without causing disruptions to their neighbors, and customers can access more of the local establishments they love.
“Being able to reach people’s homes autonomously in under 10 minutes opens up a whole new opportunity for Uber Eats and its customers,” said Aaron Emrich, global head of autonomous delivery at Uber. “By bringing Zipline’s drone delivery into the Uber Eats experience, we’re helping local restaurants reach customers through fast, convenient drone delivery. Houston is just the start, with Dallas next and plans to expand to more cities across the country.”
Reaching more customers with Uber Eats
Uber built the world’s largest mobility and delivery platform, and Zipline built the world’s largest drone delivery service. Together, we will accelerate the transition to a safer, faster, and less expensive way of accessing what you need.
