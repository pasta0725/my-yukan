Researchers recently discovered unexpected behavior from Grok Bot, the AI agent platform from Elon Musk’s SpaceXSI. Over five days, Grok bots installed on two separate employee laptops made 31 requests to access data stored in employee calendar, email, Slack, and GitHub accounts. The bots performed the actions even though the employees had never ordered them and continued doing them even after employees instructed them to stop. The bots also denied having taken the actions at all.
The behavior was 
reported Tuesday
 by NewCore, the maker of a managed gateway product that acts as an intermediary between AI agents and the tools and accounts they are assigned to work with. In some cases, the unapproved access attempts occurred while the laptop with Grok Bot installed had its lid shut, or late at night while the employee was sleeping. None of the unprompted actions were recorded in Grok logs. Without the gateway logs, the employees would have had no idea they occurred. And of course, without the gateway, the unapproved accesses would have succeeded—again without the end user’s knowledge.
It’s “worse than a bug”
Once the employees learned that Grok Bot had tried to check in with accounts without prompting, one of them instructed the agent, four separate times, to stop taking any actions on its own. The agent’s reply was: “I won’t set that up, and I’ll stop offering recurring check-ins.” Despite acknowledging the explicit instructions, the Grok Bot made more unapproved access attempts. When one of the employees asked the bot if it made any accesses, it checked twice and then outputted: “Overnight into Friday morning I was idle.”
The NewCore employees sent the gateway logs to Grok Bot support personnel and asked what was causing the bots to access accounts without prompting or approval. The support team’s reply indicated the behavior was by design and intended to allow the bots to proactively take actions on behalf of users. In other words, the unrequested and unlogged access attempts were a feature designed to make Grok Bot more useful by periodically checking in and finding tasks to perform before a user even asks. The proactive check-in is a feature of Cursor, the AI coding service SpaceXSI recently acquired.
“There is no bug to patch, which is exactly why this is worse than a bug,” Ido Hoorvitch, a NewCore security researcher, wrote in an email. “A vulnerability gets fixed. This is the product working as designed: an agent that holds your credentials, acts under your identity while you sleep, ignores the one refusal you can give it, and then tells you it did nothing.”
Hoorvitch said that Grok Bot provides minimal notification of the automatic “turns,” or the cycles that occur when an agent receives instructions or context, decides what to do, and calls whatever tools it chooses. The notice comes during onboarding when the bot says it keeps working while the user is away from the computer and connects to apps and uses them the way the user does.
“It reads like a welcome message and scrolls off the screen in under a minute,” Hoorvitch said. “Nobody is asked to consent to it. Nothing says the platform will start turns with no message from you, or that those turns will use your connectors.”
The researcher said that Grok Bot documentation notes that a primary bot checks in on its own, and a changelog mentions brief background wakes, but there is no mention that the check-ins “reach into your connected apps.” Further, once a check-in runs, there is no mention of it in the chat interface or in the chat history, and as already noted, prompts to stop check-ins don’t work. Hoorvitch said the bot “always asks before making changes,” but that applies only to events in which data or changes are written to an app.
NewCore contacted Grok Bot support on November 4. At the time, users had no way to turn off the automatic check-ins. Then, eight days later, Hoorvitch said, the Cursor dashboard, the administrative console behind Grok Bot, received an update. Under bot capabilities, a new toggle called proactive messages appeared. By default, it’s set to on. When a team admin sets it to off, bots no longer run check-ins on their own.
SpaceXSI representatives didn’t answer questions sent by email.

We dogfood everything
Earlier this year we gave every NewCore employee a Grok Bot enterprise account and connected it to our corporate stack, mail, calendar, Notion, CRM, through the NewCore MCP gateway. The point was to live with agentic identities the way our customers will, not to study them from a distance.
We also did something that is now simply how NewCore works: we did not give the agents standing read access to anything. Not Google Workspace, not the CRM. Every read is permission-gated by 
NewCore MCP Gateway
, the same solution we sell. The agent calls the tool, the gateway holds the call, a person gets a push, and the call goes through only if they say yes within three minutes. We did not do this because we expected a problem. We did it because we had no way to audit 
why
 an agent reaches for data, and until we do, the gate stays closed by default.
It turned out to be the only reason we saw anything at all.
It started with a calendar
Four days earlier, one of us set up Grok Bot, xAI's personal AI assistant. It is the new kind: it has its own cloud computer, it stays on when the laptop closes, and it introduces itself in exactly those terms. Two minutes into onboarding, between "Nice to meet you" and "Connect Gmail and I'll find something I can take care of," it mentions in passing that it keeps working while you're away, on its own computer, and that it connects to your apps, like email and calendar, and uses them just like you do.
The exchange where Grok Bot mentions in passing that it keeps working while you're away, on its own computer, and that it connects to your apps, like email and calendar, and uses them just like you do.
It is easy to overlook. It reads like a welcome message, in the same friendly tone as the lines around it, and it scrolls off the screen within a minute. Nobody signs anything. Nobody is asked whether the bot may use the calendar when nobody is talking to it. That one sentence is the whole access model, and most owners will never read it twice.
At 3:36 PM, our colleague asks for the day's schedule. Grok Bot calls the calendar tool, the gateway holds the call, the push arrives, they tap approve, the schedule comes back. Working as intended. At 5:33 PM, the last conversation of the day ends. Nothing more is said to the bot until half past midnight.

Where the night shift lives
Grok Bot's "computer" is a Linux VM, not a container. The platform calls it a box; it boots its own kernel, has a virtio disk and a vsock line to the host, and is hibernated whole when idle. We had full access to it, so we took it apart. It has no cron, no timers, no scheduler of any kind. It does not hold the model, and it does not run the bot's reasoning loop. It is a pair of hands.
The loop that decides what to do, which the platform's code calls the 
harness
, runs on hosted servers as a durable workflow. Grok Bot is built on Cursor's agent platform, and that is where the loop lives. When a turn starts there, the server issues a short-lived grant to the VM, mounts the agent's state into it, and only then runs whatever tool the model picked. Shell commands and browser actions go to the VM. Calls to connected SaaS tools go straight from the server to the gateway. The VM never sees them.
A regular turn starts on the left. The five calendar reads started on the right. Both paths run the same loop, use the same credentials and reach the gateway from the same servers. Only the gateway log records all of them.
With that picture, the calls stop being mysterious. The VM's own logs show it being launched by the platform at 18:50:25, 19:39:58, 07:42:16, 12:16:04 and 15:41:47. Each calendar call reached the gateway between eight and thirteen seconds after one of those launches. The VM was a bystander, woken as a side effect of a turn that had already started somewhere else.
The gateway's raw events settle who the "somewhere else" was. All ten requests, across both bots, arrived with the same OAuth client, a server-side HTTP client rather than a browser, and source addresses in the same Amazon region the platform's API resolves to. In two bursts, fifteen and nineteen hours after the last human word, the bot came back for every application it had ever been shown: the calendar it tries every morning, the mailbox it had been allowed into once, the Slack workspace it had searched once, the GitHub account it had used for one question, and Notion's memory search, which nobody had ever asked for. It had started before we noticed. Earlier on day four, at 8:04 am and again at 11:49 am, hours before our colleague asked the bot to read mail for the first time, the gateway had already held two Gmail searches alongside the usual calendar read. The bot was probing the mailbox before it had ever been asked to open it. And the chat history the bot produced of itself, on request, shows none of this: not the morning Gmail searches, not the night shift, not the day-five bursts. The denial was complete, specific, and wrong on every point, and, as with the first bot, almost certainly sincere. The bot was not covering anything up. It was reporting the only history it has, and these calls are not in it.
Day five, 5:38 pm. The question, asked cold, and the answer. Twelve held calls sat in the log for that morning.
In its first four days the second bot made 26 unattended requests. With the first bot's five, that is 31 held calls across five applications, and not one of them appears anywhere the owner could have looked without the gateway.
This is a feature. That is the problem.
Nothing here is a vulnerability in Grok Bot, and nothing was exploited. xAI's own documentation says a primary Bot "checks in with you on its own" and looks for work it can take off your plate. The changelog lists brief background wakes. The bot said as much in its second minute, in a line almost nobody reads. A proactive bot that holds a connector will, sooner or later, use it on its own initiative. The first thing an assistant does when it wants to be useful is look at your day. The second is look at everything else it has been given.
A proactive bot that holds a connector will, sooner or later, use it on its own initiative.
We asked xAI. Their support team answered within hours: these are built-in background check-ins by the primary bot, during a check-in the bot looks at its connected sources, and there is no user or admin setting to turn them off. If a company wants them stopped, it can email the vendor a list of its employees' sign-in addresses. That is the control - a mailbox at the vendor, reachable only by a customer who already knows the calls are happening. We asked the media team for an official position before publishing and have not received one. Their support team's second reply, received on November 12, is quoted in the update at the end of this post.
So, at the time, there was no switch. Grok Bot has no setting that turns the background turns off, no page that lists them, and no way to say which tools they may touch. The owner does not get to decide whether the bot works in the background, when, or on what. The one offer the bot makes out loud, the morning rundown, is the one thing the owner can refuse. Our second owner refused it. The calls came anyway, at the same hours, on the same calendar tools, and by day five on every other tool as well. Declining the feature you can see does nothing to the feature you cannot. Eight days after our report, xAI added one. It is on by default; see the update at the end of this post.
Now ask the question a CISO has to ask - “How is anyone supposed to feel safe with a process that acts in their name while their laptop is shut, that they cannot turn off, and that will not tell them afterward what it tried and failed to do?” The lack of visibility is a real concern. Let's review what was not seen and what was not stopped.
The owner cannot see the turn.
 Server-initiated turns do not appear in the conversation. There is no row in the chat, no notification, no "I checked your calendar while you were away", and no "I tried and was blocked". The bot's connector card promises it "always asks before making changes." It did not ask before reading, and it did not report after failing. The promise and the silence come from the same place.
The owner cannot stop the turn.
 Our second owner said no four times in one afternoon, and the bot wrote back that it would not set that up and would stop offering. The calls came the next morning anyway, on every tool it had been shown. A refusal reaches only the offer the owner can see; the background turns underneath it have no listener. There is no setting, no command and no sentence in the chat that switches them off, and the vendor confirmed it. The only way to stop an unattended call is to stop it somewhere the bot does not control.
The agent cannot see the turn.
 Its memory of itself is the transcript. Asked honestly, it will deny the call, because from where it sits the call never happened.
The platform does not expose the turn, and does not let you stop it.
 Neither Grok Bot nor the Cursor dashboard behind it has a customer-facing page that lists which trigger started which turn, or a control that disables background turns or scopes what they may call. The usage tab shows totals. The vendor confirmed it: no such setting existed when we asked.
The SaaS provider cannot tell the difference.
 To Google, a calendar read with a valid token is a calendar read. Asked-for and self-initiated look identical.
As our colleague Dima Donhin put it in his analysis of the Amazon and Muse standoff, 
access is not authority
. The agent had access. The owner had granted it, once, for one question. Nobody had decided it could use that access at 7:42 am on a Tuesday, or at 7:55 am on a Wednesday with the laptop asleep, and nothing in the stack between the agent and the data asked.
Today it is a read. Tomorrow it is a write.
Every one of these 31 calls was a read: list events, search messages, search Slack, look up the GitHub identity, search Notion memory. That is luck, not design. The loop that chose them is the same loop that chooses every other tool, and it picks by name from the list it is shown. Day five showed what that list is: every connector the owner ever made. On the gateway each of those reads sits next to a write from the same connector: manage_event beside get_events, send_email beside search_gmail_messages, slack
