Proton
, the privacy-focused tech company offering alternatives to Google’s and Microsoft’s tools for email, productivity apps, password management, cloud storage, and more, is now bringing its 
AI app Lumo
 to personal computers. On Tuesday, the company launched Lumo Desktop for Windows, macOS, and Linux, allowing users to leverage its AI to carry out local tasks on their computer involving connected apps, personal files, and other automations.
The app is designed to offer users more control over their personal data, as consumer AI technology becomes focused more on AI agents that can perform tasks on users’ behalf, rather than just answering questions. 
Already, the major model makers, including OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, and others, offer desktop tools for working with their AI technologies, but doing so invites
 increased risk to users’ personal data
. Only this week, for instance, 
Apple announced it was addressing those privacy concerns
 with tightened security settings for apps that request Full Disk Access, as the AI desktop apps do.
Proton’s idea is to offer a set of tools that let its AI access connected apps, files, and folders and automate tasks, but with more personalization over how much access the AI has. The company says users can choose which actions the AI agent takes that will need to request their approval, and it vows that the conversations and information from connected apps and files are never used to serve personalized ads or train AI models.

The new app includes a variety of 
connectors
 that support local tools, like file-system access, shell execution, and browser content fetching, as well as Proton’s email app and other third-party services, including Confluence, Dropbox, and Jira.
The connectors also support local and remote MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers, which AI agents use to access external data sources.
Proton says that users can decide for each connector which actions Lumo can perform automatically, which require approval, and which are blocked entirely. Users can also turn individual tools off from the Tools menu in their conversation. 
In addition, users can set up their own scheduled tasks, where each has its own set of permissions, in terms of what can be handled automatically and what requires approval. 
Proton notes that it uses encryption to ensure that only users can read their chat history, not the company. The company also says it keeps no server-side logs, and the limited data it does store is protected under Swiss privacy law. The data is hosted on European infrastructure, outside the scope of the 
U.S. CLOUD Act
, which specifies how law enforcement agencies access data held by cloud service providers.
The company says it’s investing in its infrastructure, with its first Nvidia B300 cluster built on Open Compute Project (OCP) standards now going live in Switzerland. 
Similar to other AI apps, 
Lumo’s desktop app
 supports Skills, which lets users save instructions, templates, and reference files that Lumo can reuse across their conversations. (An earlier version of this, Custom Lumos, will now be migrated to the Skills format.)

Connect everything, expose nothing

Connectors: Work with your apps and files
Connectors let Lumo find information and take action in the apps and tools you connect. Ask it to find a document on your computer, gather bookings from your email, or search your drive without switching tabs or copying and pasting between windows.

Local tools, including filesystem access, shell execution, and browser content fetching

Third-party services like Google Drive for your personal files, plus work tools such as Jira, and Confluence
Your own local or remote Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers

Schedules: Put everyday tasks on a schedule

There are two ways to set up a

Each task has its own permissions. You decide which actions Lumo can take automatically and which require approval. You can change the instructions or timing, pause the task, or delete it whenever you need to.

Proton 
released Lumo Desktop
 for Windows, macOS, and Linux on November 17, 2026, introducing Connectors, Schedules, and Skills for its privacy-focused AI assistant. The update lets Lumo work with local files and connected apps, run tasks at set times, and reuse personalized instructions.
The release moves 
Lumo
 beyond a chat interface and into actions across a user’s computer and online accounts. The tools were built for Proton’s internal teams before being released to users, and their source code is publicly available for inspection. That expanded reach makes control over what the assistant can read and do central to the product’s design.
Connectors Extend Lumo Across Apps and Files
Connectors let Lumo retrieve information and perform actions through local filesystem access, shell execution, and browser content fetching. Proton Mail works through Proton Mail Bridge, while supported third-party connections include Google Drive, Slack, Dropbox, Jira, and Confluence. Users can also add local or remote Model Context Protocol (MCP) servers; Proton’s 
connector documentation
 specifies support for local stdio and remote HTTP servers.
Each connector action has one of three permission settings: Auto-approve, Ask human, or Deny. For example, a user can permit inbox searches, require confirmation before an email is sent, and block message deletion. Actions default to Auto-approve, and connectors are enabled by default in conversations, although users can adjust individual permissions or disable a connector through the Tools menu. Without those changes, connected actions can run without confirmation, making the initial permission review a significant part of setup.
Schedules and Skills Target Repeated Work
Schedules can run once at a specified time or recur, with separate permissions for each task. A user can describe the desired work and timing, review and confirm the task Lumo prepares, and later revise its instructions or schedule, pause it, or delete it.
Skills save reusable instructions, templates, and reference files. Lumo can apply relevant Skills automatically and use several in the same conversation, while existing Custom Lumos will be migrated automatically. Examples include a writing Skill for email drafts and a job-application Skill containing a resume and career goals.
Proton’s examples show how the features can be combined. A monthly spending review can draw bills and receipts from local folders and Google Drive, summarize spending, flag unusual charges, and use a Skill to keep each summary in a preferred format. A travel workflow can collect tickets and reservations from an inbox into one itinerary, check weather updates before departure on a schedule, and retain travel preferences in a Skill.
Privacy Architecture and Plan Access
Proton’s 
security documentation
 describes a system that encrypts messages in transit, temporarily decrypts them on its servers for language-model processing, and encrypts them again before returning responses. Conversation history is sent encrypted to supply context and stored with zero-access encryption, leaving only the user able to decrypt stored chats. Plaintext is not retained on the server beyond what is necessary to generate the immediate response.
Proton also says Lumo keeps no server-side records of when users chatted, where they were, or what they discussed. It does not use conversations or information from connected apps and files to train models or serve personalized ads. Ghost Mode makes chats disappear when closed, and the service runs on Proton-controlled servers in Europe.
The 
Lumo download page
 lists web, iOS, and Android versions alongside the desktop apps. A free Proton account is sufficient to start, while paid plans add features and higher usage limits. Connectors and Schedules are currently desktop-only; the mobile apps are oriented toward on-the-go conversations and dictation.
Alongside the release, Proton said its first NVIDIA B300 cluster built on Open Compute Project standards is going live in Switzerland, describing it as an investment in sovereign Swiss AI infrastructure and greater control over the technology behind its AI services.
The desktop release ties Lumo’s automation capabilities directly to Proton’s privacy proposition: the more files, accounts, and recurring tasks users place within its reach, the more consequential its permission defaults and processing architecture become.
